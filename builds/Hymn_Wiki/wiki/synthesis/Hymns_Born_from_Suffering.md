# Hymns Born from Suffering

It is one of the strangest and most consistent patterns in the history of Christian hymnody: the hymns that last longest are often those that cost their authors the most. Wealth, comfort, and security rarely produce great hymns. Plague does. War does. Blindness, shipwreck, the loss of children, the long suffering of illness --- these produce hymns that cut to the bone and stay there.

This is not, it should be said, because suffering is good or because the church romanticizes pain. It is because extremity forces writers to be honest about what they actually believe when there is nothing left but belief. The theology of the comfortable can afford to be vague. The theology of the deathbed cannot.

## Plague: Nicolai and Rinkart

The Lutheran tradition was forged in plague as much as in theological controversy. Two of its greatest hymns were written in the presence of mass death.

[[Philipp_Nicolai]] was a pastor in Westphalia during the epidemic of 1597-1598. Fourteen hundred of his parishioners died. He could see the gravediggers from his study window; on some days, thirty burials were conducted. Out of that winter, he wrote the two hymns known as the King and Queen of Chorales. One draws on the parable of the ten virgins; the other is a meditation on the morning star that shines before the dawn. Both breathe an almost terrifying certainty: whatever the grave is, it is not the end, and the end that waits is incomparably more beautiful than anything the plague can take away. Nicolai wrote them not for publication but as a "manual of comfort" for his congregation. They have comforted Christians for four centuries.

[[Martin_Rinkart]] served as the only surviving pastor in the walled city of Eilenburg during the Thirty Years' War (see [[Thirty_Years_War_Hymns]]). Disease, famine, and the passage of armies left the city in ruins. Rinkart buried four thousand people during the plague years, including his own wife. In the midst of this, he wrote a table grace --- a hymn of thanksgiving that begins with the call to give thanks in all things. It became Germany's Lutheran Te Deum. Every year at great national occasions it is sung. It was written by a man who had far less to be grateful for, by any worldly measure, than the people who now sing it.

## War: Heermann and Gerhardt

[[Johann_Heermann]] lived in Silesia as the Thirty Years' War stripped the land bare. His church was plundered, his family scattered, his health broken by the recurrent strain of war and pestilence. From this period came his Passion hymns --- meditations on the crucifixion that are numbered among the greatest texts in Lutheran hymnody. There is a specificity to his anguish and his gratitude that could only have come from a man who had looked at real suffering and found in the suffering Christ a companion, not merely a doctrine.

[[Paul_Gerhardt]] lived through the same war's aftermath, then was driven from his Berlin pulpit by the theological controversies of the 1660s. He lost his wife. He lost his children. He wrote 123 hymns, and they carry every ounce of that experience. Yet they are not bitter. They are some of the most trustful, most tender, most deeply consoling texts ever produced by a Christian pen. Gerhardt's suffering did not make him hard; it made him honest, and his honesty is what four centuries of readers have recognized and reached for in their own suffering.

## Blindness: Crosby and the Inner Eye

[[Fanny_Crosby]] was blinded at six weeks old. A careless doctor treated an eye inflammation with the wrong remedy; the inflammation healed, the sight was permanently damaged. Crosby herself, in old age, insisted that this had been a blessing rather than a curse --- that her inability to see the outer world had made her more attuned to the inner one.

This is not self-pity or false piety. It is the testimony of a woman who produced, under those conditions, more hymns of genuine devotion than any other writer in the tradition. She wrote from the inside because the inside was, literally, all she had. She wrote about assurance, about trust, about the welcome that awaits beyond death --- and she wrote about these things with the authority of someone for whom the visible world had always been secondary to the invisible.

John Milton, the greatest Protestant epic poet, went blind in middle age and dictated *Paradise Lost* to his daughters. He was not a hymn writer, but his late sonnet on his blindness --- the meditation on standing and waiting, on the light that does not fail even when the eye does --- belongs in the same conversation. Both Milton and Crosby discovered that blindness, for a writer who has faith, can be a kind of clarification.

## Persecution: Gerhardt Again, and the Martyrs' Tradition

Paul Gerhardt's expulsion from his Berlin pulpit was a form of persecution --- not imprisonment, not death, but professional destruction at the hands of the Elector of Brandenburg, who demanded that Lutheran and Reformed clergy sign a political compromise that Gerhardt refused on grounds of conscience. He was fifty-seven years old. He lost his congregation, his income, and his home.

He did not stop writing. Some of his most serene hymns date from this period.

The martyrs' tradition more broadly --- from the Bohemian Brethren through the English Nonconformists driven out by the Act of Uniformity in 1662 --- produced a remarkable body of hymn writing. [[John_Fawcett]], offered the prestigious London pastorate of a famous predecessor, turned it down and went home to his poor village congregation. From that renunciation came one of the great hymns of Christian community. [[Philip_Doddridge]], last of twenty children and survivor of impoverished circumstances that might have broken a less resilient spirit, wrote about the joyful decision of faith with a clarity that his easy circumstances never could have produced.

## Grief: Spafford, Matheson, and Lyte

Three men wrote their most famous texts within hours or days of catastrophic loss. Each one represents a different kind of grief meeting a different kind of faith.

[[Horatio_Spafford]] was an American lawyer who had already survived the destruction of most of his Chicago real estate in the Great Fire of 1871. In November 1873, the ship carrying his four daughters to Europe sank after a collision; all four girls drowned. His wife survived and cabled him the two words: "Saved alone." Spafford sailed immediately to meet her. When the ship crossed approximately the latitude where his daughters had drowned, he wrote a hymn of radical, deliberate, costly peace. He wrote it, by his own account, as an act of will, not of emotion. He would say it is well because it must be well, because the alternative is a universe with no floor beneath it. The hymn has been sung at every conceivable kind of funeral, at crises both public and private, at moments when peace seems impossible. It has never felt dishonest because the man who wrote it knew exactly how hard it was to mean it.

[[George_Matheson]] was a Scottish minister who had been essentially blind since his twenties. On the evening of June 6, 1882, he experienced what he later described as the most severe mental suffering of his life. He never said explicitly what it was; scholars have speculated. What is certain is that within a few minutes he wrote a hymn of divine love that begins with a declaration of being held, refused, and kept by a love that will not let go. He described it afterward as the only hymn he had written without revision --- a single creative act, as though dictated from outside himself. The hymn asks to be traced to its source "in the rainbow of Thy purpose," a phrase that fuses the covenant sign with the nature of God's intention. It is one of the most concentrated texts in the English tradition.

[[Henry_Francis_Lyte]] served the fisherfolk of Lower Brixham, Devon, for decades, a man who was himself never entirely well. In September 1847 he preached what he knew would be his farewell sermon --- his lungs were failing and his doctors had ordered him to leave for a warmer climate. That same Sunday evening, he walked in his garden and wrote the hymn that begins with an appeal to a presence to remain when the light of day is failing. He traveled to Nice and died there three months later, his last words reported to be expressions of joy. His hymn has since been sung at the funerals of royalty, at the last services in cathedral closes, and by ordinary congregations in the gathering dark of their own evenings.

## The Persistent Pattern

| Writer | Form of Suffering | Hymn(s) Born From It |
|--------|-------------------|----------------------|
| [[Philipp_Nicolai]] | Plague (1,400 parishioners dead) | Two great chorales |
| [[Martin_Rinkart]] | War, plague, buried 4,000 | German national Te Deum |
| [[Johann_Heermann]] | Thirty Years' War devastation | Passion meditations |
| [[Paul_Gerhardt]] | War, expulsion, family deaths | 123 hymns; "O Sacred Head" |
| [[Charlotte_Elliott]] | Lifelong invalidism | Great invitation hymn |
| [[Sarah_Flower_Adams]] | Illness, early death | One enduring text of wrestling |
| [[Fanny_Crosby]] | Lifelong blindness | 8,000+ hymns of inner sight |
| [[George_Matheson]] | Blindness; unnamed acute grief | Hymn of love that holds fast |
| [[Horatio_Spafford]] | Four daughters drowned | Radical peace hymn |
| [[Henry_Francis_Lyte]] | Mortal illness, farewell | Great evening hymn |

The pattern is not accidental. Hymns born from suffering carry a kind of ballast that comfortable verse cannot. The reader in crisis can trust them because their authors have been tested and did not put down a false bottom. They knew, from the inside, that the floor held. Their testimony is credible precisely because of what it cost.
