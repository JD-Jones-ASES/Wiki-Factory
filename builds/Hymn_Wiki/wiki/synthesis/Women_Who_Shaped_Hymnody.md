# Women Who Shaped Hymnody

The story of women in hymnody is, in part, a story of invisibility made audible. For centuries, the church silenced women's public voices in worship; the hymnal was one of the few spaces where a woman's words could enter the gathered congregation. The women who wrote hymns often did so privately, anonymously, or as the byproduct of a life otherwise constrained by illness, grief, or social expectation. And then their words spread across the world and outlasted their circumstances by generations.

---

## The Scope of the Achievement

Before examining individual writers, it is worth pausing over the fact itself: women produced some of the most sung, most loved, and most theologically substantial texts in the entire Christian tradition. The invitation hymn that D.L. Moody called his most soul-winning tool was written by an invalid woman who wrote it not for publication but for her own comfort. The children's hymn that may be the most widely known Christian song ever written was composed by a woman who conducted Sunday school for West Point cadets. The text that became a dying president's prayer was written by a woman who died at forty-three.

None of them set out to reshape a tradition. Most of them were simply writing what they needed to say.

---

## Frances Ridley Havergal (1836–1879): The Consecration Poet

[[Frances_Ridley_Havergal]] is, for many readers, the most compelling figure in Victorian hymnody. She was the daughter of a clergyman-musician, educated in Germany, and gifted with both a musical ear and a prodigious memory --- she had committed all four Gospels, the Psalms, Isaiah, and the minor prophets to heart. She wrote in the white heat of total consecration, and her hymns breathe that commitment on every line.

Her great consecration hymn --- "Take my life, and let it be" --- was composed in February 1874 after she had spent the night praying for the conversion of ten people under her father's roof. Every pair of stanzas offers a different faculty or gift to divine service: silver, moments, will, feet, voice, intellect, love, self. The progression is methodical and the cumulative effect is overwhelming.

Havergal died at forty-two. In her last hours she sang "Jesus, I will trust Thee" --- turning her own hymns into her deathbed liturgy. She had once said she never felt satisfied with anything she had written until it had cost her something. It shows.

## Fanny Crosby (1823–1915): The Blind Poet of America

[[Fanny_Crosby]] is the great counter-example to every assumption about what a "serious" hymn writer looks like. She was blind from six weeks old, the result of medical negligence. She wrote thousands of hymns --- the estimates range from 8,000 to 9,000 --- under scores of pseudonyms because her publishers worried that one author's name appearing so often would devalue the brand. She wrote quickly, often from a tune the composer had already written, building her text around the musical phrase. She wrote until she was nearly ninety.

What she produced, at that pace and under those conditions, includes some of the most enduring texts in American evangelical Christianity. Her hymn of assurance, her invitation to rest, her call to mission --- these became the soundtrack of the great Moody-Sankey revivals and traveled with those campaigns across the English-speaking world. She worked principally with [[William_H_Doane]] and [[Robert_Lowry]], whose musical settings gave her texts their defining character.

There is a story about Crosby that says everything necessary: when informed that her blindness was the result of careless medical treatment and that she might have sued for compensation, she declined. She said the blindness had been essential to her writing --- that she could see the inner world more clearly for being unable to see the outer one. Whether or not the story is precisely true, it captures something real about her. She wrote from the inside.

## Charlotte Elliott (1789–1871): The Invalid Who Reached Millions

[[Charlotte_Elliott]] spent most of her adult life as an invalid, confined by chronic illness that prevented almost all public activity. She wrote her most famous text in 1836 --- the simple, dignified invitation of a soul to come to Christ exactly as it is, without preparation or merit. She wrote it for herself, during a night when her illness had prevented her from taking part in a bazaar her family was organizing for a church building fund. She felt useless. She turned her need into a hymn.

The hymn she wrote spread through the English-speaking world at a speed that astonished her. D.L. Moody said it had been used in the conversion of more souls than any other hymn he knew. Her brother, the clergyman for whom the bazaar was organized, said she had "done more for the cause of Christ than I have done in all my years of ministry."

She produced about 150 hymns in all, collected in various volumes. The great one --- just as she was, just as she is --- eclipses the rest in fame but the others reward reading. She wrote with the directness of someone who had learned that there is no energy to waste on indirection.

## Sarah Flower Adams (1805–1848): One Great Hymn

[[Sarah_Flower_Adams]] is, in a sense, a one-hymn figure --- but what a hymn. She was an actress, a writer, a Unitarian, and an intimate of the London intellectual circle that included Leigh Hunt and Robert Browning. She was commissioned by her minister, William Johnson Fox, to write texts for a congregational hymnal. The text she wrote meditating on Jacob's wrestling at Peniel --- the cry of a soul that refuses to let God go even in the darkness of struggle --- became one of the most sung and most cited hymns in the entire tradition.

President McKinley quoted it in his final hours. It has been used at funerals, during crises, in moments of profound spiritual wrestling across every Protestant denomination. Adams herself died at forty-three of tuberculosis. She was 43, the same age as her hymn's first published appearance. The brevity of her life makes the permanence of her text more striking.

Her Unitarianism has sometimes made other traditions uncomfortable with her legacy, but the hymn has always been stronger than the theology of its author. The experience it describes --- of clinging in the dark, refusing to release, awaiting the blessing that comes only with the wound --- is recognizable across every tradition that takes spiritual struggle seriously.

## Anna Warner (1820–1915): "Jesus Loves Me"

[[Anna_Warner]] lived for most of her long life at Constitution Island, directly across the Hudson River from West Point. She and her sister Susan conducted Sunday school classes for West Point cadets for decades, rowing across the river each week. The hymn she wrote --- "Jesus loves me, this I know" --- appears to have been first published as a poem in a novel her sister wrote in 1860. It was set to music shortly afterward and became, by most measures, the most widely known Christian children's text ever written.

There is a story, perhaps apocryphal, that Karl Barth --- the greatest Protestant theologian of the twentieth century --- was asked in his old age to summarize his life's work. He replied: "Jesus loves me, this I know." The story is told to make a theological point about simplicity, but it also says something about Anna Warner. She wrote for children. She wrote something that the most sophisticated adult theology cannot improve on.

Warner died at ninety-four, having outlived almost everyone she had written for. She is buried at West Point with military honors.

## Cecil Frances Alexander (1823–1895): Doctrine for Children

[[Cecil_Frances_Alexander]] wrote her most famous collection in 1848 at the age of twenty-five: *Hymns for Little Children*, a volume designed to explain the phrases of the Apostles' Creed to children in memorable verse. The "Maker of heaven and earth" stanza became one of the best-loved nature hymns in the language. The "born of the Virgin Mary" stanza became the most sung Christmas narrative hymn in the British tradition. The "suffered under Pontius Pilate" stanza became a standard of Good Friday services.

She also wrote what is probably the finest text on salvation grounded in specific imagery: the image of a hill outside a city wall, green in the spring. All three of these texts remain in virtually every English-language hymnal published in the last century.

Alexander was Irish, the wife of a Church of Ireland bishop. She was also a substantial figure in Victorian philanthropy, devoting most of the royalties from her hymn collections to institutions for the deaf. Her hymns were never sentimental --- they were, as she intended, doctrinal. But they carried doctrine in images so clear and concrete that children could hold them, and so did not put them down when they grew up.

## Other Voices

The six figures above are the most prominent, but the women's contribution to hymnody runs far wider. [[Charlotte_Elliott]]'s contemporary [[Harriet_Auber]] wrote one of the great Pentecost hymns in seclusion in Hertfordshire. [[Annie_Sherwood_Hawks]] wrote her hymn of need in 1872 --- and did not feel its weight until her husband died years later. [[Anne_Ross_Cousin]] produced one of the most architecturally complex hymns in the Scottish tradition from Samuel Rutherford's dying words. [[Elizabeth_Clephane]] wrote the parable of the lost sheep as a poem, never knowing that [[Ira_Sankey]] would one day improvise a tune for it on the spot before a crowd of thousands. [[Phebe_Hinsdale_Brown]] wrote in tears, with a baby in her lap, after a neighbor rebuked her for neglecting her house. [[Kate_Hankey]], recovering from illness in 1866, wrote two extended meditations on the gospel story that were eventually divided into the two most-sung hymns to emerge from the evangelical parlor tradition.

[[Jemima_Thompson_Luke]] wrote her Sunday school classic in a stagecoach. [[Emily_Sullivan_Oakey]] wrote her harvest hymn while teaching languages at a women's academy. [[Ellen_Gates]] wrote the text that Abraham Lincoln asked to have repeated, the same text that inspired a young [[Ira_Sankey]] to commit his life to sacred song.

Each one wrote from the circumstances of a woman's life in the nineteenth century: constrained, often private, frequently overlooked. What they produced, together, is an irreplaceable part of the tradition's best work.

## The Pattern and Its Significance

There is a pattern worth naming. Most of these women wrote their most enduring texts at moments of extremity: illness, grief, uselessness, darkness. [[Charlotte_Elliott]] during a night of feeling cut off from service. [[Sarah_Flower_Adams]] watching her health fail. [[Fanny_Crosby]] navigating a life of total darkness. [[Frances_Ridley_Havergal]] through a night of intercession. [[Phebe_Hinsdale_Brown]] in tears after a rebuke.

The pattern is not unique to women --- many of the men in this wiki wrote in similar extremity (see [[Hymns_Born_from_Suffering]]). But it has particular resonance for writers who had few public outlets for their gifts and for whom the private act of composition was itself an act of dignity and self-assertion. They wrote what they needed. The world, it turned out, needed it too.
