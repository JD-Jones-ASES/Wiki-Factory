# The Great Hymn Writers

Every tradition has its giants. In the long history of Christian hymnody, five figures stand above all others: [[Martin_Luther]], [[Paul_Gerhardt]], [[Isaac_Watts]], [[Charles_Wesley]], and [[Fanny_Crosby]]. To read these five together is to read the whole arc of evangelical song --- Reformation, suffering, liberation, revival, and the gospel made popular.

---

## Martin Luther (1483–1546): The Liberator

Before Luther, congregations sat and listened. After Luther, they sang.

That is the essential fact about [[Martin_Luther]] as a hymn writer. His achievement is not primarily literary --- though several of his hymns are magnificent --- but structural. He restored the assembly to its ancient dignity as a worshiping, singing body. He wrote in German, not Latin. He drew on popular tunes and Gregorian chant alike, refusing to treat sacred music as a specialized craft separated from the life of ordinary people. When Wittenberg heard its new hymns for the first time, the Reformation had found its popular voice.

Luther wrote some 37 hymns. Many are adaptations of Latin originals or psalm paraphrases. A few are wholly original. His great battle hymn --- drawn from Psalm 46, calling God a "mighty fortress" --- became the anthem of the Reformation. Lutheran tradition calls it the "Marseillaise of the Reformation," the song that soldiers sang going into battle for their faith.

What made Luther great: his conviction that music is the noblest of God's gifts, second only to theology. He composed music himself, collaborated with [[Johann_Walter]], and placed hymnody at the center of the new evangelical liturgy. He liberated congregational song; everyone who came after him in the Western tradition --- Catholic and Protestant alike --- benefited.

## Paul Gerhardt (1607–1676): The Sufferer

If Luther is the liberator, [[Paul_Gerhardt]] is the consoler. He lived through the worst decades of the Thirty Years' War, was driven from his Berlin pulpit by theological controversy, and buried his wife and four of his five children. His 123 hymns are soaked in suffering --- and in a confidence that somehow survives it.

Gerhardt is called the "Prince of Lutheran Hymnists." That title understates the case. He is, for many readers, simply the greatest hymn writer who ever lived --- a poet of the inner life who could write both the outdoor summer hymn that opens with meadows and birdsong and the Passion meditation that plumbs the abyss of Holy Week. He worked from scripture, from the tradition of [[Johann_Heermann]] and [[Philipp_Nicolai]] before him, and from his own unsheltered experience.

His hymns are personal without being sentimental; theological without being arid; consoling without being cheap. When [[Catherine_Winkworth]] translated them into English in the 1850s, Victorian readers encountered something they had not found in their own tradition: a Lutheran depth of pathos combined with an utterly unshaken faith.

What made Gerhardt great: the combination of specific, sensory language with doctrinal substance. He did not generalize. He trusted the particular moment --- the spring morning, the man on the cross, the night of fear --- to carry universal weight.

## Isaac Watts (1674–1748): The Liberator of English Hymnody

[[Isaac_Watts]] did for English what Luther had done for German: he freed it from the shackles of a tradition that had grown too narrow for its content.

English congregational song in 1700 meant the Old Version or New Version of the Psalms --- metrically converted, often clumsy, robbed of the gospel. Watts found this intolerable. He believed that Christians worshiping under the new covenant should sing as Christians --- that every psalm should be read in the light of Christ, and that the church should compose new songs as well as paraphrase old ones. He published his *Hymns and Spiritual Songs* in 1707. The English hymn as we know it began there.

Watts wrote approximately 600 hymns, of which perhaps 30 remain in wide use. His range is remarkable: from cosmic doxology to intimate confession, from the joyful transformation of a Christmas psalm to the solemnity of a contemplation on the Cross. He brought classical learning and Puritan theology together in a lyric form accessible to a congregation. Dr. Samuel Johnson called him "a man of great learning and an honest man."

What made Watts great: the clarity of his theological vision, combined with an ear for the memorable phrase. He did not merely versify ideas; he found the image that carried the idea further than prose could. The survey of the Cross as the emblem of sacrifice; the transformation of Psalm 90 into a meditation on human frailty before the eternal God --- these are achievements of religious imagination, not merely verse technique.

## Charles Wesley (1707–1788): The Lyric Genius

Numbers first: [[Charles_Wesley]] wrote more than 6,500 hymns. That is not a misprint. In quantity, he dwarfs every other figure in the tradition. But quantity, however staggering, is not the point. The point is that perhaps two hundred of those hymns are great, and a dozen of them are among the finest religious poems in the English language.

Wesley was a Methodist, the younger brother of John, and he wrote in the fire of revival. His hymns are simultaneously evangelical and sacramental, personal and cosmic, scriptural and experiential. He knew the Greek New Testament intimately and mined it for images that he then set to meters drawn from popular secular poetry. His Christmas hymn, his Easter hymn, his hymn of divine love, his hymn of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost --- each one is a doctrinal statement and an act of worship at the same time.

Where [[Isaac_Watts]] is majestic and deliberate, Wesley is urgent and lyric. Watts surveys; Wesley participates. Watts reasons toward praise; Wesley erupts into it. They complement each other perfectly, and the tradition is immeasurably richer for having both.

What made Wesley great: the fusion of theological precision with emotional directness. He could write a hymn that was, simultaneously, a confession of faith, a prayer, a theological argument, and a piece of beautiful English. No one before him or since has done that with such consistent power.

## Fanny Crosby (1823–1915): The Voice of the People

[[Fanny_Crosby]] is the fifth giant, and in some ways the most extraordinary. She was blind from infancy. She lived in New York. She wrote somewhere between 8,000 and 9,000 hymns under dozens of pseudonyms because her publishers were embarrassed by the scale of her production. She wrote at a pace that astonishes --- sometimes several hymns in a single sitting, often beginning with a tune the composer had already written, constructing her text to fit the melody.

This sounds like a recipe for forgettable doggerel. And there is doggerel in the Crosby corpus. But there are also texts of piercing directness and genuine devotional power --- hymns that have accompanied millions of people through grief, conversion, illness, and death. "Safe in the Arms of Jesus" was sung at President Grant's funeral. Her hymn of assurance became one of the most widely known Christian texts in the world. "Rescue the Perishing" defined the social gospel of the Gilded Age.

What made Crosby great: her absolute identification with her audience. She wrote for ordinary people, from ordinary experience, in language that ordinary people could carry in their hearts. She was not a literary artist in the way that Watts or Wesley were; she was something else --- a woman who had a gift for crystallizing exactly what a congregation needed to say and couldn't find the words for.

Her collaborators [[William_H_Doane]], [[Robert_Lowry]], and [[Ira_Sankey]] carried her texts around the English-speaking world in the great Moody-Sankey revival campaigns. She is, measured by reach and influence, perhaps the most widely sung Christian poet who ever lived.

## Comparing the Five

| Writer | Era | Tradition | Output | Distinctive Gift |
|--------|-----|-----------|--------|-----------------|
| [[Martin_Luther]] | 16th c. | Lutheran | ~37 hymns | Congregational liberation; music as theology |
| [[Paul_Gerhardt]] | 17th c. | Lutheran | 123 hymns | Suffering transformed; doctrinal poetry |
| [[Isaac_Watts]] | 18th c. | English Nonconformist | ~600 hymns | Clarity, imagination, theological vision |
| [[Charles_Wesley]] | 18th c. | Methodist | 6,500+ hymns | Lyric genius; fusion of doctrine and experience |
| [[Fanny_Crosby]] | 19th c. | American revivalist | 8,000+ hymns | Popular accessibility; revival directness |

Five writers, five traditions, five centuries, five languages (Luther in German, Gerhardt in German, Watts in English, Wesley in English, Crosby in American vernacular). Together they tell the whole story of how the Christian song moved from the Wittenberg church to the Madison Square Garden revival meeting.
