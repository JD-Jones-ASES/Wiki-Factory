# Rowland Hill

Rowland Hill was one of those figures who inhabited the edges of every institution without belonging fully to any of them. Ordained in the Church of England, he spent half a century at a London chapel without episcopal sanction. Aligned in doctrine with George Whitefield and Lady Huntingdon, he was too independent to be anyone's colleague. A fervent Calvinist and an ordained clergyman, he ministered to a congregation that included many Nonconformists. His chief legacy to hymnody was less the hymns he wrote than the influence he exerted over fifty years in popularizing hymn-singing among London's working class and middle class.

## Life and Ministry

Hill was born in 1744, the sixth son of a baronet, and received a classical education. At Cambridge he came under the influence of the Evangelical Revival, associated himself with the Calvinistic side following Whitefield rather than the Wesleyan, and began preaching while still a student. His unconventional itinerant ministry after his Cambridge years lasted twelve years, during which he preached wherever he could find a congregation.

In 1783 Hill opened Surrey Chapel in London — a large circular building designed specifically for popular preaching. For the next fifty years, until his death in 1833, he preached there to enormous congregations, conducting what Benson describes as a ministry "without episcopal sanction" yet with considerable use of Church of England formularies. He was straitly Calvinistic in doctrine but regarded as too erratic for the established ecclesiastical order, and he worked outside the boundaries of both the Church of England and organized Dissent.

## Hymn-Singing and the Surrey Chapel Tradition

Hill believed powerfully in congregational singing as a means of popular evangelism. His ministry coincided with a significant moment in English religious culture: the period when Evangelical influence was pushing against the older resistance to hymns in both Anglican and Dissenting churches. Hill's Surrey Chapel was one of the places where popular hymn-singing developed freely, without the constraints of either Anglican liturgical conservatism or Dissenting theological caution.

He published for his congregation's use a *Collection of Psalms and Hymns* (1783) and an earlier collection (1774) *chiefly intended for the use of the poor.* He believed in setting hymns to popular melodies — a practice that put him in the same tradition as the Methodist use of folk tunes, and which anticipated the later Gospel Song movement's willingness to borrow from secular music. His organist B. Jacob cooperated with him in providing and adapting tunes.

## Hill as Hymn Writer

As a hymn writer Hill belonged to Watts' school — doctrinally serious, practically aimed, without literary pretension. Several hymns from his collections passed into wider use: "Cast thy burden on the Lord," "We sing His love who once was slain," and "With heavenly power, O Lord, defend" were associated with him and remained in use beyond the Surrey Chapel circle.

He was also an early Sunday school worker and published collections specifically for children's use: *Divine Hymns attempted in easy language for the use of children* (1790, revised by Cowper), *A Collection of Hymns for children* (1808), and *Hymns for schools* (1832).

Perhaps his most celebrated hymnic moment came during the Napoleonic wars, when his hymn "When Jesus first, at Heav'n's command" was set to the tune of "Rule Britannia" and sung by the Volunteer forces. The effect on the congregations who heard it was long remembered.

## Significance in Hymnodic History

Benson places Hill in the chapter on the Evangelical Revival's by-streams, seeing him as a figure whose long London ministry diffused Evangelical hymn-singing practice widely and helped accustom Church of England circles to the kind of congregational singing that was then a Dissenting and Methodist habit. His Surrey Chapel became a model of what popular London ministry looked like, and the hymn-singing culture he developed there influenced a generation of ministers and congregations.

He represents the Calvinistic side of the English Evangelical Revival in its English (as distinct from American) expression — sharing the doctrinal commitments of Whitefield's circle but outliving that circle by decades and carrying its hymnodic habits into the nineteenth century.
