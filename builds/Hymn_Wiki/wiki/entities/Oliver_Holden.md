# Oliver Holden

Few stories in American hymn history are more charming than that of Oliver Holden, the self-taught carpenter of Shirley, Massachusetts, who composed a tune that has been ringing through American churches for more than two centuries.

## The Carpenter Who Composed

Holden was born in Shirley, Massachusetts, in 1765, and trained as a carpenter. Music was his passion, not his trade, and he was entirely self-taught. Despite this, his musical instincts were sound enough to produce one of the most celebrated tunes in Christian worship.

Butterworth-Brown describe him as a man for whom "sacred music was the good man's passion to the last." He died in 1844.

## The Little Pipe Organ

The original instrument on which Holden struck the first notes of "Coronation" --- a little pipe organ --- still survives. When the story of the tune became known, the granddaughter of Holden's family, Mrs. Fanny Tyler, donated it to the Historical rooms of the Old State House in Boston. Its tones, Butterworth-Brown note, are "as mellow as ever, and the times that 'Coronation' has been played upon it by admiring visitors would far outnumber the notes of its score."

## "Coronation" --- An American Te Deum

The tune "Coronation" was composed in 1792, the very year of Edward Perronet's death. No one knows exactly when hymn and tune first found each other, but when they did, they were immediately recognized as made for one another. "Heaven evidently meant them to be wedded for all time," Butterworth-Brown write.

The tune's fame spread quickly. Dr. Timothy Dwight of Yale College (1798) loved it so much that the college choir often sang it while he, "catching as it were the music of the heavenly world, would join them and lead with the most ardent devotion." Butterworth-Brown call it "his American 'Te Deum.'"

Holden also wrote other tunes --- "Cowper," "Confidence," and "Concord" among them --- and published collections including *The American Harmony*, *The Union Harmony*, and the *Worcester Collection*, along with the patriotic anthem "Mt. Vernon." But none of his other works achieved the wings of "Coronation."

## The Deathbed Words

On his deathbed, the old musician whispered words that capture his whole life:

*"Such beautiful themes! Such beautiful themes! But I can write no more."*

## Musical Context (Metcalf)

Metcalf's *American Writers and Compilers of Sacred Music* (1925) devotes a full chapter to Holden, calling him "the first one of the earlier tune writers whose work is still found in the hymnals" --- specifically because "Coronation" appeared in every one of the twenty-five modern hymnals Metcalf examined. (Metcalf, p. 124)

Holden was born September 18, 1765, in Shirley, Massachusetts. At twenty-one the family moved to Charlestown. The rebuilding of Charlestown, burned by the British, provided carpenter's work. He prospered, became a real estate operator, gave land for a new Baptist church, and eventually became the head and preacher of a Puritan-style congregation that met in a one-story wooden church largely built by his own hands. He represented Charlestown in the Massachusetts House of Representatives for eight annual terms between 1818 and 1833, and was a prominent Mason.

Metcalf records the striking scene of Holden's last hours: "when his strength was almost gone, and he lay dying, his wife and daughter heard him whisper, 'I have some beautiful airs running in my mind, if I only had strength to note them down.' These were his last words." (Metcalf, p. 126) He died September 4, 1844. The inscription on his gravestone in Charlestown names him "the composer of the tune Coronation."

"Coronation" first appeared in *The Union Harmony* (1793) set to the words of Edward Perronet, "All hail the power of Jesus' name" --- though Holden's book mistakenly attributed those words to Rev. Mr. Medley, an error Metcalf corrects. The hymn had in fact first appeared in *Occasional Verses, Moral and Sacred*, London, 1785. (Metcalf, p. 126)

Metcalf lists Holden's other collections: *American Harmony* (1792), *The Union Harmony* (2 vols., 1793; 2nd ed. 1796; 3rd ed. 1801), *The Massachusetts Compiler* (1795, jointly with Hans Gram and Samuel Holyoke), *The Modern Collection of Sacred Music* (November 1800), *Sacred Dirges* (1800), *A Plain Psalmody* (1800, seventy original tunes), and *The Charlestown Collection of Sacred Songs* (1803). His last-named work noted its purpose as a supplement to the Worcester Collection.

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

"Coronation" is the American counterpart to the English tune "Miles Lane" (by William Shrubsole, 1780), which was actually written first and published in the *Gospel Magazine* during Perronet's lifetime. America chose "Coronation"; England chose "Miles Lane." Both carry Perronet's words well, but Americans have found "Coronation" inseparable from the text. Butterworth-Brown note there is also "a fine processional tune sung in the English Church" to the same hymn.

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Burrage's account of Holden situates him as a Baptist composer in a way that other sources omit. He was the fifth in descent from Richard Holden, who came from England in 1634 and settled in Ipswich, Massachusetts. Born in Shirley on September 17, 1765, he settled in Charlestown as early as 1792 and opened a music store there, devoting himself increasingly to musical composition.

Burrage notes that Holden was also a hymn writer. Rev. F. M. Bird of South Bethlehem, Pennsylvania (a well-known hymnologist), possessed a book probably edited by Holden containing twenty-one hymns marked "H." — two of which are also confirmed as Holden's in the Boston Collection (1808). Burrage lists the first lines: "Arise, my love, my undefiled," "Who will ope the iron gate," "How sweet is the language of love," "Weeping sinner, dry your tears," as well as "With conscious guilt and bleeding heart" (Village Hymns, 1825) and "Within these doors assembled now" (Select Hymns, 1836, wrongly ascribed to Miller). The only Holden hymn still found in modern collections, Burrage notes, is the prayer-meeting favorite "They who seek a throne of grace" (later altered to "All those who seek a throne of grace") — originally published in the Union Harmony (1793).

"Coronation" was composed in 1792, Burrage confirms, consistent with its first appearance in the Union Harmony the following year.
