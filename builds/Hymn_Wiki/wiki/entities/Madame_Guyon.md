# Madame Guyon

Jeanne Marie Bouvier de la Mothe — known always as Madame Guyon — was born at Montargis, France, in 1648, and died in Blois on the Loire in her seventieth year, 1717 (one source says 1771; her actual death was 1717). A convent-educated girl of high family, she was a wife at the age of fifteen and a widow at twenty-eight. Through the dazzling but corrupt society of Louis XIV's reign, her early piety was ridiculed; but through a long life it blossomed into religious ministries and sacred poetry of remarkable fervor.

## Imprisonment and Persecution

Madame Guyon became a mystic, and her book *Spiritual Torrents* indicates what Butterworth-Brown call "the impetuous ardors of her soul." A pious old Franciscan father had once said to her, "Seek God in your heart, and you will find Him" — and she took that counsel to its furthest extreme.

Her teaching, which she called "Quietism," was offensive to the established Church. Bossuet, the greatest preacher of the age, was her adversary. Fénelon, the saintly archbishop, was her friend but could not shield her. She was imprisoned in successive dungeons and, after four years in the Bastille, was exiled to a distant province.

Butterworth-Brown note her "martyr spirit" and observe that her love-flights of devotion do not conceal "the angel that walked in the flame." When we understand her faith — with its "absolute certainty" — we can sing her hymn:

> My Lord, how full of sweet content
> I pass my years of banishment.
> Where'er I dwell, I dwell with Thee,
> In heaven or earth, or on the sea.

## Musical and Thematic Context

The hymn is [[William_Cowper]]'s translation from the French, and Dr. Thomas Upham also translated her religious poems. The tune "Alsace" — a chorale arranged from a sonata by Ludwig van Beethoven — is associated with the hymn, linking the nationalities of writer and composer in its very name.

Madame Guyon sang more "songs in the night," Butterworth-Brown write, "than any hymn-writer outside of the Dark Ages." That her prison years produced some of the most radiant devotional poetry in the French tradition is the central paradox of her life. The stones of her dungeon, she said, "looked like jewels."
