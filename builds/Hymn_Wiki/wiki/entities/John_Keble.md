# John Keble

The Rev. John Keble was born in Caln, St. Aldwyn, on April 25, 1792. He took his degree of A.M., was ordained, and settled at Fairford, where he began the parochial work that ceased only with his life. He died at Bournemouth on March 29, 1866.

His was a life of deliberate smallness. Butterworth-Brown note that his settlement at Fairford, "in charge of three small curacies, satisfied his modest ambition, though altogether they brought him only about £100 per year. Here he preached, wrote his hymns and translations, performed his pastoral work, and was happy. Temptation to wider fields and larger salary never moved him." That self-imposed limitation is itself a kind of spiritual statement — the same quality of unhurried contemplation that gives *The Christian Year* its distinctive character.

## Sun of My Soul

Keble's "Sun of My Soul, My Saviour Dear" is among the most beloved evening hymns in the English tradition. It comes from *The Christian Year*, Keble's collection of poems for the Sundays and holy days of the Church's calendar, published in 1827.

> Sun of my soul, my Saviour dear,
> It is not night if Thou be near.
> O may no earth-born cloud arise
> To hide Thee from Thy servants' eyes.

Butterworth-Brown describe the hymn as one of "almost unparalleled poetic and spiritual beauty."

## Musical and Thematic Context

The tune "Hursley" — arranged from a German chorale by Peter Ritter (1760–1846) by [[William_Henry_Monk]] — perfectly interprets the quiet, reflective mood of Keble's evening prayer. Butterworth-Brown call it "a choice example of polyphonal sweetness in uniform long notes of perfect chord." The tune superseded an earlier setting by [[Thomas_Hastings]] called "Halle."

Robert Schumann's "Canonbury" has also been used with Keble's morning hymn "New Every Morning Is the Love," and is described as "deservedly a favorite for flowing long metres" — but "Hursley" is irreplaceable for "Sun of My Soul."

Keble represents that strand of English hymnody rooted not in evangelical revival but in the quiet sacramental piety of the Anglican parish: thoughtful, devout, and satisfied with small fields well-tended.
