# George Matheson

*"It was composed with extreme rapidity; it seemed to me that its construction occupied only a few minutes, and I felt myself rather in the position of one who was being dictated to than an original artist. I was suffering from extreme mental distress, and the hymn was the fruit of pain."*

That is George Matheson's own account of how "O Love that wilt not let me go" came to be written, on a summer evening in 1882. What makes it more remarkable still is that Matheson was blind when he wrote it.

## A Brilliant Mind in Darkness

Born in Glasgow, Scotland, on March 27, 1842, Matheson enjoyed partial vision as a boy. But after he entered Glasgow University at the age of fifteen, his sight began to fail, and he became totally blind. He was a brilliant scholar nonetheless, graduated with honor in 1861, and remained for four additional years of theological studies.

A devoted sister who loved him made an extraordinary sacrifice for his sake: she learned Greek, Latin, and Hebrew in order to assist him in his studies. Throughout his entire ministry she remained his co-worker, helper, and constant guide in all his pastoral calls.

## The Song in the Night

Matheson tells us he was suffering "extreme mental distress" when the hymn came to him. Many conjectures have been made regarding the cause of that distress. Some suggest he had been bitterly disappointed in his hopes of marrying a young woman, and that her refusal was on account of his blindness.

Whether or not that story is true, there are many significant hints in the hymn itself. The "flickering torch" and the "borrowed ray" in the second stanza speak of someone who lives in darkness. The beautiful thought of tracing "the rainbow through the rain" speaks of someone who has learned to find hope not around suffering but through it. The "cross" referred to in the final stanza speaks of someone who has stopped asking to be delivered.

> O Love that wilt not let me go,
>   I rest my weary soul in Thee:
> I give Thee back the life I owe,
> That in Thine ocean depths its flow
>   May richer, fuller be.

> O Light that followest all my way,
>   I yield my flickering torch to Thee:
> My heart restores its borrowed ray,
> That in Thy sunshine's blaze its day
>   May brighter, fairer be.

> O Joy that seekest me through pain,
>   I cannot close my heart to Thee:
> I trace the rainbow through the rain,
> And feel the promise is not vain
>   That morn shall tearless be.

## A Ministry of Memory

Despite his blindness, Matheson served a fruitful ministry. During the early part of his work he wrote all his sermons in full. He possessed such a remarkable memory that after a sermon had been read to him twice, he could repeat it perfectly.

After twelve years of this practice, he suffered a complete collapse of memory one Sunday morning in the middle of a sermon. Unable to proceed, he calmly announced a hymn and sat down. At the conclusion of the singing he told the congregation what had happened --- and then preached a sermon of great power from an entirely different text.

After eighteen years at Innellan, he was called as pastor of St. Bernard's church in Edinburgh, where he ministered for thirteen years and attracted great multitudes by his preaching. The later years of his life were spent in literary work. His devotional book *Moments on the Mount* is among the finest of its kind.

George Matheson died on August 28, 1906, confident in the assurance he had written:

> ... the promise is not vain
> That morn shall tearless be.

## Legacy

Matheson's hymn is one of the most artistically constructed and poetically rich in English hymnody. It repays careful study. But its enduring power comes from a simpler source: it was written by a man in real pain, who had learned to rest in a Love that would not let him go. That is the testimony of every Christian who has ever sung it since.
