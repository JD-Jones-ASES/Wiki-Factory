# Thomas Tallis

Called "the father of English Cathedral music," Thomas Tallis is the founding figure of the Anglican school of sacred composition. He was born around 1520, spent his earliest musical years as a boy chorister at St. Paul's Cathedral, and after his voice changed became organist at Waltham Abbey. He would go on to serve at the Chapel Royal through the reigns of four English monarchs.

## Four Sovereigns

The rustic epitaph on his tomb at Greenwich, charming in its earnestness, records the remarkable fact:

> *He served long tyme in chappel with grete prayse,*
> *Fower sovereygnes reignes, (a thing not often seene);*
> *I mean King Henry and Prince Edward's dayes,*
> *Quene Marie, and Elizabeth our quene.*

This span --- Henry VIII, Edward VI, Mary, Elizabeth I --- carried Tallis through the entire English Reformation, from the Catholic mass to the Protestant service. His adaptability was as remarkable as his gifts.

## The Organist Royal

Queen Elizabeth appointed him organist royal. His financial returns were modest --- his stipend was sevenpence a day --- though some gain came from the special patent she granted him to publish a collection of hymns and tunes. He shared this patent with his younger colleague, William Byrd.

## "Tallis' Evening Hymn"

Butterworth-Brown write that "for more than three hundred and fifty years devout people have enjoyed that melody of mingled dignity and sweetness known as 'Tallis' Evening Hymn.'" The tune is now inseparably joined to Thomas Ken's evening prayer:

> *Glory to Thee, my God, this night*
> *For all the blessings of the light.*

The tune was composed originally to Latin words, but when vernacular hymn use was introduced after the Reformation, Tallis apparently adapted his scores freely to either language.

## The Anglican School

Butterworth-Brown describe the musical difference between Tallis and his Italian contemporary Giovanni Palestrina: English church music "differed less from the Italian (or Catholic) psalmody than that of the Continental churches, where the revolt of the Reformation extended to the tune-worship as notably as to the sacraments and sermons." The extremer Protestants --- the Puritan or Genevan school --- represented their style in "Old Hundred," while the Anglican is represented by "Tallis' Evening Hymn." The two schools, Butterworth-Brown note, "were gradually reconciled, and together made the model after which the best sacred tunes are built."

Tallis was buried in the old parish church at Greenwich in November 1585.

## Musical and Thematic Context

Butterworth-Brown place Tallis in a direct lineage from Palestrina, as the English parallel to the Italian master. Where Palestrina rescued contrapuntal music for Roman worship, Tallis shaped what church music would sound like in the English-speaking world for centuries.
