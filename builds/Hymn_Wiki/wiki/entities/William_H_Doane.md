# William Howard Doane

William Howard Doane led two lives simultaneously: one as a successful Cincinnati manufacturer, the other as one of the most prolific gospel composers of his century. The combination was unusual, but Doane wore both roles with equal earnestness, and it was the gospel work that made him immortal.

## The Manufacturer and the Musician

Doane was born in 1832 and built a business career that afforded him the time and resources to pursue sacred music as a serious vocation rather than a livelihood. He held the honorary degree of Mus.D., testifying to his standing in the musical community. He composed hundreds of tunes for evangelical texts and became, above all, the composer whose name is inseparable from [[Fanny_Crosby]].

## The Crosby Partnership

The Doane-Crosby collaboration produced some of the most sung music in nineteenth-century American evangelicalism. Doane set her texts "Rescue the Perishing" and "Safe in the Arms of Jesus" to tunes that became the standard musical clothing for those words — so much so that most singers have never heard those hymns in any other setting. He also composed the tune for Kate Hankey's "Tell Me the Old Old Story."

His tune for "To the Work," a marching gospel song calling Christians to active service, gave that text the vigor its message required. His setting of the Prodigal Son theme carried a similar directness.

## Character of His Music

Doane's tunes are marked by melodic accessibility and rhythmic clarity. They sit comfortably in the throat and in the memory. Whether tender (as in "Safe in the Arms of Jesus") or urgent (as in "Rescue the Perishing"), they serve their texts without overwhelming them. His instinct was always to support the singer rather than to display the composer.

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Burrage gives Doane a detailed biographical treatment that places him firmly in the Baptist world. He was born in Preston, New London County, Connecticut, on February 3, 1832, and educated at the Academy at Woodstock. His business career traced a rise from his father's cotton manufacturing counting-room through increasing responsibility: he managed J. A. Fay and Company's western operations in Chicago, became a partner in 1860, and settled permanently in Cincinnati as company president. The company's sole manufacturing establishment moved to Cincinnati with him.

Doane was converted in 1847, baptized by Rev. Frederic Denison in 1851, and united with Central Baptist Church in Norwich, Connecticut. He was married in 1857 to Fanny M. Treat, daughter of his father's business partner. At the time of Burrage's writing he resided at Mount Auburn, a suburb of Cincinnati, and was a prominent member of Mount Auburn Baptist Church.

Burrage traces Doane's musical development from childhood with unusual specificity: singing in public at age six, in the church choir at ten, accomplished flutist at twelve, playing double bass viol at thirteen, cabinet organ at fifteen, beginning musical composition shortly thereafter. His instructors included Holbrook, B. F. Baker, A. N. Johnson, and the German musician Kanhoiser. In 1852-54 he conducted the Norwich Harmonic Society.

His first Sunday school hymn book, "Sabbath Gems," appeared in 1861. In collaboration with [[Robert_Lowry]] he subsequently published an extensive series of music books, including "Pure Gold," "Royal Diadem," "Temple Anthems," "Tidal Wave," "Brightest and Best," "Welcome Tidings," "Fountain of Song," "Good as Gold," "Glad Hosanna," "Joyful Lays," and "Glad Refrain." He was also co-editor with Lowry on "The Gospel Hymn and Tune Book" for the American Baptist Publication Society, and a musical editor of "The Baptist Hymnal." Denison University conferred the Mus.D. in 1875.
