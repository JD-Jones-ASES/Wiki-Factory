# Johann Heermann

## A Poet Forged in Suffering

Johann Heermann ranks with [[Martin_Luther]] and [[Paul_Gerhardt]] as one of the three greatest hymn-writers the Lutheran Church has produced --- a judgment that becomes more understandable when you know what he lived through. His hymns were written not from a comfortable study but from the wreckage of a town repeatedly sacked, burned, and plagued during the [[Thirty_Years_War_Hymns|Thirty Years' War]].

## Biographical Sketch

Heermann was born at Rauden, Silesia, on October 11, 1585, the youngest of five children. Four of his siblings died in quick succession, and little Johann too became gravely ill. His mother, in despair, made a vow to God: if He would spare her last child, she would raise him for the ministry.

God took her at her word. The boy recovered, received his education at several institutions, and in 1611 entered the Lutheran ministry as pastor of the church at Koeben in Silesia, not far from his birthplace.

## The Thirty Years' War

A few years into his pastorate, the Thirty Years' War erupted across Germany. No one felt its horror more personally than Heermann and his people in Koeben.

- In 1616, fire nearly destroyed the city.
- From 1629 to 1634, Wallenstein's Catholic Imperial army sacked Koeben four separate times, attempting to restore the German principalities to Rome by force.
- In 1631, the dreadful plague struck the town.

Heermann was forced to flee repeatedly, losing all his earthly possessions more than once. Crossing the Oder River, he was pursued by enemy soldiers who shot after him. Twice he was nearly killed by the sword. Yet through all of this, he kept writing hymns --- and the experiences of flight, loss, and grace under fire pour off every page.

## "Ah, Holy Jesus" --- Herzliebster Jesu

His masterpiece is "Herzliebster Jesu" (1630), translated into English most famously as "Ah, Holy Jesus, How Hast Thou Offended." It is a meditation on the Passion of Christ --- the singer confronting the fact that it was his own sin that brought Jesus to the cross:

> *Who was the guilty? Who brought this upon Thee?*
> *Alas, my treason, Jesus, hath undone Thee!*
> *'Twas I, Lord Jesus, I it was denied Thee:*
> *I crucified Thee.*

The hymn immediately became beloved throughout Germany, partly because it captured what Protestants everywhere felt in those years of suffering --- and partly because of the gripping tune composed for it in 1640 by the great musician Johann Crueger (the same composer who later partnered with [[Paul_Gerhardt]]). Johann Sebastian Bach set it in his St. John Passion and St. Matthew Passion.

One stanza rarely included in translations reveals the unfaltering faith that sustained Heermann through his trials:

> *Whate'er of earthly good this life may grant me*
> *I'll risk for Thee; no shame, no cross shall daunt me;*
> *I shall not fear what man can do to harm me,*
> *Nor death alarm me.*

## Other Hymns

**"O Christ, Our True and Only Light"** --- a hymn for the unconverted, praying that Christ's light would reach those in spiritual darkness.

**"O Jesus, Saviour Dear"** --- the hymn associated with the battle of Leuthen (December 5, 1757), where Frederick the Great's Prussian army, facing Austrians three times their number, spontaneously began singing its second stanza before the battle. When asked if the music should be silenced, Frederick replied: "No, let it be. With such men God will today certainly give me the victory." The Prussians won. Afterward Frederick exclaimed: "My God, what a power has religion!"

**"Lord, Thy Death and Passion Give"** and **"Faithful God, I Lay Before Thee"** --- further examples of Heermann's gift for personal devotional verse in the midst of public catastrophe.

His collection known as "Songs of Tears" (1630) also contains a striking hymn called "Treuer Wächter Israel," imploring God to "build a wall around us." A remarkable story from 1814 tells of a widow's home in Schleswig that was miraculously bypassed by invading Allied troops --- a snowdrift had hidden it from view. Her grandson, who had been reading the hymn aloud just days before, recognized the answer to Heermann's prayer.

## Significance

Heermann's hymns have an urgency that only suffering can produce. He did not write from distance or theory; he wrote as a man who had stared down death and loss repeatedly and found in Christ the only ground that held firm. Ryden notes that "because his hymns were written during such times of distress and suffering, they seemed to grip the hearts of the German people to an extraordinary degree."

He belongs squarely in the tradition of [[Thirty_Years_War_Hymns|war-born hymnody]] that also includes [[Martin_Rinkart]]'s thanksgiving and [[Paul_Gerhardt]]'s later meditations on trust and nature.
