# Charlotte Elliott

Dwight L. Moody, who had seen more conversions than most men could dream of, said of Charlotte Elliott's hymn "Just as I am" that it had probably touched more hearts and brought more souls to Christ than any other hymn ever written. Her own brother, a minister, said much the same: in a long ministry, he hoped he had seen some fruit, "but I feel far more has been done by a single hymn of my sister's."

Charlotte Elliott herself spent most of her eighty-two years on a sickbed. She was rarely well. She often endured seasons of great physical distress. What she gave the Church came at real cost.

## A Soul Seeking Peace

Charlotte Elliott was born in 1789 and grew up in a devout home, a daughter of the Church. And yet, like so many earnest souls, she had never found true peace with God. She felt, as so many do, that something must be done to *earn* salvation --- that a person could not simply come to Christ as a helpless sinner and find complete redemption.

In 1822, the noted Swiss preacher Dr. Caesar Malan came to visit the Elliott home in Brighton, England. He quickly discerned the cause of her spiritual perplexity and spoke plainly to her: *"You have nothing of merit to bring to God. You must come just as you are, a sinner, to the Lamb of God that taketh away the sin of the world."*

Throughout the rest of her life, Charlotte Elliott celebrated the anniversary of that day as her spiritual birthday. Though it was fourteen years before she wrote her hymn, she never forgot the words of Dr. Malan. They form the very core and essence of it.

## Written on a Couch

"Just as I am" was written in 1836. The inspiration came one day when the frail invalid had been left alone at her brother's home. She was lying on a couch, pondering the words Dr. Malan had spoken so many years before, when suddenly the whole glorious truth of salvation as the free gift of God flashed upon her soul.

She rose from the couch and wrote:

> Just as I am, without one plea
> But that Thy blood was shed for me,
> And that Thou bidd'st me come to Thee,
> O Lamb of God, I come, I come!

She published the hymn that same year in the second edition of *The Invalid's Hymn Book*, to which she had contributed 115 pieces.

The hymn sets forth, in very simple but gripping words, the all-important truth that we are not saved through any merit of our own, but by the sovereign grace of God through faith in Jesus Christ. It does not flatter the soul or minimize its condition. It pictures utter helplessness and wretchedness --- and then very lovingly invites the soul to come to Him "whose blood can cleanse each spot."

After her death, more than a thousand letters were found among her papers, written by people who wished to express their gratitude for what the hymn had brought them.

## The Cost of Writing

Charlotte Elliott described her own afflictions with unflinching honesty:

*"He knows, and He alone, what it is, day after day, hour after hour, to fight against bodily feelings of almost overpowering weakness, languor and exhaustion, to resolve not to yield to slothfulness, depression and instability, such as the body causes me to long to indulge."*

Her own suffering became her ministry. Very beautifully she resigned herself to the will of God: *"God sees, God guides, God guards me. His grace surrounds me, and His voice continually bids me to be happy and holy in His service, just where I am."*

## The Hymns

Charlotte Elliott wrote some 150 hymns in all. By common consent she is given first place among English women hymn-writers. Her finest, aside from "Just as I am," is "My God, my Father, while I stray."

## Legacy

There is something deeply moving about the fact that the most soul-winning hymn in English history was written by a woman who could not leave her house, who lay on a couch on the day inspiration came, and who spent her life fighting the temptation to call herself useless.

She was not useless. She was one of the most fruitful believers in the history of the Church.

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

Butterworth-Brown supply additional details about the hymn's origin and reach. They record that Dr. Caesar Malan's words came to Charlotte Elliott simply: "Come just as you are." The hymn tells the result.

They include a vignette from the eloquent John B. Gough, who found himself seated at church next to a man of strange appearance — face mottled, limbs and mouth twitching. When the congregation sang "Just as I am," the man leaned toward Gough and asked how the next verse began. It was:

> Just as I am, poor, wretched, blind.

"That's it," sobbed the strange man, "I'm blind — God help me! — and I'm wretched — and paralytic," and then he tried hard to sing the line with the rest. "After that," said Gough, "the poor paralytic's singing was as sweet to me as a Beethoven symphony."

On the tune, Butterworth-Brown note that [[William_Bradbury]]'s "Woodworth" has mostly superseded Mason's "Elliott" and is now the accepted music — and that the hymn's currency "has been too swift for its authorship and history to keep up with."

## Hymnological Context (Duffield)

Duffield (1886) treats 8 of Charlotte Elliott's hymns, providing several details of biographical and textual significance.

On **"Just as I Am"**, Duffield records that the son-in-law of the poet Wordsworth wrote to Miss Elliott thanking her for the hymn, saying it "had afforded comfort to his wife on her dying-bed." He also notes that the hymn had been translated into French by a significant French hymn-writer — "the author of three hundred of the best French hymns could not be indifferent to the verses of his English friend when rendered into his own tongue." The hymn was composed "on the north shore" (of a lake, Duffield implies, during a period of illness). He cites the dying words of a person urged to "cut the cable" of worldly attachments — words directly evoked by the hymn.

On **"My God, My Father! While I Stray"**, Duffield notes an important textual correction: the second occurrence of "my" is actually incorrect — she wrote "My God and Father" (singular). He also confirms that the stanza "If thou shouldst call me to resign" was originally written in the past tense as a personal statement, later generalized.

On **"Christian, Seek Not Yet Repose"**, Duffield gives six stanzas as the original count and identifies its first publication.

On **"The Sabbath-Day Has Reached Its Close"** (1839), Duffield records a "beautiful tradition" about the occasion of its composition.

Duffield observes that Miss Elliott's hymns were notable for reaching the sympathies of ordinary people — not just the theologically sophisticated — which he attributes to their directness of personal address.
