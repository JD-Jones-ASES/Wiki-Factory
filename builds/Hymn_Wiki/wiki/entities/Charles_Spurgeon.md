# Charles Haddon Spurgeon

Charles Haddon Spurgeon was born June 19, 1834, at Kelvedon, Essex. His father was pastor of an Independent church; at an early age Charles was placed under the care of his grandfather, also an Independent minister, at Stambourne in the same county. He attended a private academy at Colchester (his father's later residence), studied a year at an agricultural college at Maidstone, and then worked as an assistant in a school at Newmarket.

## Conversion and Early Ministry

In the autumn of 1850, Spurgeon became deeply interested in his spiritual welfare. A few months later, at a Primitive Methodist Chapel in Colchester, he heard a sermon from the text "Look unto me, and be ye saved, all ye ends of the earth" (Isaiah 45:22). According to his own testimony, he gave himself to the Lord Jesus Christ then and there. When considering how to confess his faith publicly, he decided to unite with a Baptist church, and on May 3, 1851, was baptized at Isleham, near Newmarket.

He removed to Cambridge, where he united with the Baptist church in St. Andrews Street — the very church where [[Robert_Robinson]] had once been pastor, and afterward Robert Hall. Here he engaged in Christian work as opportunity offered, preaching his first sermon at Teversham at age sixteen with a license as a lay preacher.

In 1852, he was called to the pastorate of the little Baptist church at Waterbeach. Crowds flocked to hear him. His fame reached London, and in the autumn of 1853, the deacons of [[John_Rippon]]'s old church in New Park Street invited him to preach as a supply. The impression he made was so powerful that he at once received a call to the permanent pastorate.

## The Metropolitan Tabernacle

The growing congregation quickly outgrew the New Park Street building, and Spurgeon preached at Exeter Hall and then at the Surrey Gardens Music Hall (capacity seven thousand). The Metropolitan Tabernacle was built with a corner-stone laid in August 1859 and completed in 1861 at a cost of one hundred and fifty thousand dollars. It seated fifty-five hundred, with standing room for a thousand more.

When the church took possession of the Tabernacle it had a membership of 1,178; by the time Burrage was writing in 1888, membership had grown to upward of five thousand. Connected with the church were the Pastor's College (training young men for ministry), almshouses, and orphan asylums. His brother Rev. James A. Spurgeon had served as associate pastor since 1868.

Spurgeon's sermons were published weekly and widely circulated in English and in translations. His commentary on the Psalms, entitled *The Treasury of David* (seven volumes), is still in use.

## Our Own Hymn Book

In 1866, Spurgeon published *Our Own Hymn Book, a Collection of Psalms and Hymns for Public, Social and Private Worship*. This admirable collection represents 220 authors in 1,129 hymns. Spurgeon's own contributions were fourteen psalms and ten hymns, plus three he had altered.

A favorite among his original hymns begins:

*Sweetly the holy hymn / Breaks on the morning air; / Before the world with smoke is dim / We meet to offer prayer.*

But the hymn Spurgeon himself liked best, and which has found its way into many collections, begins:

*The Holy Ghost is here, / Where saints in prayer agree; / As Jesus' parting gift, he's near / Each pleading company.*

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Burrage treats Spurgeon as a living figure (the book was published in 1888, four years before Spurgeon's death in 1892), and includes him in the English Baptist section as the preeminent representative of his generation. The pastoral connection from Rippon to Spurgeon — via the New Park Street / Metropolitan Tabernacle congregation — is one of the great unbroken lines in English Baptist institutional history, running from Keach's era straight through to the Victorian age.

Spurgeon's *Our Own Hymn Book* performs a function analogous to what Rippon's *Selection* had done a century earlier: gathering the best of the tradition into a single authoritative collection for Baptist corporate worship.
