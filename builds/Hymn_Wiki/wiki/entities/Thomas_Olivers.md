# Thomas Olivers

Few conversion stories in hymn history are as dramatic as that of Thomas Olivers. Born in Tregynon, near Newtown, Montgomeryshire, Wales, in 1725, he was according to local testimony "the worst boy known in all that country for thirty years." Left an orphan at five years old, he was passed from one relative to another until all were tired of him and he was "bound out" to a shoemaker. Almost inevitably the neglected lad grew up wicked, and as he sank lower in the vices of bad company, there were more kicks for him than helping hands.

## The Conversion at Bristol

When Olivers was eighteen, his reputation in his home town had grown so unsavory that he was forced to shift for himself elsewhere. Providence led him, shabby and penniless, to the old seaport town of Bristol, where Whitefield was preaching. Whitefield's text was, "Is not this a brand plucked out of the fire?" (Zach. 3:2).

"When that sermon began," Olivers said, "I was one of the most abandoned and profligate young men living; before it ended I was a new creature. The world was all changed for Tom Olivers."

His new life lasted on earth more than sixty useful years. He left a shining record as a preacher of righteousness and died in the triumphs of faith in November 1799. Before he passed away he saw at least thirty editions of his great hymn published.

## The God of Abraham Praise

The story of how Olivers composed his famous hymn is one of the most distinctive in all of hymnody. He visited the great "Duke's Place" Synagogue at Aldgate, London, and heard Meyer Lyon (known as Leoni) sing the Yigdal — the long Jewish doxology — to an air so noble and impressive that it haunted him until he learned it and fitted to it his own sublime stanzas:

> The God of Abraham praise,
>   Who reigns enthroned above;
> Ancient of everlasting days,
>   And God of love.
> Jehovah, Great I AM!
>   By earth and heaven confessed,
> I bow and bless the sacred Name,
>   Forever blest.

Lyon was a noted Jewish musician and vocalist who served as chorister of the London Synagogue during the latter part of the eighteenth century. The Yigdal itself was a portion of the Hebrew Liturgy composed in medieval times, said to have been written by Daniel Ben Judah. Olivers gratefully put the singer's name to the tune, which is why it is known as "Leoni."

The Methodist leaders who took Olivers from his shoemaker's bench to become one of their preachers answered any suggestion that he had simply copied the Jewish hymn: he knew no Hebrew, and a literal translation of the Yigdal shows hardly a similarity to his evangelical lines. Only the music as Leoni sang it prompted his own song.

The poet Montgomery, who greatly admired the hymn's majestic style and glorious imagery, said of its author: "The man who wrote that hymn must have had the finest ear imaginable, for on account of the peculiar measure, none but a person of equal musical and poetic taste could have produced the harmony perceptible in the verse."

## Musical and Thematic Context

The tune "Leoni" is strongly Hebrew in tone and diction, drawing its inspiration from the Old Testament Psalter and from an actual synagogue chant. Butterworth-Brown describe the hymn as one of the great praise-songs that "drew its inspiration from the Hebrew Psalter, the text-book of all true praise-song." Its twelve eight-line stanzas — found in both the *Plymouth* and *Methodist Hymnals* — are too long for complete singing, but the eminent Methodist theologian Richard Watson reportedly died with lines of the hymn on his lips:

> I shall behold His face,
> I shall His power adore,
> And sing the wonders of His grace
>     Forevermore.
