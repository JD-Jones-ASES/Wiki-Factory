# Joseph Addison

Joseph Addison was born at Milston Rectory, Wiltshire, on May 1, 1672, the son of the Rev. Lancelot Addison, rector of Milston and afterwards Dean of Lichfield. Educated at Oxford, where his excellence in both English and Latin poetry gave him early reputation, he rose through patronage and talent to the highest offices of state: Commissioner of Appeals, Secretary to the Lord Lieutenant of Ireland, and ultimately Secretary of State in 1717. He died at Holland House, Kensington, on June 17, 1719.

Butterworth-Brown describe him simply as "the best English writer of his time." His hymns are said to number only five, "but they are remarkable for the simple beauty of their style, as well as for their Christian spirit."

## When All Thy Mercies, O My God

This hymn was written about 1701, in grateful commemoration of Addison's deliverance from a shipwreck in a storm off the coast of Genoa, Italy. It originally contained thirteen stanzas, of which four to six are commonly sung. Butterworth-Brown note that it "has put the language of devotional gratitude into the mouths of thousands of humble disciples who could but feebly frame their own":

> When all Thy mercies, O my God,
>   My rising soul surveys,
> Transported with the view I'm lost
>   In wonder, love and praise.

Another hymn — "How Are Thy Servants Blest, O Lord" — was probably composed after the same return from a foreign voyage, and has been called his "Traveller's Hymn."

Of his fine metrical version of the Twenty-Third Psalm — "The Lord My Pasture Shall Prepare" — tradition holds that he gathered its imagery as a boy living near Salisbury Plain, during his solitary two-mile walks to school at Amesbury and back again.

## Musical and Thematic Context

All his hymns appeared first in the *Spectator*, to which he was a prolific contributor. The tune "Geneva" — one of the rare survivals of the old "canon" musical method, in which parts enter one after another with identical notes — was long the sole partner of "When All Thy Mercies," composed by John Cole (born Tewkesbury 1774, came to Baltimore 1785, died 1855). "Manoah," adapted from Haydn's *Creation*, is also commended as "admirably suited to 'Addison's' hymn," as is "Belmont" by Samuel Webbe.

Addison represents the distinguished literary man who found his way to hymn-writing through genuine personal devotion — a tradition that also includes [[Oliver_Wendell_Holmes]] and [[John_Greenleaf_Whittier]]: figures who enriched the hymnal not through professional ministry but through the overflow of genuine religious feeling.
