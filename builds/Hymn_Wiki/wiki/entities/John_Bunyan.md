# John Bunyan

John Bunyan occupies a unique place among Baptist hymn writers: he connects to the tradition by only a slender tie. He was not, strictly speaking, a hymn writer. But certain lines embedded in *The Pilgrim's Progress* have been used in Baptist churches for well over two centuries, and his life is so woven into the fabric of English Nonconformist faith that no account of Baptist hymnody could pass him by.

## Life

John Bunyan was born in Elstow, near Bedford, in 1628 — the church register records "John the sonne of Thomas Bonnionn, Junr. the 30th of Novemb." His parents were poor but sent him to school to learn reading and writing. In his sixteenth year his mother and then his sister Margaret died; his father quickly remarried; and Bunyan's world changed. He served briefly in the army, returning to Elstow around 1646. Two or three years later he married, and the four years that followed were years of intense spiritual conflict which he later described in his spiritual autobiography *Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners*.

Peace came at last. In 1653 Bunyan united with Mr. Gifford's church in Bedford. His pastor soon died, and Bunyan — recognized by his brethren as gifted — began to preach. Crowds "came to hear the word by hundreds, and that from all parts." In November 1660 he was arrested for "devilishly and perniciously abstaining from coming to church" and for being "a common upholder of several unlawful meetings and conventicles." Then followed his twelve years' imprisonment in Bedford jail, from 1660 to 1672.

It was during a second, shorter imprisonment (winter 1675–76) that he wrote *The Pilgrim's Progress* — the book that has been translated into between seventy and eighty languages, sold in untold numbers of editions, and remains, after the Bible, the most widely read book in Christian history. Lord Macaulay said of his *Holy War*: "If the Pilgrim's Progress did not exist, it would be the best allegory that was ever written."

Bunyan's last years were busy with writing and preaching. Wherever he went, crowds gathered. He died in London, August 31, 1688, and was buried in Bunhill Fields — the great Nonconformist burial ground.

## His Connection to Hymnody

Burrage notes that Dr. Belcher (*Historical Sketches of Hymns*) records that certain lines in the Second Part of *The Pilgrim's Progress* "have long been used in some of the Baptist churches in England at the admission of members." These are the words Bunyan puts into the lips of Mercy as she and Christiana set out on their pilgrimage:

*Let the Most Blessed be my guide, / If 't be his blessed will, / Unto his gate, into his fold, / Up to his holy hill.*

Another set of lines — the shepherd boy's song — has been more widely used:

*He that is down needs fear no fall; / He that is low no pride; / He that is humble ever shall / Have God to be his guide.*

Burrage remarks: "Had Bunyan lived a century later, the treasury of Christian song would doubtless have been greatly enriched by hymns from his pen." The observation is probably right. Bunyan had the gift of plainness, the gift of moving narrative, and the deep conviction of a man who had suffered greatly for his faith. All that was lacking was the context — the tradition of congregational singing had not yet been established in English Baptist worship when he lived. That establishment was left to his near-contemporary, [[Benjamin_Keach]].

## Place in the Tradition

Spurgeon's Metropolitan Tabernacle was in the direct pastoral line of the Cambridge church where [[Robert_Robinson]] had ministered — and Bunyan's Bedford church was another of those founding congregations of English Dissent. His *Pilgrim's Progress* has never left the canon of Baptist devotional reading, and the Bunhill Fields graveyard where he lies buried contains also [[Isaac_Watts]], [[John_Gill]], and the Wesleys' mother Susanna.
