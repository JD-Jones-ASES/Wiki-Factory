# Timothy Swan

Timothy Swan was born July 23, 1758, in Worcester, Massachusetts, the eighth of thirteen children of William and Lavina Swan. He died on the same date in 1842, completing his eighty-fourth year exactly.

## The Self-Taught Hatter

Swan's musical education consisted of three weeks at a singing school in Groton. With that preparation alone, he began composing airs. In 1774, at seventeen, he apprenticed himself to a hatter in Northfield, and it was while working at the hat trade that he composed most of his early tunes — "Montague" being the first, then "Poland" and many others, which circulated in manuscript form across much of New England. He was intensely curious about [[William_Billings]], who "for a long time gave direction to the music of New England," and eventually met him in Boston.

At twenty-five he married a daughter of the Rev. Ebenezer Gay, D.D. of Suffield, Connecticut, and lived there for twenty-eight years, writing most of the music he eventually published. He removed to Northfield, Massachusetts, in 1807 and remained there until his death.

## "China"

Swan considered "China" (composed 1790, first sung publicly 1794) his best tune, and the public agreed — it is the only one that survived into modern hymn books. Written in a minor key, it was usually set to Watts's words "Why Do We Mourn for Dying Friends?" Critics called it "one of the most unscientific tunes ever published," but the people found it the most effective. As Metcalf observed: "Science did for him almost nothing — nature everything." (Metcalf, p. 103-107)

## Character

Swan was a fine-looking gentleman, well-read, with a retentive memory and genial temperament. He sat up reading until past midnight, leading his Northfield neighbors to say he was "poor, proud, and indolent." He was an ardent admirer of Robert Burns and often wrote poetry in the Scottish dialect. He was in charge of the Northfield library for a long time, was a frequent contributor of poetry to local papers, and loved the lilac — he planted three rows of Lombardy poplars around his house and guarded the blackbirds that nested in them as his pets.

## Publications

His main publications were *The Songster's Assistant* (a secular collection, c. 1800), *The Songster's Museum* (1803, anonymous), and *The New England Harmony* (Northampton, 1801) — 123 pages, containing psalm tunes, set pieces, and anthems. The family reported it was a financial loss and never went beyond a first edition.
