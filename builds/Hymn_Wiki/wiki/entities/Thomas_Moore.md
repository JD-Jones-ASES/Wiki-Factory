# Thomas Moore

Thomas Moore was Ireland's most celebrated poet in his own lifetime — the author of *Irish Melodies*, *Lalla Rookh*, and many political and satirical verses. He was born in Dublin in 1779 and died in 1852. His friendship with Lord Byron and his nationalist sympathies made him one of the most famous literary men of the early nineteenth century.

Moore was not an evangelical Christian, and "Come Ye Disconsolate" was not written as a hymn. Butterworth-Brown date the poem to about 1814, and note that it "differed somewhat" in its original form from the hymn now sung.

## Come Ye Disconsolate

Thomas Hastings was the one who transformed Moore's poem into a usable congregational hymn, making several significant emendations:

- Moore's "Come, at God's altar fervently kneel" became "Come to the mercy seat"
- Moore's "Hope when all others die" became "Hope of the penitent"
- The final stanza was largely rewritten in evangelical terms

Butterworth-Brown are generous to both: "Dr. Hastings was not much of a poet, but he could make a *singable* hymn, and he knew the rhythm and accent needed in a hymn-tune." The determination was to make "an evangelical hymn of a poem 'too good to lose.'" But it is noted fairly that "it was to Moore, however, that the real hymn-thought and key-note first came, and the title-line and the sweet refrain are his own — for which the Christian world has thanked him."

> Come, ye disconsolate, where'er ye languish,
> Come to the mercy seat, fervently kneel;
> Here bring your wounded hearts, here tell your anguish;
> Earth has no sorrow that heaven cannot heal.

## Musical and Thematic Context

The tune is attributed to Samuel Webbe — possibly an arrangement of a German air to which Moore says he originally set the words. Butterworth-Brown invoke the principle of musical affinity: "One can scarcely conceive a happier yoking of counterparts. Try singing 'Come ye Disconsolate' to 'Rescue the Perishing,' for example, and we shall feel the impertinence of divorcing a hymn that has found its musical affinity."

Moore represents the gifted secular poet who contributes to the hymnal by accident — through a single lyric that the church recognized as too good to leave outside its walls.
