# Elizabeth Cecilia Clephane

Elizabeth Cecilia Clephane lived only thirty-eight years and died not knowing that the poem she had written in a quiet moment would move thousands to tears in her very city. She did not live to see her hymn in print. But a sister survived her, and was there when the story was told.

## The Poem in the Pocket

Clephane was born in Edinburgh on June 10, 1830, and died of consumption on February 19, 1869, at Melrose, Scotland. She had written "The Ninety and Nine" early in 1868 and published it in a small religious paper, *The Christian Age*.

Two or three years later [[Ira_Sankey]] saw the poem in the paper, thought it might be useful, and cut it out. He carried it in his vest pocket for weeks. Then came the moment in an Edinburgh meeting when D.L. Moody, just finishing a discourse on the Good Shepherd, turned to Sankey in a quiet aside and said: "Sing something."

## The Miraculous Tune

Sankey had no tune. With a silent prayer for guidance, he took out the slip of paper with Clephane's poem, placed it on the little reed organ, and began to play and sing. He had to read the unfamiliar words and simultaneously invent the melody. The tune came, and grew as he sang through the first verse. He remembered it well enough to repeat it with the second, and after that finished the hymn.

A new melody had been born in the presence of more than a thousand people. Not a word was lost. Sankey said afterward: "It was the most intense moment of my life." He had a tearful audience to reward him.

In a touching meeting afterward, a sister of Mrs. Clephane told Sankey that the authoress had not lived to see her hymn in print, and had never known of its blessed mission.

## The Hymn

"The Ninety and Nine" paraphrases the parable of the lost sheep from Luke 15. The first stanza places the ninety-nine safely in the fold while one is "out on the hills away, / Far off from the gates of gold." The Shepherd searches through mountains "thunder-riven" and over "rocky steeps," until the final stanza: "Rejoice! for the Lord brings back His own." Butterworth-Brown print the opening and closing stanzas as representative of the hymn's power.

## Legacy

Clephane also wrote "Beneath the Cross of Jesus," which has become a standard Lenten and Good Friday hymn. Her output was small, her life brief, her reputation built on two or three pieces --- but those pieces are among the most enduring in the Gospel tradition.
