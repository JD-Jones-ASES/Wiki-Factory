# John Leland

Elder John Leland was born in Grafton, Massachusetts, in 1754. He was a strenuous personality in the Baptist denomination and well known throughout New England as a revival preacher. He was "the minister who wrote his own hymns" — a habit he shared with Watts and Doddridge, and which he practiced as a matter of practical pastoral strategy rather than literary ambition. He died in North Adams, Massachusetts, on January 14, 1844.

## The Cheshire Cheese

Butterworth-Brown give full and delighted weight to the most famous episode of Leland's career. The farmers of Cheshire, Massachusetts, conceived the plan of sending "the biggest cheese in America" to President Jefferson as a gift from a loyal Republican district. Leland — a good Democrat and then a settled pastor in Cheshire — offered to go to Washington on an ox-team with it and "preach all the way," which he actually did.

The cheese weighed 1,450 pounds. It arrived in Washington in 1801.

## The Day Is Past and Gone

Of Leland's hymns, only one has survived in common use: his simple evening song, sung to Amzi Chapin's tune (which appeared as early as 1805):

> The day is past and gone,
>   The evening shades appear,
> O may we all remember well
>   The night of death draws near.

Butterworth-Brown note that "gray-headed men and women remember being sung to sleep by their mothers with that old-fashioned evening song" — and that "with all its solemnity and other-worldliness it is dear to recollection, and its five stanzas are lovingly hunted up in the few hymnals where it is found."

## Musical and Thematic Context

Leland's other revival hymns served their generation and passed away with it. He wrote hymns for specific pastoral purposes — to "fortify" baptismal candidates against the cold ("Christians, if your hearts are warm"), to rally members from bad-weather absences. The hymns were functional first and literary second, and most functioned their way into oblivion. But "The Day Is Past and Gone" outlived its occasion and became something more: a quiet, honest evening meditation that carries the weight of everything the old New England piety felt about mortality and trust.

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Burrage corrects a detail: Leland died on January 14, 1841 (not 1844 as sometimes given), at North Adams after being taken ill following his last sermon on January 8. Burrage also fills in the outline of his ministry more precisely. Baptized at Northbridge by Rev. Noah Alden of Bellingham in 1774, Leland received a license from the Bellingham Baptist Church and went to Virginia in October 1775, where he was ordained. He labored there for about fifteen years, preaching 3,009 sermons and baptizing 700 converts. Back in Massachusetts, he settled at Cheshire for the remainder of his life; by 1821 the total number of persons he had baptized had risen to 1,352.

His written works — occasional sermons, essays on moral, religious, and political subjects — were collected after his death in a large octavo volume edited by Miss L. F. Greene of Lanesborough.

The origin of "Christians, If Your Hearts Be Warm" is preserved in Leland's own words in Burrage. Writing about a Virginia meeting in 1788, he recalled: "I had a meeting at John Lea's in Louisa, when something seemed to descend on the people... The next day there were five to be baptized. The day was very cold. While Mr. Bowles was preaching to the people, I composed the hymn, 'Christians, if your hearts be warm.'" The hymn originally had three stanzas; three more were added later.

Burrage quotes Rev. S. W. Duffield's assessment of "The Day Is Past and Gone": "There is an Ambrosian simplicity about this hymn which suggests at once a pure and unaffected piety, like that of the early church. The piece is really classic in its unpretending beauty." Duffield also cited a remarkable incident from the siege of Vicksburg (June 5, 1863), recorded in a lady's diary: her house had just been struck by a shell, and after the soldiers helped board up the damage, she descended as usual to the cellar for the night — thinking of "the evening hymn that grandmother taught me when a child: 'Lord, keep us safe this night, secure from all our fears; may angels guard us while we sleep, till morning light appears.'"
