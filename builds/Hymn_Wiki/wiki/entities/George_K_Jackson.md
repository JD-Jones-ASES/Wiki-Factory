# George K. Jackson

Dr. George K. Jackson was the most formally trained musician in early Boston --- an Englishman of great learning and considerable girth who played the organ with "great power and pure touch" and linked the musical culture of the Old World to the New.

## Life and Career

Jackson was born in Oxford in 1745 and was a schoolmate there of Raynor Taylor, another musician who would eventually settle in Philadelphia. At eleven he was a choir boy in the Chapel Royal; he studied under the celebrated Dr. James Nares and received his diploma from Saint Andrews College in 1791. Before emigrating, he published *A Treatise on Practical Thorough Bass* and various songs, and married the eldest daughter of a London physician, by whom he had eleven children.

He came to America in 1796, stopping in Alexandria, Baltimore, Philadelphia, and New York before reaching Boston in 1812. In Boston he taught, conducted oratorios, and served as organist in a series of churches --- Brattle Street, King's Chapel, Trinity, and Saint Paul's, where he was engaged at "a salary extraordinarily high for that time." (Metcalf, p. 47-48) He held the Saint Paul's position until his death in 1823.

Two of his sons ran a music store on Market Street in Boston around 1800. When Jackson died, his estate totaled only $98.86, including "129 volumes of old music books appraised at six cents apiece" --- an ironic close for so learned a musician.

## Musical Significance

It was Jackson, in his capacity as organist of the Handel and Haydn Society, who examined and approved Lowell Mason's first manuscript collection. His endorsement was the decisive moment: without it, Mason's *Handel and Haydn Collection of Sacred Music* (1822) might never have been published, and the course of American sacred music would have been different.

A contemporary observer described him as "of vast ponderosity, and like Falstaff, 'larded the lean earth as he walked along'" --- a physically imposing figure who played voluntaries that were "beyond anything I had heard." (Metcalf, p. 50) His music did not survive into common use, but his influence on the musicians around him --- Mason, [[Gottlieb_Graupner]], and others --- shaped the Boston musical world of the early nineteenth century.
