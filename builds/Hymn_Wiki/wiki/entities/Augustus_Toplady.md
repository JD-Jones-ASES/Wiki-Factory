# Augustus Toplady

A British magazine once invited its readers to name the hundred English hymns they most esteemed. 3,500 persons responded. "Rock of Ages" was named first by 3,215 of them.

That is the legacy of Augustus Montague Toplady, who lived only thirty-seven years, who fought bitterly with John Wesley, and who wrote his most famous hymn while dying of consumption --- and managed to write it in a way that has brought comfort to millions who knew nothing of the quarrel.

## Converted in a Barn

Toplady was born on November 4, 1740, at Farnham, England. His father, a major in the English army, was killed the following year. The widowed mother later moved to Ireland, where Augustus was educated at Trinity College, Dublin.

It was in Ireland, at sixteen years of age, that he was converted to Christ. The circumstances were as unlikely as they were memorable. He chanced to attend an evangelistic service held in a barn. The preacher was an unlettered layman who could "hardly spell his own name." But his message gripped the heart of the young man.

Toplady afterward wrote of the experience:

*"Strange that I who had so long sat under the means of grace in England should be brought nigh unto God in an obscure part of Ireland, amidst a handful of people met together in a barn, and by the ministry of one who could hardly spell his own name. Surely it was the Lord's doing and is marvelous."*

## The Quarrel with Wesley

Toplady was ordained at twenty-two as a minister of the Church of England. He was a convinced Calvinist, and he had no patience with John Wesley's Arminian theology. The dispute between the two men was unfortunately bitter, even by the standards of theological controversy. Wesley called Toplady a "chimney-sweep" and "a lively coxcomb." Toplady retorted by calling Wesley "Pope John" and declaring that his forehead was "petrified" and "impervious to a blush."

There are reasons for believing that "Rock of Ages" was born from this controversy. It appeared in the March 1776 issue of *The Gospel Magazine*, of which Toplady was editor, appended to a curious article in which the author attempted to calculate by mathematical computation how dreadful is the sum total of sins committed by a man in a lifetime --- and how impossible it is for a sinner to redeem himself from this debt. But Christ has paid the entire debt. It was this glorious thought that inspired him to sing:

> Rock of Ages, cleft for me,
> Let me hide myself in Thee.

The article was almost certainly aimed at Wesley's theology. The hymn, however, transcended the argument entirely.

## The Hymn

Ryden writes that the strength of "Rock of Ages" lies undoubtedly *"in the clear and simple manner in which it sets forth the glorious truth that we are saved by grace alone, through the merits of Christ."* Even a child can understand:

> Nothing in my hand I bring,
> Simply to Thy cross I cling.

The hymn has been translated into almost every known language. An old Chinese woman who had spent her life digging a well in an act of penance to her heathen gods was converted to Christianity, and when she was eighty years old she held out her crippled hands and sang: *"Nothing in my hands I bring."*

## The Death

Toplady was frail of body, and years before his death he was stricken with consumption. It was while fighting the ravages of this disease that he wrote his famous hymn.

A few hours before his death he exclaimed: *"My heart beats every day stronger and stronger for glory. Sickness is no affliction, pain no curse, death itself no dissolution."* His last words were: *"My prayers are all converted into praises."*

Friends had expressed hope for his recovery. Toplady shook his head:

*"No mortal man can live after the glories which God has manifested to my soul."*

He had requested to be buried beneath the gallery of Totenham Court Chapel --- a building built by Whitefield, where Wesley himself had preached Whitefield's funeral sermon. Perhaps it was Toplady's way of expressing the hope that all the bitterness of his controversy with Wesley might be buried with him.

Today, when we find "Rock of Ages" on one page of our hymnals and Charles Wesley's "Jesus, Lover of my soul" on the next, the old quarrel seems as distant as it ought to be. Two men of God, both used by grace --- their hymns neighbors now, for eternity.

## Legacy

Toplady died in 1778, at thirty-seven. He had been fierce in debate and tender in prayer, a man whose Calvinism was no cold creed but a flame of devotion. The hymn born from controversy became the comfort of the dying around the world. Toplady would have recognized the irony, and it would have pleased him.

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

Butterworth-Brown add rich detail to Toplady's story. They note that the popular account of the hymn's origin — that it was "written after the author, during a suburban walk, had been forced to shelter himself from a thundershower, under a cliff" — is "but one of several stories about the birth-occasion of the hymn."

They document the hymn's remarkable reach across cultures and crises. A foreign dignitary visiting Queen Victoria at her "Golden Jubilee" was a native of Madagascar, who asked leave to sing and delighted the queen by singing "Rock of Ages" — "it was a favourite of hers — and of Prince Albert, who whispered it when he was dying." When the steamer "London" sank in the Bay of Biscay in 1866, the last sounds heard from the helpless passengers were their voices singing "Rock of Ages" as the ship went down. A company of Armenian Christians sang it in their native tongue while being massacred in Constantinople. Gen. J.E.B. Stuart, the Confederate cavalry leader, called for it to be sung to him as he lay mortally wounded at Yellow Tavern.

Of the tune, Butterworth-Brown observe that [[Thomas_Hastings]]'s "Toplady" (named for the poet) made hymn and music inevitably one: "Almost anywhere to call for the tune of 'Toplady' is as unintelligible to the multitude as 'Key' would be to designate the 'Star-spangled Banner.' The common people — thanks to Dr. Hastings — have learned 'Rock of Ages' by *sound*."

They also note with some literary edge that the line "When I soar through tracts unknown" (modernized to "When I soar to worlds unknown") represents modern taste "getting rid of the unpoetic word, and bettering the elocution, but missing the writer's thought (of the unknown *path*)." The Unitarians have their own version with "atonement lines" substituted — but, they note, "the Christian lyric maintains its life and inspiration through the vicissitudes of age and use."

One of the most extraordinary anecdotes involves William Gladstone: the great statesman composed a Latin translation of "Rock of Ages" while sitting in the House of Commons — "masterly in his scholarly recreations as in his statesmanship."

## Hymnological Context (Duffield)

Duffield (1886) devotes his most substantial single entry to Toplady's "Rock of Ages," confirming it was first published in the **Gospel Magazine for March 1776**, titled "A Living and Dying Prayer for the Holiest Believer in the World." He gives the composition date as approximately 1770 and quotes Spurgeon's vivid response: "A glimpse at the thorn-crowned head and pierced hands and feet is a sure cure for 'modern doubt,' and all its vagaries."

Duffield documents the Wesley-Toplady controversy with striking quotations from both sides. Toplady, he notes, called Wesley "Pope John" and declared that his forehead was "petrified" and "impervious to a blush," while accusing him of writing "a known, wilful, palpable lie to the public." Yet Duffield juxtaposes this bitterness with the theological beauty of both men's hymns, standing "abashed before the imperfections" of such controversies.

Duffield mentions the famous deathbed account of the American preacher Summerfield — who, while dying, turned to request "Rock of Ages" — and cites Spurgeon at length on the hymn's power to combat theological doubt. He confirms the tradition linking the hymn's composition to the Toplady-Wesley dispute over Arminianism, while noting that the hymn itself wholly transcended the argument.

According to Duffield, Gladstone composed a Latin translation of "Rock of Ages" while seated in the House of Commons — "masterly in his scholarly recreations as in his statesmanship."

### From Lorenz (1938)

Lorenz treats Toplady briefly in Chapter XVI among the Calvinistic-Methodist hymn writers. He notes that Toplady (1740--1778) was associated with the Wesleys and the Calvinistic-Methodist leaders but was a Church of England clergyman who wrote 419 hymns, only a few of which continue in use. Of "Rock of Ages," Lorenz says it "has been almost universally used and most mercilessly amended and revised" and has been translated into many languages --- Gladstone having rendered it into Latin, Greek, and Italian. He quotes Montgomery's assessment of Toplady's hymns as having "a peculiarly ethereal spirit" in which "whether mourning or rejoicing, praying or praising, the writer seems absorbed in the full triumph of faith." Lorenz also notes Toplady's important role as a hymnbook compiler: his 1776 *Psalms and Hymns* (containing 418 hymns, including revised Wesley hymns used without credit) anticipated the later literary hymn ideal of Heber, Keble, and Milman, and continued in use for nearly fifty years.
