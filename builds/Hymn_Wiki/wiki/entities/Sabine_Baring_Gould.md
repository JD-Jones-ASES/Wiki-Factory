# Sabine Baring-Gould

On Whitsunday, 1865, Rev. Sabine Baring-Gould sat up a greater part of the night composing a hymn. He did not realize he was writing words that would be sung through the centuries. The hymn he wrote was "Onward, Christian soldiers."

## One Night's Work

At the time, Baring-Gould was minister of the Established Church at Lew-Trenchard, England. On Whitmonday, the children of his village were to march to an adjoining village for a Sunday school rally. *"If only there was something they could sing as they marched,"* the pastor thought, *"the way would not seem so long."*

He searched diligently for something suitable but found nothing. Finally he decided to write a marching song himself. It took the greater part of the night, but the next morning the children's pilgrimage was made lighter and happier by "Onward, Christian soldiers."

Thirty years later, commenting on the hymn, Baring-Gould said: *"It was written in great haste, and I am afraid some of the rhymes are faulty. Certainly, nothing has surprised me more than its popularity."*

The tune to which it is inseparably wedded --- composed by Sir Arthur Seymour Sullivan, the great English organist, in 1872 --- contributed enormously to its popularity. Words and music, each remarkable on its own, together became one of the most recognizable combinations in all of Christian worship.

## The Hymn's Meaning

Objections have sometimes been voiced against the hymn's martial spirit. But as Baring-Gould and Ryden both insist, the hymn gives not the slightest hint of warfare with carnal weapons. It is spiritual warfare from beginning to end. The warrior is the Christian soldier of Ephesians 6 --- wrestling not against flesh and blood, but against *"the principalities, against the powers, against the world-rulers of this darkness."*

The hymn also carries a note of confidence in the Church's ultimate victory. Christ's *"own promise"* has been given: though kingdoms rise and fall, His kingdom shall stand, for the gates of hell shall not prevail against it.

> Onward, Christian soldiers!
>   Marching as to war,
> With the cross of Jesus
>   Going on before.

## A Man of Extraordinary Versatility

Baring-Gould was one of England's most versatile ministers. In addition to hymn-writing, he was a novelist of considerable reputation and for many years regularly produced a novel every year. His *Lives of the Saints* in fifteen volumes, *Curious Myths of the Middle Ages*, and *Legends of the Old Testament* are all notable works. He did all his writing in longhand, without a secretary, and declared that he often did his best work when he felt least inclined to write.

He also wrote the beautiful evening hymn "Now the day is over" and composed the tune for it himself. He was additionally a translator of Bernhardt Severin Ingemann's Danish hymn "Through the night of doubt and sorrow," thus connecting [[English_Hymnody]] to [[Scandinavian_Hymnody]].

Despite his unceasing labors, Baring-Gould lived to the ripe age of ninety. He died in 1924, but his hymn goes marching on.

## Legacy

Baring-Gould's place in hymnody rests on one hymn, written in haste, in one night, for a children's walk between villages. That is often how the most enduring things are made.

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

Butterworth-Brown supply precise details about both the author and the composer of "Onward, Christian Soldiers." Baring-Gould was born at Exeter on January 28, 1834, educated at Clare College Cambridge, and was Rector of East Mersea, Essex from 1871 — the source text in Ryden describing him as minister at Lew-Trenchard. He "introduced into England from Flanders numbers of carols with charming old Christmas music" as well as original processionals.

For the composer, they provide the definitive biographical note: Sir Arthur Seymour Sullivan was born in London on May 13, 1842, gained the Mendelssohn Scholarship at the Royal Academy of Music and at the Conservatory of Leipzig, and "was a fertile genius" whose compositions included operettas, symphonies, overtures, anthems, hymn-tunes, and the oratorio *The Prodigal Son*. "Queen Victoria knighted him in 1883." Sullivan died in London on November 22, 1900.

Their summary of the combined effect: "The grand rhythm of 'Onward, Christian Soldiers' — hymn and tune — is irresistible whether in band march or congregational worship."
