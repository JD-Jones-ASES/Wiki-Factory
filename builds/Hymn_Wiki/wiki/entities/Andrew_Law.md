# Andrew Law

Andrew Law was one of the most energetic music educators of post-Revolutionary America --- a man who traveled tirelessly, compiled tune books for forty years, and invented an entirely new system of musical notation, even if the world declined to adopt it.

## Life and Formation

Law was born in Milford, Connecticut, in March 1748, the oldest son of Jahleel Law and Ann Baldwin, and the grandson of a governor of Connecticut. When he was five, the family moved to Cheshire, and Cheshire remained his anchor for the rest of his life. He graduated from Brown University in 1775, received a master's degree in 1778, was ordained a Congregational minister on September 8, 1787, and received honorary degrees from Yale (1786) and Allegheny College (LL.D., 1821). He never married.

For four decades Law devoted himself to the teaching and publishing of sacred music, traveling widely and producing multiple editions of several tune books. The *Connecticut Courant* of Hartford, July 17, 1821, announced his death: "Died, at the house of William Law, Esqr., of Cheshire, on the 13th, inst., the Rev. Andrew Law, in the 73d. year of his age. For the last forty years Mr. Law has been an assiduous cultivator and teacher of sacred music." (Metcalf, p. 70)

## Books and the Shape-Note System

Law's first publication was a collection of *Plain Tunes*, issued at Boston in 1767, with later editions in 1772, 1781, and 1785. The tune "Mear," included in his early collection, is still found in hymnals. His *Select Harmony* of 1779 contained one hundred pages of engraved music.

Most distinctive was his *Musical Magazine*, begun around 1791, in which he promoted a novel system of notation using four different shapes of notes in place of the standard round forms. The idea anticipated the later shape-note tradition that flourished in rural American churches, though Law's particular system was not widely adopted. He was fierce about its merits and not shy about criticizing rivals --- his magazine contains some pointed remarks about Andrew Adgate's Philadelphia singing school.

None of Law's own compositions survived into common use in later hymnals. But his lifelong effort to spread musical literacy, from New England to the South, was part of the broad movement that prepared the ground for Lowell Mason and the next generation of American church musicians.
