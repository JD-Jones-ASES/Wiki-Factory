# Robert Grant

Sir Robert Grant was born in the county of Inverness, Scotland, in 1785, and educated at Cambridge. He served many years as member of Parliament for Inverness and as a director of the East India Company, and in 1834 was appointed Governor of Bombay. He died at Dapoorie, Western India, on July 9, 1838, scarcely past his prime.

Sir Robert was a man of deep Christian feeling and a poetic mind. His writings were not numerous, but their thoughtful beauty endeared him to a wide circle of readers. In 1839 his brother, Lord Glenelg, published twelve of his poetical pieces, with a new edition appearing in 1868.

## O Worship the King

Grant's hymn "O Worship the King all-glorious above" has become almost universally known, and is often used as a morning or opening service song by choirs and congregations of all creeds. Butterworth-Brown describe it as "a model hymn of worship," strongly Hebrew in its tone and diction, drawing its inspiration from the Old Testament Psalter. Its favorite stanzas include the famous opening:

> O worship the King all-glorious above,
> And gratefully sing His wonderful love —
> Our Shield and Defender, the Ancient of Days,
> Pavilioned in splendor, and girded with praise.

> Frail children of dust, and feeble as frail,
> In Thee do we trust, nor find Thee to fail.
> Thy mercies how tender! how firm to the end!
> Our Maker, Defender, Redeemer, and Friend!

His other hymns include "The starry firmament on high," "Saviour, when in dust to Thee," and "When gathering clouds around I view."

Grant's early death, possibly from illness and affliction, lends a poignant weight to the submissive lines beginning "Thy mercy heard my infant prayer" — which speak of a heart still inflamed with faith even in "age and grief," with "a trembling hand and drooping lyre" that has "yet a strain for Thee."

## Musical and Thematic Context

The tune most often sung to Grant's hymn is Haydn's "Lyons." Butterworth-Brown note its vigor and spirit best fit it for Grant's noble lyric. The hymn belongs to a tradition of Old Testament praise-poetry that Butterworth-Brown connect to both Grant and [[Thomas_Olivers]]'s "The God of Abraham Praise" — both are "strongly Hebrew in tone and diction" and both drew directly from the Psalter as their supreme text-book of praise.
