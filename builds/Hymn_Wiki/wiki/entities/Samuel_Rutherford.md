# Samuel Rutherford

Samuel Rutherford was a Scottish Presbyterian minister and theologian, born around 1600. He served as rector at Anworth parish — the "little birds of Anworth" of [[Anne_Ross_Cousin]]'s hymn — and later became rector of the college at St. Andrews. His letters, *Rutherford's Letters*, became one of the most beloved works of Scottish devotional literature: intimate, fiery, and saturated with a longing for heaven.

## The Covenanter's End

Butterworth-Brown recount the closing chapter of Rutherford's life with characteristic economy. His outspoken defense of Presbyterianism against Charles II's imposition of episcopacy brought him into legal peril. The king's decree indicted him for treason, stripped him of all his offices, and would have forced him to the scaffold — had he not been stricken with his last sickness first. When the officers came to take him he said, simply and sublimely: "I am summoned before a higher Judge and Judicatory, and I am behooved to attend them." He died soon after, in 1661.

One of his joyful exclamations as he lay dying was the phrase that became the refrain of Cousin's hymn: "Glory shineth in Immanuel's Land!"

## His Influence on Hymnody

Rutherford wrote no hymns himself. But [[Anne_Ross_Cousin]], more than two centuries after his death, distilled his spirit and his very words into one of the most fervent hymns in the Scottish tradition. The hymn — "The Sands of Time Are Sinking" — is Rutherford's hymn in the truest sense: it is his voice, mediated through Cousin's gift for verse, and his dying words provide its unforgettable refrain.

The tune "Rutherford" — composed by Chrétien Urhan (D'Urhan), a French violinist — was named for him by those who fitted it to Cousin's words long after both Rutherford and the composer had died.
