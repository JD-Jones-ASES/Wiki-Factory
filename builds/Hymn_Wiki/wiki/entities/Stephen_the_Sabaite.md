# Stephen the Sabaite

Stephen the Sabaite is one of those figures who is known almost entirely through a single hymn --- but what a hymn it is.

## Raised in the Desert Monastery

Stephen was the ten-year-old nephew whom [[John_of_Damascus]] brought with him when he left court life to enter the monastery of St. Sabas. Stephen grew up within those ancient walls, in the harsh beauty of the Judean wilderness --- the deep gorge of the brook Kedron near Bethlehem, looking out over the Dead Sea. It was the kind of place that shaped a person's soul profoundly.

He became, Ryden says, "a gifted hymnist" in his own right, following in the tradition of his uncle. He died in 794 AD.

## The Hymn

Stephen's most famous hymn was translated into English by [[John_Mason_Neale]] and is known by its opening question: "Art thou weary, art thou languid?" It is one of the most personally searching hymns in the Christian tradition --- a dialogue between the weary soul and the Christ who calls it home. Its gentle, questioning tone stands in beautiful contrast to the more triumphant Easter hymns of his uncle John.

The hymn has been loved especially by people in seasons of spiritual exhaustion or doubt. It speaks to anyone who has found the journey of faith harder than they expected, and offers the simplest possible comfort: the one who calls you home bore the cost of calling you Himself.

## Significance

Stephen the Sabaite is a reminder that great hymns don't always come from great platform figures. He was a monk who lived and died in a desert monastery, relatively unknown outside his community. But a single poem of rare tenderness and depth has kept his memory alive for twelve centuries. Sometimes the most lasting contributions to Christian worship come from the quiet places.
