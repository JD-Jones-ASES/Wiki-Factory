# Horatio Gates Spafford

Horatio Gates Spafford is known for one thing above all: writing a hymn of perfect submission in the year he lost everything. The story behind "It Is Well with My Soul" is one of the most harrowing in all of hymnody.

## The Losses

Spafford was born in New York state on October 30, 1828, and was a lawyer. While connected with a Chicago institution as professor of medical jurisprudence, he lost a great part of his fortune in the great Chicago fire. This disaster was followed on November 22, 1873, by the loss of his children on the steamer *Ville de Havre* on the Atlantic.

At the end of that year of catastrophe, he wrote the hymn of submissive faith that begins with the image of peace like a river attending his way and sorrows like sea-billows rolling --- and whatever his lot, he testifies "It is well, it is well with my soul."

## The Hymn's Impact

A friend of Spafford who knew his history read the hymn while nursing a much smaller affliction. "If he can feel like that after suffering what he has suffered," the friend said, "I will cease my complaints." This response captures the hymn's peculiar power: it does not minimize grief; it confesses it and then transcends it.

The tune by [[Philip_Paul_Bliss]] --- a gentle, gliding melody --- suits the mood of the words. Bliss wrote it as a tender companion to the text, not a march or a shout.

## Musical Context (Metcalf)

Metcalf's *American Writers and Compilers of Sacred Music* (1925) devotes a chapter to Spafford, drawn from the *Choir Herald* and contemporary accounts.

Metcalf gives his birth date as October 20, 1829, in Lawrenceburg, near Troy, New York. Spafford was a lawyer in Chicago and an elder at the Fullerton Avenue Presbyterian Church. The financial crisis of 1873 had taken most of his property, and his wife and four daughters — ranging in age from eighteen months to twelve years — had been sent on a trip to France. The company of the *Ville-du-Havre* numbered over three hundred; religious services were held daily and a Sunday school organized for the children on board.

On the night of November 22, 1873, the Loch Earn collided with the French packet and the ship sank in minutes, carrying down 226 souls. Of the 87 survivors, only 28 were passengers. The four Spafford daughters were lost; Mrs. Spafford survived. She telegraphed from France: "Saved alone. Children lost. What shall I do?" Spafford left immediately to join his wife, and when the ship passed the location of the wreck, he wrote that he could not think of his daughters as buried there: "Involuntarily I lifted my eyes to heaven. Yes, I am sure they are there — on high — and happier far than if they were still with me."

It was during his return to Chicago that the hymn was written. Metcalf notes that Spafford had previously cried to God in the noon prayer meeting for a baptism of the Holy Spirit on his workers. Mrs. Spafford wrote that the experience of the shipwreck intensified his spiritual longing and drew others with like aspirations. This was the nucleus of the colony that in 1881 left Chicago and settled in Jerusalem, arriving September 26, 1881. Spafford died seven years to the day from that arrival date. (Metcalf, p. 304-307)

## The Later Years

Butterworth-Brown note that about seven or eight years after writing the hymn, Spafford "went to Jerusalem under the hallucination that he was a second Messiah" and died there on September 5, 1888 --- on the seventh anniversary of his landing in Palestine. Butterworth-Brown offer no condemnation, only compassion: "The aberrations of an over-wrought mind are beckonings to God's compassion. When reason wanders He takes the soul of His helpless child into his own keeping --- and 'it is well.'"
