# Benjamin Beddome

Benjamin Beddome was born at Henley, Warwickshire, on January 23, 1717. He was settled over the Baptist congregation at Bourton-on-the-Water, Gloucestershire, in 1743, and remained with the same church until his death on September 3, 1795 — a ministry of fifty-two years. His hymns were not collected and published until 1818, long after he was gone.

## Did Christ O'er Sinners Weep?

This tender hymn-poem was written while Beddome served his lifelong Gloucestershire congregation. Butterworth-Brown note the tune most associated with it is "Dennis" — adapted by [[Lowell_Mason]] from [[Johann_Georg_Nageli]]'s melody — described as "a soft and smoothly modulated harmony" with "no note out of sympathy with their deep feeling":

> Did Christ o'er sinners weep,
>   And shall our cheeks be dry?
> Let floods of penitential grief
>   Burst forth from every eye.

> He wept that we might weep;
>   Each sin demands a tear:
> In heaven alone no sin is found,
>   And there's no weeping there.

## Let Party Names No More

This hymn sounds, as Butterworth-Brown observe, "like a prelude to the grand rally of the Christian Churches a generation later for united advance into foreign fields." It was an after-sermon hymn — like so many of Watts and Doddridge — speaking a good man's longing to see all sects stand shoulder to shoulder:

> Let party names no more
>   The Christian world o'erspread;
> Gentile and Jew, and bond and free,
>   Are one in Christ the Head.

The tune assigned to it is "Boylston."

## Musical and Thematic Context

Beddome exemplifies the tradition of the settled pastor-hymnist: a man who wrote hymns not for publication but for his own congregation, as a natural extension of preaching. The fact that his collected hymns were not published until after his death is consistent with his evident lack of literary ambition. He sang because he had something to say — and the church, eventually, kept what he said.

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Burrage supplies a wealth of biographical detail that earlier accounts omit. Beddome was born not simply "at Henley" but at Henley-in-Arden, Warwickshire, and his father was Rev. John Beddome, minister at the Pithay Baptist Church in Bristol. Benjamin's conversion came at his father's church on August 7, 1737 — from the text Luke 15:7 — and he was ordained September 23, 1743 at Bourton, with Dr. Joseph Stennett preaching. His marriage followed on December 27, 1749, to Elizabeth Boswell, the daughter of a deacon.

One of the most striking details Burrage preserves is a prophetic coincidence: around 1742 — before he ever lived at Bourton — Beddome wrote a few lines that turned out to be his own biography: the Lord had already, in a sense, told him where he would spend his life.

The London call Beddome received after the death of Samuel Wilson gives an interesting parallel to [[John_Fawcett]]'s famous refusal. In Beddome's case, it was not his own heart but his congregation at Bourton that refused to release him — and so he stayed.

The most remarkable story Burrage records concerns Beddome's son. On January 4, 1778, Beddome preached from Psalm 31:15 — "My times are in thy hand" — and was so moved by the text that he wrote a hymn on it that same day. He did not yet know that his son had died in Edinburgh on that very day. The hymn he wrote was born in the same hour his son died, from a text about time and divine sovereignty — though he did not learn of the death until afterward.

Burrage notes that Beddome died on September 3, 1795, "having been engaged in writing a hymn only a few hours before his departure" — a fitting end for a man who made hymn-writing as natural as breathing.

The 1818 posthumous collection contained 822 hymns and 8 doxologies, with a recommendatory preface by [[Robert_Hall]]. More than fifty of the hymns had already appeared in [[John_Rippon]]'s Selection — Rippon had quietly been drawing on Beddome's congregation-sung verse for decades. James Montgomery's assessment, quoted by Burrage, captures what makes these hymns endure: "very agreeable as well as impressive, being for the most part brief and pithy... a single idea, always important, often striking... with the terseness and simplicity of the Greek epigram." An honorary A.M. was conferred on Beddome by Rhode Island College (now Brown University) in 1770.

Among his best-known hymns: *Did Christ o'er sinners weep*, *And must I part with all I have*, *Let party names no more*, *Come, Holy Spirit, come*, *Jesus my Lord my chief delight*, *If Christ is mine then all is mine*, *Prayer is the breath of God in man*, *God in the Gospel of his Son*, *Blest Comforter divine*, and *Buried beneath the yielding wave*.
