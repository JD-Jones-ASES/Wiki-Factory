# Philipp Nicolai

## The King and Queen of Chorales

Philipp Nicolai holds a unique distinction in the history of Christian hymnody: he wrote the words *and* composed the music for what are called the King and the Queen of Chorales. These two masterpieces --- "Wachet auf, ruft uns die Stimme" ("Wake, Awake, for Night Is Flying") and "Wie schön leuchtet der Morgenstern" ("How Brightly Shines the Morning Star") --- were born out of one of the most harrowing experiences a pastor can face: watching his entire congregation die around him.

## Biographical Sketch

Nicolai was born at Mengerinhausen on August 10, 1556, the son of a Lutheran pastor. He studied at the Universities of Erfurt and Wittenberg and was ordained in 1583. His first charge was at Herdecke, but the town council was Catholic and he was soon forced to leave. He served next at Niederwildungen and Altwildungen before becoming pastor at Unna in 1596.

Throughout his career, Nicolai stood as a fierce defender of Lutheran doctrine, particularly against the Calvinists on the question of the Lord's Supper. He was regarded as the "pillar" of the Lutheran Church in the controversy over Christ's presence in the Eucharist. In 1601 he was chosen chief pastor of St. Katherine's Church in Hamburg, where he gained fame as a preacher and was hailed as "a second Chrysostom." He died October 26, 1608.

## Born Out of a Plague

The circumstances that produced Nicolai's two great hymns are almost impossibly dramatic.

Between July 1597 and January 1598, a dreadful plague swept through Westphalia. In the small town of Unna, where Nicolai was pastor, 1,300 people died --- 170 of them in a single week in August. From the parsonage window overlooking the churchyard, Nicolai watched the burials day after day. On one day alone, thirty graves were dug.

To sustain himself and his people, Nicolai began writing a series of meditations, which he published under the title *Freuden Spiegel* ("Mirror of Joy"). His purpose, as he wrote in the preface dated August 10, 1598, was to leave it behind as "the token of my peaceful, joyful, Christian departure" should he die --- or, if he survived, "to comfort other sufferers whom He should also visit with the pestilence."

He wrote: "There seemed to me nothing more sweet, delightful and agreeable, than the contemplation of the noble, sublime doctrine of Eternal Life obtained through the Blood of Christ. This I allowed to dwell in my heart day and night, and searched the Scriptures... Then day by day I wrote out my meditations, found myself, thank God! wonderfully well, comforted in heart, joyful in spirit, and truly content."

Both great hymns appeared for the first time in the *Mirror of Joy*.

## "Wake, Awake" --- The King of Chorales

"Wachet auf" is based on the parable of the ten virgins (Matthew 25). Nicolai titled it "Of the voice at Midnight, and the Wise Virgins who meet their Heavenly Bridegroom." It is a hymn of eschatological longing and bridal joy --- the Church awaiting its Lord:

> *Wake, awake, for night is flying;*
> *The watchmen on the heights are crying,*
> *Awake, Jerusalem, arise!*

The chorale melody Nicolai composed for it is recognized as one of the greatest in the Christian tradition. Handel borrowed its seventh and eighth lines for the famous passage "The kingdoms of this world" in the Hallelujah Chorus. Mendelssohn introduced the air in his overture to "St. Paul" and used the complete chorale in his "Hymn of Praise." Johann Sebastian Bach built one of his most celebrated cantatas (BWV 140) around it.

## "How Brightly Shines the Morning Star" --- The Queen of Chorales

"Wie schön leuchtet der Morgenstern" Nicolai subtitled "A spiritual bridal song of the believing soul concerning Jesus Christ, her Heavenly Bridegroom, founded on the 45th Psalm." It is a hymn of adoring love for Christ, radiant with joy and wonder. Its melody became so popular that numerous church chimes were set to it. Mendelssohn also used this theme in the first chorus of his unpublished oratorio "Christus."

The Queen of Chorales has lent its melody to several other hymns, including "All Hail to Thee, O Blessed Morn" and "O Holy Spirit, Enter In."

## Significance

Nicolai stands at the head of a very short list of figures in Christian hymnody who excelled equally as poet and composer. His two great hymns were written not in ease and security but in the shadow of mass death --- and they breathe not despair but soaring, luminous hope. The very experience of watching his parishioners die drove Nicolai to contemplate eternal life with extraordinary intensity, and the result was music that has outlasted every plague.

Ryden concludes simply: "It has been called the 'King of Chorales,' and well does it deserve the title."
