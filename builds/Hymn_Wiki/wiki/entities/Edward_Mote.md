# Edward Mote

The Rev. Edward Mote was born in London in 1797. According to his own testimony, his parents were not God-fearing people, and he "went to a school where no Bible was allowed." At the age of sixteen he received religious impressions from a sermon of John Hyatt in Tottenham Court Chapel; he was converted two years later, studied for the ministry, and ultimately became a faithful preacher of the gospel. Settled as pastor of the Baptist Church in Horsham, Sussex, he remained there twenty-six years — until his death on November 13, 1874.

## My Hope Is Built on Nothing Less

The refrain came to Mote one Sabbath morning while he was on his way to Holborn to exchange pulpits:

> On Christ the solid Rock I stand,
> All other ground is sinking sand.

The refrain is, as Butterworth-Brown note, "a fine one, and really sums up the whole hymn, keeping constantly at the front the corner-stone of the poet's trust." The original six stanzas began not with the familiar opening but with:

> Nor earth, nor hell, my soul can move,
> I rest upon unchanging love.

The hymn as now sung usually begins with:

> My hope is built on nothing less
> Than Jesus' blood and righteousness.
> I dare not trust the sweetest frame,
> But only lean on Jesus' name.

## Musical and Thematic Context

[[William_Bradbury]] composed the tune in 1863. It is usually named "The Solid Rock." The tune's four-square solidity — its steady bass, its refrain that repeats the central declaration — perfectly embodies the theological point of the hymn: the permanence of Christ as foundation against the instability of everything else.

Mote is one of those figures who published modestly but gave the church something it could not lose: a refrain that has passed into the common speech of devotion as thoroughly as any passage of scripture.

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Burrage adds detail that gives the hymn's origin story a more precise texture. Mote was born in Upper Thames Street, London, on January 21, 1797. His parents kept a public house, and he describes his own early life in stark terms: "My Sabbaths were spent in the streets at play. So ignorant was I that I did not know there was a God." He was apprenticed to a cabinetmaker, and it was during that apprenticeship — in 1813, not at sixteen as commonly stated — that he heard John Hyatt preach, at the Tottenham Court Road Chapel and the Tabernacle at Moorfields.

After his hearing of Hyatt, Mote first joined the church of Alexander Fletcher (author of Family Devotions), but not finding satisfaction there he moved to the congregation of Rev. John Bayley, by whom he was baptized on November 1, 1815. He continued his trade as a cabinetmaker in Southwark, writing for the press on the side, until 1852 — considerably later than earlier accounts suggest — when he became pastor of the Baptist church at Horsham, Sussex.

Burrage records one of the most characteristic moments of Mote's ministry. The congregation, out of gratitude for his role in securing their chapel building, offered to make the property his own. He refused: "I do not want the chapel, I only want the pulpit; and when I cease to preach Christ, then turn me out of that." He was never once prevented from preaching by illness or any other cause during his entire ministry.

Burrage also corrects the detail in the refrain's origin. Mote told Mr. Miller (author of "Singers and Songs of the Church") that the refrain came to him on a morning as he was walking up Holborn Hill — not "to exchange pulpits" as other accounts say, but on his way to work, around 1834. Four stanzas were quickly written, two more on the following Sunday. They were immediately put to use bringing comfort to a dying friend.

At his death Mote's own words express the theology he had spent his ministry on: "The truths I have preached I am now living upon; and they will do to die upon." The day before he died he was heard speaking of "the precious blood, precious blood, which takes away all our sins; it is this makes peace with God." He was buried in the little graveyard behind Rehoboth Chapel, Horsham, on November 13, 1874.
