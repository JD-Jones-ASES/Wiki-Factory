# Philip Doddridge

Philip Doddridge was born June 26, 1702, the last of twenty children. In a century crowded with remarkable hymn-writers, this fact alone might seem like a footnote --- but it tells you something essential about who he was: a man who came into the world at the end of a long line, frail and nearly despaired of from birth, and who spent his life proving that the last sometimes become the first.

## The Last of Twenty

His grandfather had been one of the Independent ministers ejected in 1662 for refusing to conform to the Established Church. His parents were pious people, and Philip grew up in a household where faith was not merely observed but lived. Both parents died while he was still young, and kind friends took in the orphan boy and sent him to school.

He proved so gifted that the Duchess of Bedford offered to send him to university --- on one condition: that he enter the Church of England. Philip declined. He entered a nonconformist seminary instead. It was the kind of choice that would define his entire life: loyalty to conscience over comfort.

## Preacher, Teacher, Whole Faculty

At twenty-one he was ordained as pastor of the Independent congregation at Kibworth. Six years later he began his real life work at Northampton, heading a theological training school while preaching to the local congregation. Students came from all parts of the British Isles and even from the Continent.

Doddridge was practically the whole faculty. His subjects ranged from Hebrew and Greek to Algebra, Trigonometry, Logic, and Philosophy. It was not unusual for great teachers to be also great scholars; what was unusual was the warmth that accompanied his learning. He was a friend and admirer of [[Isaac_Watts]], whose hymns had set all England singing, and something of Watts' fire passed into his own work --- though Ryden notes that Doddridge's hymns, while lacking the strength and majesty of Watts, possess "more personal warmth and tenderness."

## The Hymns

Doddridge wrote about four hundred hymns. Most were composed for his own congregation, to be sung in connection with his sermons. None was published during his lifetime, but manuscript copies circulated widely among Independent congregations throughout England. The fact that about one-third are still in common use on both sides of the Atlantic speaks to their unusual merit.

Notable among them:

- **"O happy day, that stays my choice"** --- the great confirmation hymn
- **"Hark, the glad sound, the Saviour comes"**
- **"Awake, my soul, stretch every nerve"**
- **"See Israel's gentle Shepherd stand"** --- a children's hymn of rare tenderness
- **"Great God, we sing that mighty hand"**
- **"Father of all, Thy care we bless"**

There is a spiritual joy in his most famous hymn that must be heard:

> O happy day, that stays my choice
>   On Thee, my Saviour and my God!
> Well may this glowing heart rejoice,
>   And tell its raptures all abroad.

Something of his own confiding trust in God is expressed in his quieter lines:

> Shine on our souls, eternal God!
>   With rays of beauty shine;
> O let Thy favor crown our days,
>   And all their round be Thine.

## The Final Journey

Throughout his life Doddridge struggled under the burden of feeble health. When he was only forty-eight, it became apparent that he had fallen victim to tuberculosis. Friends advised him to leave England for Lisbon, Portugal. Funds were lacking, and Christians from all parts of England came to his aid.

He undertook the journey, but got no farther than Lisbon before the disease overtook him. He died there on October 26, 1751.

The man who had declined a university education rather than compromise his convictions, who had taught algebra and Greek to a generation of ministers, who had never published a single hymn in his lifetime --- he is now best remembered by hymns sung in churches around the world, carried there in manuscript copies by the students he trained.

## Legacy

Doddridge stands as a quiet giant of English hymnody. His hymns do not thunder like Watts' or soar like Wesley's; they draw close, the way a friend draws close to whisper something true. At his best, Doddridge translates doctrine into tenderness.

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

Butterworth-Brown place "O Happy Day That Fixed My Choice" at the very heart of the revival meeting experience: Doddridge's hymn and Charles Wesley's "O How Happy Are They" were "voices as sure to be heard in converts' meetings as the leader's prayer or text." The Doddridge hymn was "sung inevitably to Rimbault's tune, 'Happy Day.'" Edward Francis Rimbault (born Soho, June 13, 1816; died September 26, 1876) was an organist at the Soho Swiss Church from age sixteen — a skilled though not prolific composer who declined an appointment as professor of music at Harvard College. His name has largely been forgotten, while his tune is sung wherever revival Christianity has taken root.

## Hymnological Context (Duffield)

Duffield (1886) covers 29 of Doddridge's hymns — the third-highest count after Watts (133) and Charles Wesley (35) — a measure of how central Doddridge was to the English dissenting hymn tradition. Duffield consistently refers to the three-volume edition of Doddridge's *Works* as his primary documentary source, noting that each hymn was assigned a number, a title, and a scripture text in the original manuscript compilation.

On **"O God of Bethel, by Whose Hand"** (1737), Duffield records the sermon origin: written to follow a sermon on "Jacob's Vow" (Gen. 28:20–22), preached January 16th, 1737. The hymn subsequently appeared in altered form among Logan's *Poems* (1781) and among the Scottish Paraphrases, altered by Michael Bruce (died 1767). Duffield notes that "a pathetic interest attaches to these verses from their association with the story of the heroic missionary, Dr. Livingstone" — who quoted it on his death march.

On **"Awake, My Soul, to Meet the Day"**, Duffield provides an intimate detail: Doddridge composed this as a "morning hymn" and habitually rose at five o'clock to sing its seven stanzas as his morning devotion — rising from his bed as he reached the sixth stanza. This glimpse of personal piety helps explain the warmth that distinguishes Doddridge from more formal hymn-writers.

On **"Why Will Ye Waste in Trifling Cares?"**, Duffield uses the hymn as a case study in how hymns improve through editorial hands, quoting the original opening stanza — "Why will ye lavish out your years / Amid the beggars' throng?" — and comparing it to the common version, arguing the alterations "decidedly improved" a "grand hymn by a little modification."

On **"My Father, God! How Sweet the Sound"**, Duffield offers his general assessment of Doddridge: though the hymns circulated only in manuscript during his lifetime and were sometimes neglected, "they are so thoroughly excellent in spirit, and oftentimes so admirable in phraseology, that they are indispensable to any collection of sacred verse." He notes that Doddridge's famous epigram "Live while you live" represents his characteristic blending of classical sentiment and evangelical faith.

Duffield also records the story of "My God, and Is Thy Table Spread": a woman who caught the lines "My God, and is thy table spread" during a service "was greatly moved by them and wept bitterly" — pointing to the peculiar power of Doddridge's eucharistic verse.
