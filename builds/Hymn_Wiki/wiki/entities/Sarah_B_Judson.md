# Sarah B. Judson

Sarah Boardman Judson was born at Alstead, New Hampshire, November 4, 1803, the eldest daughter of Ralph and Abiah Hall. Her parents moved successively to Danvers and Salem, Massachusetts, where in her seventeenth year she became a member of the First Baptist church, under the pastorate of Dr. Lucius Bolles. Dr. Bolles had organized in Salem as early as 1812 a society to aid William Carey in translating and publishing the Scriptures, and the young convert grew up with the spirit of missions already around her.

## First Marriage and Life in Burma

As the wife of George Dana Boardman, to whom she was married July 4, 1825, she embarked July 19, bound for Calcutta, arriving December 13. On account of the Burmese war they were obliged to remain until March 1827, then proceeded to Amherst, shortly after to Maulmain, and later to Tavoy. Three children were born to them, of whom only one, George Dana, survived infancy. Mr. Boardman died at Tavoy, February 11, 1831.

At his grave Sarah Boardman wrote: "I thought I must go home with George. But these poor, inquiring and Christian Karens, and the school boys, and the Burmese Christians, would then be left without any one to instruct them; and the poor, stupid Tavoyans would go on in the road to death, with no one to warn them of their danger. How then, oh, how can I go?" She stayed.

## Second Marriage and Death at Sea

On April 10, 1834, Mrs. Boardman was married to [[Adoniram_Judson]], whose heroic first wife Ann Judson had died at Amherst eight years before. For eleven years Judson and Sarah Boardman toiled together.

Her health failing, she embarked with her husband and elder children for London, April 26, 1845. During the voyage the vessel sprang a leak and put in to the Isle of France for repairs. As she appeared to be improving, it seemed possible she might continue the voyage home while Judson returned to Burma. It was under these circumstances that she wrote the moving lines:

*We part on this green islet, love, / Thou for the eastern main, / I for the setting sun, love, / O, when to meet again!*

*My heart is sad for thee, love, / For lone thy way will be; / And oft thy tears will fall, love, / For thy children and for me.*

But the parting was not to take place. A relapse followed, and on July 25 the family together embarked on the ship Sophia Walker for the United States. Mrs. Judson died on shipboard in the harbor of St. Helena, September 1, 1845, in the forty-second year of her age and the twenty-first of her missionary life. She was buried on the island.

Judson said: "I saw her safely deposited, and in the language of prayer, which we had often presented together at the throne of grace, I blessed God that her body had attained the repose of the grave, and her spirit the repose of Paradise."

## Her Hymns

A hymn of four stanzas beginning

*Proclaim the lofty praise / Of him who once was slain, / But now is risen, through endless days / To live and reign*

appeared without attribution in Urwick's Selection (Dublin, 1829) and was transferred to *The Psalmist* (1843) as "Urwick's Collection." Dr. Hatfield (*Poets of the Church*) ascribes it to Sarah B. Judson, though Burrage notes he is not certain of the grounds.

Her farewell verses from the Isle of France, written when she believed she and Judson were about to part permanently, are among the most moving poems in the Baptist missionary canon — a farewell that turned out not to be a farewell at all, because she died before reaching home.

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Burrage's account of Sarah B. Judson forms a natural complement to his account of her husband. Taken together — and Emily C. Judson following — the three Judson hymn writers represent the closest thing American Baptist hymnody has to a family tradition. Sarah's story, in particular, illustrates the theology that animated the entire Baptist missionary movement: the willingness to stay and suffer, not because duty demands it abstractly, but because love for specific people — "these poor Karens," "the school boys" — makes departure impossible.
