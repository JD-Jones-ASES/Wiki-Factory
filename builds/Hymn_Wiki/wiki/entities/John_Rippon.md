# John Rippon

If [[Benjamin_Keach]] opened the door to hymn-singing in English Baptist churches, John Rippon built the house. His *Selection of Hymns from the Best Authors* (1787) was the gathering point for everything that the Baptist hymn tradition had produced in the preceding century, and it shaped Baptist worship on both sides of the Atlantic for generations.

## Life and Ministry

Rippon was born at Tiverton, Devonshire, April 29, 1751. Converted at sixteen, he united with the Baptist church in Tiverton and in the following year entered the Baptist College at Bristol, where his instructors were Rev. Hugh Evans and his son Rev. Caleb Evans.

In 1772, on completing his studies, he was invited to preach in the pulpit of the Baptist church in Carter Lane, Tooley Street, London — the very church that [[Benjamin_Keach]] had once served, and which had later been the long pastorate of the learned Dr. John Gill. The result was a call to the pastorate, and Rippon was ordained November 11, 1773.

He served that church for sixty-three years, until his death on December 17, 1836. This is, by itself, one of the most remarkable facts in Baptist ministerial history. During that ministry the church was forced by the building of the new London Bridge to relocate, and erected a new house of worship in New Park Street — the same building to which [[Charles_Spurgeon]] would be called in 1854, stepping into a pulpit with a direct succession from Gill and Rippon.

## The Selection of Hymns

The first edition of Rippon's *Selection* appeared in 1787, entitled *A Selection of Hymns from the Best Authors, intended to be an appendix to Dr. Watts' Psalms and Hymns*. More than thirty editions were published in England, and many in America.

This was not simply a personal anthology. Rippon was a devoted admirer of Isaac Watts and positioned his *Selection* as a supplement to Watts' great body of work — bringing in the best of what had been written since Watts, particularly the treasury of Baptist hymns that had accumulated in the previous fifty years. [[Anna_Steele]], [[Samuel_Medley]], [[John_Fawcett]], [[Samuel_Stennett]], and many others were represented.

Rippon's other significant editorial project was an improved edition of Dr. Watts' own productions (1798), followed by *An Arrangement of the Psalms, Hymns and Spiritual Songs of the Rev. Isaac Watts* (1801). When American Baptist minister James M. Winchell later prepared his own arrangement of Watts — the standard for American Baptist churches before the *Psalmist* — he acknowledged his debt to Rippon's earlier work. Dr. Hatfield (*Poets of the Church*) called Rippon's edition of Watts "probably the most accurate edition of Dr. Watts' book ever published."

Rippon also published his *Baptist Annual Register* from 1790 to 1802, an important denominational record.

## His Own Hymns

Rippon contributed several hymns to his own *Selection*, but since they were undesignated, it is now difficult to identify them with certainty. The hymn

*Here, Lord, my soul convicted stands / Of breaking all thy ten commands*

is ascribed to him by John Gadsby (*Memoirs of Hymn Writers and Compilers*), who notes he cannot find it in any book earlier than the *Selection*. Another hymn,

*As when in silence vernal showers*

(which appears in the *Selection* beginning *As showers on meadows newly mown*), is also credited to him.

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Burrage's account of Rippon underscores the institutional importance of his work: the *Selection* was the canonical Baptist hymnbook of its era. Many of the hymn writers Burrage surveys appear in Rippon's collection, and he regularly notes which hymns were "in Rippon's Selection" as the ultimate mark of currency and acceptance.

Rippon's church at New Park Street is also notable as the place where [[Charles_Spurgeon]] began his London ministry in 1854, preaching first as a supply and then as permanent pastor. In this way the institutional line from Keach through Rippon to Spurgeon traces the arc of English Baptist congregational life over two centuries.
