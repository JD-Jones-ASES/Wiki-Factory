# Ellen M.H. Gates

Mrs. Ellen M. Huntington Gates was born in Torrington, Connecticut. She was the youngest sister of the late Collis P. Huntington, the railroad magnate. Her hymns and poems are described by Butterworth-Brown as "much admired and loved, both for their sweetness and elevated religious feeling, and for their poetic quality." Her published books of verse include "Night," "At Noontide," and "Treasures of Kurium." She lived in New York City.

## Your Mission

The story of "Your Mission" — published under the title "If You Cannot on the Ocean" — is one of the most remarkable in American hymn history.

In the winter of 1861–62, on a stormy day, Miss Gates was looking through the window at the falling snow. Words came to her:

> If you cannot on the ocean
> Sail among the swiftest fleet.

She turned away and wrote the lines on her slate, verse after verse, until the poem was complete. "It wrote itself," she said. Reading it afterward, she was surprised at what she had produced, and fell on her knees to consecrate it to a divine purpose. It appeared in the Cooperstown, New York local paper and in the New York *Examiner*.

## The Washington Performance

In February 1865, Philip Phillips — a gospel singer with "a wonderfully sweet tenor voice" — was invited to sing at a great meeting of the United States Christian Commission in the Senate Chamber at Washington. President Lincoln and Secretary Seward were present, and the hall was crowded with leading statesmen and army generals. Phillips chose "Your Mission."

The hushed audience listened spellbound as the verses unfolded, interest growing to what Butterworth-Brown call "feverish eagerness." When the fifth stanza was reached:

> If you cannot in the conflict
>   Prove yourself a soldier true,
> If where fire and smoke are thickest
>   There's no work for you to do,
> When the battlefield is silent
>   You can go with careful tread;
> You can bear away the wounded,
>   You can cover up the dead.

President Lincoln passed a note to Chairman Seward: "Near the close let us have 'Your Mission' repeated."

## The Chain of Influence

Butterworth-Brown trace a remarkable chain of consequence from this moment. Phillips's success that evening brought so many calls for his services that he devoted himself entirely to gospel singing, becoming "the first gospel songster, chanting round the world the divine message of the hymns." It was this performance that first impressed Ira D. Sankey with the power of evangelical solo song — and helped him years later to leave his business as a revenue officer to devote his rare vocal gift to sacred music.

"Heaven alone," Butterworth-Brown write, "can show the birth-records of souls won to God all along the journeys of the 'Singing Pilgrims,' and the rich succession of Mr. Sankey's melodies, that can be traced back by a chain of causes to the poem that 'wrote itself' and became a hymn."
