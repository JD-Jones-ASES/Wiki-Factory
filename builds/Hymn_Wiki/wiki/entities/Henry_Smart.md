# Henry Smart

Among the five great English composers who followed America's Lowell Mason, Henry Smart stands out for both his range and his purity of style. He was born in London on October 25, 1812, and chose music over an offered commission in the East Indian Army --- a choice the Church has never ceased to be grateful for.

## The Gift Undimmed by Blindness

Smart's talent as a composer, especially of sacred music, was described by Butterworth-Brown as "marvellous." What makes his story extraordinary is that he became blind during the course of his career --- yet his loss of sight proved no more a hindrance to his genius than loss of hearing was to Beethoven. He kept composing by dictating to assistants, and the work he produced in his sightless years is among his best.

No composer of his time equalled Smart as a writer of music for female voices, and his cantatas were greatly admired.

## The Tunes

Smart's hymn tunes are "unsurpassed for their purity and sweetness." Among the best known:

- **Setting of the "Te Deum"** in his *Morning and Evening Service* --- the antiphonal choral rendering, indicating choir parts as Decani and Cantor
- **"Colebrook"** --- the English music for Samuel Medley's "O could I speak the matchless worth"
- **A choral in Barnby's** ***Hymnary*** (No. 170) --- set to "When I survey the wondrous cross"

Beyond hymn tunes, Smart composed the oratorio "Jacob" and a wide range of anthems and other sacred pieces. He died on July 10, 1879, "universally mourned for his inspired work, and his amiable character."

## Musical and Thematic Context

Butterworth-Brown praise Smart's Te Deum setting for its faithful use of the antiphonal structure inherited from the ancient chant, with choir parts designated by the Latin terms Decani (the dean's side) and Cantor (the precentor's side). His hymn tunes have a characteristic nobility that Butterworth-Brown call "unsurpassed" in purity and sweetness among English composers of his era.
