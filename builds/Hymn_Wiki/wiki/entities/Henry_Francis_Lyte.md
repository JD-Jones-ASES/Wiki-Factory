# Henry Francis Lyte

When Henry Francis Lyte died in 1847, he was little known beyond his humble seashore parish at Lower Brixham, England. Today, wherever his hymns are sung throughout the Christian world, he is gratefully remembered as the man who wrote "Abide with me."

## Born in Scotland, Formed by Loss

Lyte was always frail in health. He was born in Scotland on June 1, 1793, and was early left an orphan. Despite the handicap of poverty, he struggled through college and won prizes for his poems on three occasions. His first ambition was to become a physician, but during his college days he turned toward the ministry.

The change came through a death. A young brother clergyman was dying, and Lyte was called to his bedside to offer consolation. He discovered, to his sorrow, that both he and the dying man were "blind guides, groping for light." Through a prayerful search of the Scriptures together, they both came to a firm faith in Christ. Of his friend Lyte wrote:

*"He died happy under the belief that though he had deeply erred, there was One whose death and sufferings would atone for his delinquencies, and that he was forgiven and accepted for His sake."*

And of his own transformation: *"I began to study my Bible and preach in another manner than I had previously done."*

## The Parish at Lower Brixham

For nearly twenty-five years after this turning point, Lyte labored among the humble fisherfolk and sailors of the parish at Lower Brixham. His deep spiritual zeal led him to overtax his frail physical powers, and from time to time he was obliged to spend winters in more gentle climates. Throughout these years, he gave the Church a treasury of hymns that stand among the finest in English.

In response to a questionnaire sent to American readers by *The Etude* magazine, 7,500 out of nearly 32,000 persons named "Abide with me" as their favorite hymn --- easily displacing older favorites such as "Rock of Ages" and "Jesus, Lover of my soul."

This hymn, it is worth knowing, is not in reality an evening hymn. Its theme is the evening of life, and it was written when Lyte himself felt the shadows of death gathering. *"Swift to its close ebbs out life's little day"* --- that is not the setting of the sun over the sea, but the ending of a life.

## The Farewell

In the autumn of 1847, Lyte wrote to a friend that the swallows were flying southward and he felt they were inviting him to accompany them. His family and friends urged him not to preach that last Sunday, but the conscientious minister would not be dissuaded: "It is better to *wear* out than to *rust* out."

He did preach, and his hearers' hearts were full. They seemed to know it would be the last time. The service closed with the Lord's Supper, administered by Lyte to his sorrowing flock.

That afternoon he walked along the shore to watch the sun setting in a glory of crimson and gold. Returning home, he shut himself in his study for a brief hour, and when he came out he handed a near relative the manuscript of "Abide with me." He also had composed his own tune for the words --- but it never came into general use.

The following week, Lyte left England for Italy. He got no farther than Nice, France, where he was obliged to stop. He died there on November 20, 1847. His last words were: *"Joy! Peace!"* --- and then he fell asleep.

> Abide with me! fast falls the eventide;
> The darkness deepens; Lord, with me abide!
> When other helpers fail, and comforts flee,
> Help of the helpless, O abide with me!

## The Hymns

In Lyte, poetry and religion are "more exquisitely united" than in almost any other hymnist. His hymns are real lyrics --- Scriptural in language, rich in imagery, exalted in poetic conception.

- **"Abide with me"** --- his swan song, written his last Sunday
- **"Praise, my soul, the King of heaven"** --- a hymn of soaring adoration
- **"Jesus, I my cross have taken"**
- **"Pleasant are Thy courts above"**
- **"God of mercy, God of grace"**
- **"As pants the hart for cooling streams"**
- **"O that the Lord's salvation"** --- one of the few hymns ever written as a prayer for the salvation of Israel

## Legacy

It was Lyte's lifelong wish to write a hymn that would outlive him. In an earlier poem he had voiced the longing to write *"some simple strain, some spirit-moving lay, some sparklet of the soul that still might live when I was passed to clay."* He prayed to spend his last breath *"in song that may not die."*

God heard that prayer. As long as men worship the crucified and risen Lord, they will continue to sing the sad and beautiful words of Lyte's swan song.

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

Butterworth-Brown add important details about both of Lyte's most-loved hymns.

For "Abide with Me," they note that Lyte himself had composed a tune for his dying words — and that his own setting was quietly set aside when the compilers of *Hymns Ancient and Modern* (1861), discovering the hymn had no suitable music, asked [[William_Henry_Monk]] to supply one. "In ten minutes," it is said, "Dr. Monk composed the sweet, pleading chant that is wedded permanently to Lyte's swan song." There is, Butterworth-Brown note, "a pathos in the neglect and oblivion of Lyte's own tune set by himself to his words" — especially given that it was "in a sense the work of a dying man" who had prayed to be "not wholly mute and useless" while lying in his grave.

For "Jesus, I My Cross Have Taken" — another characteristic Lyte hymn — Butterworth-Brown note it "originally consisted of six stanzas" and that "the grief of an unhappy division or difference in his church weighed upon his spirit." The tunes "Autumn" by F.H. Barthelemon and "Ellesdie" from Mozart are described as alternatives, both familiar.

## Hymnological Context (Duffield)

Duffield (1886) treats 10 of Lyte's hymns, giving him one of the most extended biographical treatments in the volume. His account of the composition of **"Abide with Me"** is detailed and moving.

Duffield confirms that Lyte served from 1823 until his death in 1847 as perpetual curate of Brixham, Devonshire — "among a hardy class of seafaring folk." He quotes Lyte's own words about being forced to leave England for health reasons: "The swallows are preparing for flight, and inviting me to accompany them; and yet, alas! while I talk of flying, I am just able to crawl, and ask myself whether I shall be able to leave England at all."

Duffield records that on the final Sunday Lyte conducted communion for his congregation, he dragged himself to his room afterward and "that very evening he gave to a relative this hymn in its original eight stanzas" — accompanied by music adapted by Lyte himself, which Duffield notes "has no other merit than this association." The eight-stanza original length (most collections use only a few stanzas) is confirmed by Duffield. Shortly after reaching Nice, France, Lyte died "pointing upward, and whispering, 'Peace! joy!'"

Duffield also notes that in 1883, the church of Lower Brixham was rebuilt as a monument to Lyte's name — a memorial erected thirty-six years after his death. He mentions that a young man wept copiously at Lyte's grave in Nice, saying the words of "Abide with Me" had been "directly instrumental in his conversion."

On **"Jesus, I My Cross Have Taken"**, Duffield records that Lyte entered the ministry of the Church of England after giving up his original intention of studying medicine — a biographical detail enriching the hymn's theme of consecration.
