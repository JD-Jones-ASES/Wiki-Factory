# Anatolius

Anatolius is one of those figures about whom history has preserved very little --- but whose hymns have proven surprisingly durable.

## What We Know

Ryden notes with candor that "concerning this man very little is known except that he lived in the seventh or eighth century." Even the basic facts of his life are obscure. He is identified as a Greek hymnist and believed to have lived in or around Constantinople, but beyond that the biographical record is nearly blank.

What is not blank is his output. He left about one hundred hymns --- a substantial body of work. Among these, Ryden identifies at least three that were still in common use at the time he was writing (1930):

**"Fierce was the wild billow"** --- a meditation on the stilling of the storm in Galilee, which Ryden calls "one of the classics of Greek hymnology." Its evocation of the disciples' terror and Christ's commanding peace has a vividness that age has not dimmed:

> Fierce was the wild billow,
> Dark was the night;
> Oars labored heavily,
> Foam glimmered white;
>
> Trembled the mariners,
> Peril was nigh;
> Then said the God of God,
> "Peace! It is I."

**"The day is past and over"** --- an evening hymn.

**"A great and mighty wonder"** --- a Christmas hymn that Ryden describes as being "of unusual charm."

All three were translated into English by [[John_Mason_Neale]], who rescued much of the Greek hymn tradition from obscurity in the nineteenth century.

## Significance

Anatolius stands as evidence that the Greek hymn tradition remained alive and creative well into the medieval period, even as congregational singing declined in the Eastern church. His hundred hymns represent a significant body of work, and the three that survived into English use through Neale's translations have earned him a permanent, if modest, place in the history of Christian song.
