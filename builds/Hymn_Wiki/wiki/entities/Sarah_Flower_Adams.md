# Sarah Flower Adams

William McKinley, the martyred president of the United States, was dying of an assassin's bullet when his attending physician heard him murmur: *"'Nearer, my God, to Thee, e'en though it be a cross' has been my constant prayer."* The hymn that had become the dying man's prayer was written sixty years earlier by a woman who never fully recovered from nursing her sister through the same disease that killed them both.

## The Flower Sisters

Sarah Flower was born at Harlow, England, on February 22, 1805, the daughter of Benjamin Flower, editor of the Cambridge *Intelligencer*. Her mother died when Sarah was only five years old. She grew up alongside her sister Eliza --- Sarah with the gift for words, Eliza with the gift for music. In later years, Eliza would write the music for Sarah's hymns.

Sarah was drawn to the stage and believed the theater could teach great moral truths as powerfully as the pulpit. Her dreams of becoming an actress, however, were frustrated by poor health. In 1834 she married John Bridges Adams, a civil engineer, and made her home in London.

## The Hymn

"Nearer, my God, to Thee" was written in 1840, the same year Sarah was nursing her consumptive sister. It was published the following year in a volume of hymns edited by her pastor, Rev. William Johnson Fox, a Unitarian minister. For this reason Mrs. Adams has sometimes been classified as a Unitarian. It is said, however, that she became a Baptist near the close of her life, and her later hymns suggest a living faith in Christ. Perhaps the trials she suffered proved to be the means of bringing her to the Saviour. Thus was fulfilled in her own life the beautiful lines:

> E'en though it be a cross
>   That raiseth me.

The hymn has aroused more discussion than almost any other. Because it is based entirely on the story of Jacob at Bethel and does not explicitly name Christ, it has been called more Unitarian than Christian. Various attempts have been made to write a substitute hymn with a more definite evangelical note, but none has displaced the original. The hymn endures because it touches something deeper than doctrine --- the ache of a soul that longs to be nearer to God, even through suffering.

> Nearer, my God, to Thee!
>   Nearer to Thee!
> E'en though it be a cross
>   That raiseth me,
> Still all my song shall be,
> Nearer, my God, to Thee,
>   Nearer to Thee!

## The End

Sarah cared for her sister Eliza through the final stages of tuberculosis. Eliza died in 1846, and Sarah survived her by less than two years, dying in 1848. She was forty-three.

Her great hymn outlived her by more than a century. Ryden calls it "one of the greatest sacred lyrics ever given to the world, and probably the finest ever written by a woman."

## Legacy

There is a remarkable fitness in the fact that a hymn about drawing near to God through crosses was written by a woman acquainted with crosses --- failed health, the stage she could not reach, the sister she could not save. It was murmured by a dying president. It has been sung at state funerals. It has accompanied the dying to the threshold of eternity.

That is more than most lives accomplish.

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

Butterworth-Brown supply the backstory of the "providential chain of causes" that produced the hymn. William Stead recalls that Benjamin Flower — Sarah's father and editor of the *Cambridge Intelligencer* — was imprisoned in 1798 at the instigation of Bishop Watson for an article defending the French Revolution. One of the ladies who visited him in jail was Miss Eliza Gould. That first acquaintance led to marriage, and of that marriage Sarah Flower was born. Stead concludes that "the forgotten vindictiveness of a British prelate was the *causa causans* of one of the most spiritual and aspiring hymns in the Christian Hymnary."

On the hymn's reach, they note that it was dear to President Roosevelt for its memories of the battle of Las Quasimas, "where the Rough Riders sang it at the burial of their slain comrades." Bishop Marvin was saved from hopeless dejection during the Civil War by hearing it sung in a log hut in the wilds of Arkansas. And a forger who had been a fugitive from justice for eight years heard it at a Pittsburgh theater service in November 1905, went out weeping bitterly, walked the floor of his room all night, and in the morning telephoned the police to surrender himself.

On the attempts to "evangelize" the hymn by those troubled by Adams's Unitarian associations, Butterworth-Brown are dismissive: "Such zeal is as needless as trying to sectarianize an Old Testament Psalm. The poem is a perfect religious piece — to be sung as it stands, with thanks that it was ever created."

For the tune: English churches since 1861 used "Horbury" by J.B. Dykes and "St. Edmund" by Arthur Sullivan, both "simple and appropriate." But Lowell Mason's "Bethany" (1856) captured the popular heart, and in America hymn and tune became each other's property — so fixed that one wonders why it was not named "Bethel" instead, given the Bethel imagery of Jacob's stone pillow that pervades the words.
