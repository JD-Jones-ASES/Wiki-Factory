# Benjamin Carr

Benjamin Carr was born in England around 1769, received thorough musical training there, and had been connected with the London Ancient Concerts before emigrating to America in 1793. After landing in New York he set up briefly as a music dealer, then moved to Philadelphia, where he advertised in 1793-94 as "B. Carr & Co., Music Printers and Importers." He ran his Musical Repository in Philadelphia from 1794 to 1800. His New York branch was sold to James Hewitt around 1797-1798. A probable brother, Joseph Carr, opened a Musical Repository in Baltimore in 1794, and the firm continued far into the nineteenth century, Joseph in Baltimore, Benjamin in Philadelphia.

## The Musical Fund Society

Carr's most lasting civic contribution was the organization of the Musical Fund Society of Philadelphia. As early as 1816 he was among a quartet of musicians trying to form a society for regular practice. The first organizational meetings were held at his house, and before the society was finally constituted it was determined that assisting needy musicians would be among its purposes. It was formally instituted February 29, 1820, and chartered February 22, 1823. Among its earliest members were Leopold Meignen, Raynor Taylor, John Darley, Thomas Loud, and Joseph C. Taws.

## Musical Work

Carr served for a time as organist of Saint Joseph's Catholic Church in Philadelphia. He compiled *Masses, Vespers, Litanies, Hymns, Psalms, Anthems and Motetts* for use in Catholic churches across the United States. In 1811 he issued *Lessons and Exercises in Vocal Music* (60 pages, Opus VIII).

"The Archers, or the Mountaineers of Switzerland" — an opera founded on the story of William Tell, with words by William Dunlap and music by Carr — was first produced at the John Street Theater in New York, April 18, 1796, and may have been the first American opera ever composed.

He died May 24, 1831, aged 62. His monument at Saint Peter's Church reads: "Charitable without ostentation, faithful and true in his friendships, with the intelligence of a man, he united the simplicity of a child." Metcalf notes: "Of all the early musicians of Philadelphia he wrought the most vigorously to introduce the best music and especially the oratorios into the church." (Metcalf, p. 140-142)
