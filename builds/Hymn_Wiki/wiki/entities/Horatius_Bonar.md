# Horatius Bonar

One of Scotland's most earnest soul-winners was also its greatest hymnist. He was Horatius Bonar, and a name that will be forever cherished by all who find their love for Christ so beautifully expressed in spiritual song.

Ryden describes him as "the strange anomaly of a man with a strong physique and powerful intellect combined with the gentle, sympathetic nature of a woman and the simple, confiding faith of a child." No better description of Bonar has been written.

## Born into Ministry

Bonar was born in Edinburgh on December 19, 1808. His father was a lawyer, but he came from a long line of eminent Scottish ministers. His mother was a gentle, pious woman, and it was largely through her influence that her three sons --- John, Horatius, and Andrew --- all entered the ministry of the Church of Scotland. Andrew became a noted Bible commentator.

After completing his studies at the University of Edinburgh, Horatius began mission work in Leith, under Rev. James Lewis. He worked in one of the most squalid parts of the city, conducting services and Sunday school in a hall. The children did not enjoy the metrical Psalm paraphrases then used exclusively in Scottish worship. So Bonar did what Luther had done centuries before: he chose happy tunes familiar to the children and wrote words to fit them. His first two hymns were "I lay my sins on Jesus" and "The morning, the bright and beautiful morning."

## The Heart of His Hymns

*"I try to fill my hymns with the love and light of Christ,"* Bonar once said. He kept his word. The warmth and sincerity of his personal faith may be seen in everything he wrote.

He is ever pointing in his hymns to Christ as an all-sufficient Saviour, dwelling in simple language on the blessings of the Atonement. In days of theological fashion that emphasized the "Christ-life" while downplaying Christ's atoning work, Bonar kept singing:

> Upon a Life I have not lived,
>   Upon a Death I did not die,
> Another's Life; Another's Death:
>   I stake my whole eternity.

## A Minister of Prayer

Bonar was ordained in 1843 as minister of a new church at Kelso. He was a man of prayer, and his first sermon to his people was an exhortation to prayer. A story is told of a young servant in his home who was converted simply by overhearing his earnest supplications from behind his locked study door. "If he needs to pray so much," she thought, "what will become of me if I do not pray?"

His pastoral gifts were equally tender. A young man came to him tormented by a grievous sin, unable to believe God could forgive him, insisting on the greatness of his transgression. Finally Bonar asked him: "Tell me --- which is of greater weight in the eyes of God: your sin, black as it is, or the blood of Jesus, shed for sinners?" Light broke, and the young man cried joyfully: *"Oh, I am sure the blood of Jesus weighs more heavily than even my sin!"*

## Sorrow and Song

Bonar married Miss Jane Lundie in 1843, and for forty years they shared joy and sorrow together. She too was a gifted writer; she gave the Church the beautiful gem "Fade, fade, each earthly joy."

Five of their children died in early years. It required much grace to write, in the midst of such losses:

> Spare not the stroke; do with us as Thou wilt;
>   Let there be naught unfinished, broken, marred.
> Complete Thy purpose, that we may become
>   Thy perfect image, O our God and Lord.

## The Hymns

Bonar wrote about 600 hymns, at least 100 of which are in common use today. He never used them in his own church worship --- but when on one occasion near the end of his life he broke that rule, two of his elders showed their disapproval by walking out of the service. He bore it with characteristic patience.

His most famous hymns include:

- **"I heard the voice of Jesus say"** --- perhaps his finest
- **"I lay my sins on Jesus"**
- **"Thy works, not mine, O Christ"**
- **"Not what my hands have done"**
- **"Blessing, and honor, and glory, and power"**
- **"Thy way, not mine, O Lord"**
- **"A few more years shall roll"**
- **"Go, labor on! Spend and be spent"**

## The Last Hymn

At Bonar's funeral, one of his own hymns was sung. It was written on the theme of his family motto, "Heaven at Last":

> What a city! what a glory!
> Far beyond the brightest story
> Of the ages old and hoary:
>   Ah, 'tis heaven at last!

Horatius Bonar died in 1889, deeply mourned by all Scotland.

## Legacy

Bonar stands as one of the great Christocentric hymnists of the nineteenth century. His hymns are not cold theology set to meter; they are the record of a soul in love with a Saviour. They drew people to Christ in his lifetime, and they still do.

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

Butterworth-Brown note that hymns used to "come to" Bonar while riding on railroad trains, and that he studied theology under Dr. Chalmers and was greatly influenced by Dr. Guthrie — following them into the Free Church of Scotland. They credit "I Heard the Voice of Jesus Say" with a literary distinction: Dr. David Breed calls it "one of the most ingenious hymns in the language," referring to the fact that "the invitation and response exactly halve each stanza between them — song followed by countersong." Butterworth-Brown gently demur: "'Ingenious' seems hardly the right word for a division so obviously natural and almost automatic. It is a simple art beauty that a poet of culture makes by instinct."

For the tune, they survey an unusual variety of musical companions:

- **"Evan"** — long a standard, made by Lowell Mason in 1850 from a song written four years earlier by William Henry Havergal (Frances Ridley Havergal's father, Canon of Worcester Cathedral). Its weakness, they note, is that it "divides the stanzas into quatrains, which breaks the happy continuity."
- **"Athens"** by Felice Giardini (1716-1796) — the more ancient option, with "involuntary accent of its sextuple time, exactly reproducing the easy iambic of the verses." Thousands of older singers "will have no other music with 'I heard the voice of Jesus say.'"
- **"Vox Jesu"** from Louis Spohr's quartet — "a psalm-tune of good harmony, but too little feeling."
- **Hubert P. Main's arrangement from Franz Abt** (in A flat, triple time) — their preferred choice: "gentle music through the first fifteen bars, in alternate duet and quartet, utters the Divine Voice with the true accent of the lines, and the second portion completes the harmony in glad, full chorus — the answer of the human heart."
- **"Vox Dilecti"** by J.B. Dykes — written in B flat minor, "which seems a needless substitution of divine sadness for divine sweetness … its shift of key to G natural after the first four lines marks it rather for trained choir performance than for assembly song."

## Hymnological Context (Duffield)

Duffield (1886) treats 21 of Bonar's hymns — giving him more coverage than any other Scottish writer in the volume. Duffield's entries provide precise publication data and occasional biographical circumstances.

**"A Few More Years Shall Roll"** is placed in Hymns of Faith and Hope, First Series, 1857 (titled "A Pilgrim's Song" in six stanzas). Duffield notes the hymn's chorus varies by a single word in each stanza — "great" day, "blest" day, "calm" day, "sweet" day, and "glad" day — and ranks it in popularity "next to 'I lay my sins on Jesus.'"

**"I Lay My Sins on Jesus"** Duffield calls "The Substitute" — Bonar's own title — noting that it was among Bonar's most widely used hymns for spiritual counsel in sick-rooms and deathbed settings.

**"I Heard the Voice of Jesus Say"** (Hymns of Faith and Hope, First Series, 1857, titled "The Voice from Galilee") has its scriptural text as John 1:16 and was composed in three stanzas. Duffield's brief entry belies the hymn's enormous pastoral significance.

**"Beyond the Smiling and the Weeping"**: Duffield records that some of Bonar's hymns were written at Edinburgh, noting they were "already to some of us tender and dear associations" when he wrote in 1886.

**"He Has Come! the Christ of God"** (Year of Praise, 1862) is confirmed as appearing in that specific collection, giving a precise date.

**"Thy Works, Not Mine, O Christ"** was written in 1861 in four six-line stanzas. Duffield notes Bonar's brother John also wrote hymns "of which some found acceptance."

**"Yes, for Me, for Me He Careth"** is said by Duffield to have been written in 1844, with the title drawn from 1 Peter 5:7.

Duffield notes that Bonar's collection Hymns of Faith and Hope was issued in three series (1857, 1861, 1866), providing a useful framework for dating his works chronologically.
