# George Frederic Handel

George Frederic Handel, author of the immortal "Messiah," was born at Halle, Saxony, in 1685, and died in London in 1759. The musical bent of his genius was apparent almost from his infancy. At the age of eighteen he was earning his living with his violin and writing his first opera.

After a sojourn in Italy, he settled in Hanover as Chapel Master to the Elector, who afterwards became the English King George I. The friendship of the king and several of his noblemen drew him to England, where he spent forty-seven years and composed his greatest works.

## The Magnificat and Sacred Works

None among the many musical settings of the Magnificat, writes Butterworth-Brown, "have equaled the work of Handel. His 'Magnificat' was one of his favorite productions, and he borrowed strains from it in several of his later and lesser productions."

Like [[Joseph_Haydn]] at work on "The Creation," Handel composed his "Messiah" in a spirit of daily prayer. Butterworth-Brown quote Haydn's account of his own practice with evident intention to imply the same devotion in Handel: both men composed their supreme sacred works under what they understood to be divine guidance.

## Hymn Tunes

Handel wrote three hymn tunes, said to have been composed at the request of a converted actress:

- **"Canons"** — an invitation: "Sinners, Obey the Gospel Word"
- **"Fitzwilliam"** — a meditation: "O Love Divine, How Sweet Thou Art"
- **"Gopsall"** — a resurrection song to Wesley's "Rejoice, the Lord is King"

Of these three, "Gopsall" still survives in some hymnals, its jubilant character making it a natural expression of [[Charles_Wesley]]'s triumphant praise.

## Musical and Thematic Context

Butterworth-Brown place Handel's sacred music at the summit of the classical hymnic tradition. Henry Smart, they note, wrote "a noble one" for the Magnificat — but Handel's version is placed above even that. His tune "Thacher," also associated with Isaac Watts's "Is This the Kind Return?" carries "the meaning and pathos of the lyric text to voice and instrument" in a way that Butterworth-Brown distinguish from the older "Golden Hill." The reach of Handel's influence on Christian song was felt not only through his formal compositions but through the way his harmonic language shaped the expectations of generations of Anglican and Dissenting worshippers.

## "Antioch" and "Joy to the World"

One of Handel's most pervasive hymnic contributions arrived through adaptation rather than original composition. The tune "Antioch," used for Isaac Watts's "Joy to the World," was drawn from Handel's "Messiah" and shaped into its familiar carol form by Lowell Mason. The melody carries enough of the Messiah's jubilant character that the adaptation has always felt natural --- the Handel of the "Hallelujah Chorus" and the Handel of "Antioch" are clearly the same composer, working in the same register of celebratory faith.

## Handel and Blindness

Handel went blind in 1753 at the age of sixty-eight, eight years before his death. He continued to conduct and play from memory during those final years, presiding over performances of the Messiah even when he could no longer see the score. He died on Good Friday, April 14, 1759.

## "Dead March from Saul"

The "Dead March" from Handel's oratorio *Saul* became standard funeral music in England and remained so through the nineteenth century. Butterworth-Brown note its use at state occasions and military funerals --- a secular composition that the Christian community adopted as the musical expression of solemn grief and formal farewell.

## "Christmas" Tune

The tune "Christmas," drawn from Handel's opera *Ciroe* (Cyrus, 1738), was adapted for Philip Doddridge's hymn "Awake My Soul, Stretch Every Nerve." This is another instance of the pattern common in hymnody: a secular melody lifted from its original context and given a sacred life it keeps for generations.
