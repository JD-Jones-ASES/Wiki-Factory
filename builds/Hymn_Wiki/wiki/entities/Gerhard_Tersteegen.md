# Gerhard Tersteegen

## The Mystic of the Rhine

Among German hymn-writers of the eighteenth century, Gerhard Tersteegen occupies a singular and somewhat solitary position. While [[Pietism|the Pietist movement]] was producing a great crop of warm devotional hymns, Tersteegen stood somewhat apart from organized movements --- a mystic whose home became a "Pilgrim's Hut," a Reformed layman who influenced believers of every tradition, including the Wesleys in England.

Ryden writes that "except for the Wesleys in England, no man during his age exerted so great a spiritual influence in evangelical circles of all lands as did Tersteegen."

## Biographical Sketch

Tersteegen was born at Mörs, Rhenish Prussia, on November 25, 1697. His father died when he was only six, and the family's financial circumstances made it impossible for him to pursue the ministry as his parents had hoped. At sixteen he was apprenticed to a merchant; at twenty he set up in business for himself.

Even in these early years he was drawn toward solitude and prayer, and began experiencing deep seasons of spiritual despondency that lasted nearly five years. During this period he took up silk weaving, choosing it over more profitable occupations because it allowed more time for prayer and meditation.

The turning point came in 1724, while he was traveling to a neighboring town. Light broke through his troubled soul; he became filled with assurance that God's grace in Christ was sufficient for all his sin. In his joy he immediately wrote the hymn "How Gracious, Kind and Good, My Great High Priest, Art Thou."

## The Blood Covenant

In one of his most extraordinary spiritual acts, Tersteegen wrote what he called "a covenant between himself and God" --- and signed it with his own blood. The act captures the intensity of his devotional life: Tersteegen was not a man who did anything halfway.

## The Pilgrim's Hut

Having left the organized Reformed Church (though never joining any other formal body), Tersteegen became increasingly known as a spiritual counselor and healer. His home became the "Pilgrim's Hut" --- a refuge for multitudes of sick, troubled, and spiritually seeking people who came from all over his region to receive his guidance. He also traveled widely in his district and made frequent visits to Holland to hold meetings.

Several sects, including the Moravians (see [[Count_Nikolaus_von_Zinzendorf]]), sought to draw him into membership, but he refused to align formally with any church body. He never married (which led to accusations of teaching celibacy) and lived a life of voluntary simplicity and prayer until his death at Mülheim on April 30, 1769.

## His Hymns

Tersteegen's hymns breathe the air of mysticism --- a deep sense of the nearness of God, a longing to be hidden in Christ, a renunciation of everything that competes with the divine love. They are sometimes described as belonging more to the tradition of Bernard of Clairvaux and Thomas à Kempis than to the Reformation stream; yet they are thoroughly evangelical in their grounding.

**"Thou Hidden Love of God, Whose Height"** (*Verborgene Gottes Liebe du*) --- the finest of his hymns; a meditation on the soul's longing to be wholly God's:

> *Thou hidden love of God, whose height,*
> *Whose depth unfathomed no man knows,*
> *I see from far Thy beauteous light,*
> *Inly I sigh for Thy repose:*
> *My heart is pained, nor can it be*
> *At rest; till it find rest in Thee.*

John Wesley was so moved by this hymn that he translated it into English in 1736 during his time in Georgia, having first encountered it through Moravian pilgrims on the Atlantic crossing.

**"Lo, God Is Here, Let Us Adore"** (*Gott ist gegenwärtig*) --- also known as "God Himself Is Present"; a call to reverent worship in the presence of the omnipresent God. Several English versions exist. Wesley also translated this one.

**"God Calling Yet! Shall I Not Hear?"** --- one of the most stirring calls to repentance in Christian hymnody; translated into English by Sarah Borthwick Findlater in her series "Hymns from the Land of Luther."

**"Jesus, Whom Thy Church Doth Own"**, **"O Love Divine, All Else Transcending"**, and **"Triumph, Ye Heavens"** (a Christmas lyric) round out the hymns of his that found wide use.

## On Singing Hymns

Tersteegen's own words about hymn-singing are worth preserving:

> "The pious, reverential singing of hymns has something angelic about it and is accompanied by divine blessing. It quiets and subdues the troubled emotions; it drives away cares and anxieties; it strengthens, refreshes and encourages the soul... When you sing, O soul, remember that you are as truly communing with the holy and omnipresent God as when you are praying. Consider that you are standing in spirit before the throne of God with countless thousands of angels and spirits of the just and that you are blending your weak praises with the music of heaven."

## Significance

Tersteegen represents the contemplative current within Protestant hymnody --- the tradition that insists on the hiddenness of God and the soul's need for quiet, sustained communion rather than excitement and outward activity. His influence crossed denominational lines freely: admired by the Moravians (though he never joined them), translated by the Methodists (Wesley), sung by the Lutherans. In this regard he is perhaps the most ecumenical figure in the German hymnody of his era.

He stands alongside [[Joachim_Neander]] as the other great pillar of German Reformed hymnody, and his hymns remain in use in contemplative Christian circles today.

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

Butterworth-Brown provide precise biographical detail on Tersteegen and a thoughtful comparative analysis of the two tunes for "God Calling Yet."

Tersteegen was born in 1697 in the town of Mors in Westphalia, left an orphan by his father's death and sent to work as an apprentice ribbon-weaver at Mülheim on the Ruhr. At about fifteen he became "deeply concerned for his soul." At twenty-seven he dedicated all his resources and energies to Christ, writing the dedication in his own blood: "God graciously called me out of the world, and granted me the desire to belong to Him, and to be willing to follow Him." He gave up secular employment entirely, and his house became famous as the "Pilgrims' Cottage," visited by people "high and humble from all parts of Germany." He wrote 111 hymns.

The translation typically used in English hymnbooks is by Jane Borthwick, born in Edinburgh in 1813, who with her sister Mrs. Findlater jointly published *Hymns From the Land of Luther* (1854). There is also a more euphonious but less literal translation with "feminine" rhymes ("God calling yet! and shall I never hearken? / But still earth's witcheries my spirit darken") which Butterworth-Brown note is "less easily fitted to music not specially composed for it."

For the tune, Butterworth-Brown compare the two standard companions with characteristic precision. Dykes's "Rivaulx" is "a sober choral that articulates the hymn-writer's sentiment with sincerity and with considerable earnestness, but breathes too faintly the interrogative and expostulary tone of the lines." Their preference is [[Henry_Kemble_Oliver]]'s "Federal Street" — "to voice the devout solicitude and self-remonstrance of the hymn there is no tune superior." The full story of how "Federal Street" was composed (while Oliver was reading Anna Steele's verse at age thirty-one) is told in the *Henry Kemble Oliver* entity; notably the tune was published by Lowell Mason, named for the street in Salem where Oliver's wife was born and married, and was "sung at her burial." At the great Peace Jubilee in Boston (1872), Oliver himself — then in old age — conducted a chorus of ten thousand voices singing his tune.
