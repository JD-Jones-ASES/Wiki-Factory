# William Batchelder Bradbury

William B. Bradbury was, together with [[Lowell_Mason]], the most formative musical influence on American evangelical hymnody in the mid-nineteenth century. His tunes are woven into the fabric of childhood faith for generations of Protestant Christians.

## The Composer of Beloved Tunes

Bradbury composed the tune for "Saviour, Like a Shepherd Lead Us" (Mrs. Dorothy Ann Thrupp's words) in 1856, and it has been the universally accepted and loved musical companion of that hymn ever since.

For "Sweet Hour of Prayer" (words by the blind English minister Rev. William W. Walford), Bradbury composed his tune in 1859, and it first appeared with the hymn in *Cottage Melodies*. The long, meditative lines of the hymn found in his melody a patient, warm companion.

For the Sunday-school hymn "Jesus the Water of Life Will Give" (words by [[Fanny_Crosby]]), he provided the music in his *Fresh Laurels* (1867). The authors observe that "the composer being many years *en rapport* with the writer, knew how to put all her metrical rhythms into sweet sound."

Bradbury also composed the tune for "O Bliss of the Purified! Bliss of the Free!" (Dr. Francis Bottome's words), titled "Songs of the Beautiful" in *Fresh Laurels*.

For "Why Should We Start and Fear to Die?" (Watts' words), his "Zephyr" and "Rest" are praised as the finest musical interpretations of that most hopeful of consolation hymns.

For the funeral hymn "Asleep in Jesus" (Margaret Mackay's words), his "Rest" has carried grieving congregations for a century and a half.

## Relationship with Lowell Mason

Bradbury was trained in the Mason tradition, and like Mason, he was devoted to the cause of music in the Sunday school as much as in the formal church service. His publications, especially *Fresh Laurels* (1867), became standard collections for Sunday-school singing across America.

## Musical Context (Metcalf)

Metcalf's *American Writers and Compilers of Sacred Music* (1925) gives Bradbury a full chapter, tracing his career from Maine farm boy to one of the three men who "led the prevailing school of sacred music at the beginning of the last century" (alongside Mason and Hastings).

Bradbury was born October 6, 1816, at York, Maine, the middle child of five. Both parents were excellent singers. Before fourteen he had learned every instrument available to him, though the organ was not among them in his Maine home. He came to Boston, entered the Mason-Webb circle at the Boston Academy of Music, and served as organist at Bowdoin Street Church for three months at $25 per month --- pulling the keys back up by hand to stop the sound, since the organ had no pedal mechanism for this. He then secured a better-paying position. Mason recommended him to Machias, Maine --- the same settlement where James Lyon had been minister in the 1770s --- and he taught there for a year and a half.

He went to New York, became organist at a church where the new organ had been introduced over considerable opposition, and began his juvenile music classes. At one Musical Festival he had a chorus of one thousand children. In 1847 he sailed with his wife and daughter to England, then spent two years studying in Leipzig --- practicing six hours a day and overworking his right arm for several months. On November 4, 1847, Mendelssohn died in Leipzig and Bradbury attended the funeral; his next collection was named *The Mendelssohn Collection* (1849).

In 1854 he formed a piano manufacturing and sales partnership with his brother. Overwork induced disease of the lungs, and he died January 7, 1868, at his home in Montclair, New Jersey.

Metcalf counted fifty-nine separate books with Bradbury's name on the title page during the twenty-six years from 1841 to 1867 --- more than two a year. His most popular tune, "Woodworth," appeared in all ten hymnals Metcalf surveyed; "He Leadeth Me" appeared in nine of the ten. In the Sunday-school market his sales have been estimated at over two million copies. (Metcalf, p. 274-278)

## Legacy

Bradbury's tunes have a gift that his more sophisticated contemporaries sometimes lacked: they are genuinely singable by a congregation, including one that has never seen a music teacher. His melodies have a natural curve that the human voice follows almost instinctively. Those who sing "Sweet Hour of Prayer" or "Saviour Like a Shepherd" rarely know they are singing Bradbury --- but they could not imagine the words in any other music.
