# Bernard of Clairvaux

Bernard of Clairvaux was the most powerful churchman of his age --- a monk who summoned kings, preached crusades, and swayed the course of European history. But it is not for any of that that he is most remembered in the history of Christian worship. He is remembered for a Good Friday hymn that has moved hearts to tears in three languages and five centuries.

## The Making of a Monk

Bernard was born in 1091, the son of a Burgundian knight. He was destined for a career in the church, but it was his mother's death, when he was twenty years old, that became the decisive turning point. Grief broke something open in him, and he determined to enter a monastery.

He did not go alone. With extraordinary personal magnetism --- a quality that would define his entire career --- he persuaded twelve companions to join him, including an uncle and four of his five brothers. The whole household walked into the cloister together.

When Bernard was just twenty-four, he founded his own monastery in a wild valley in France nicknamed "Wormwood," famous as a haunt of robbers. Bernard renamed it *Clara Vallis* --- "Beautiful Valley" --- from which we derive the name "Clairvaux." Under his leadership it became one of the most famous monasteries in history.

## The Most Powerful Monk in Christendom

Ryden says plainly that "no preacher ever exerted a more profound influence over the age in which he lived" than Bernard. The claim is hard to dispute.

From his monastery, Bernard wielded an influence that reached emperors. Popes sought his counsel. Six of his students became cardinals. Thirty became bishops. One became Pope Eugenius III. Bernard himself refused every offer of higher office, preferring to remain Abbot of Clairvaux --- and wielding more power from that position than most bishops ever dreamed of.

Luther, who had little patience for the religious establishment of any era, paid Bernard an extraordinary tribute: "If there has ever been a pious monk who feared God it was St. Bernard, whom alone I hold in much higher esteem than all other monks and priests throughout the globe."

## The Preacher of the Crusade

Bernard's greatest public act --- and ultimately his greatest sorrow --- was preaching the Second Crusade in 1146. Traveling through France and Germany, he preached with an effect that Ryden describes as "almost miraculous." Whole towns seemed to empty as people volunteered for the crusade. "In the towns where I have preached," Bernard said, "scarcely one man is left to seven women."

The crusade was a disaster. Perhaps only one-tenth of the vast army reached Palestine. The rest died or scattered along the way. The expedition returned in defeat and disgrace. The blame fell heavily on Bernard, who had been its great advocate. It is generally believed that the sorrow of that failure hastened his death in 1153.

## The Hymns

Bernard's hymns survive the disaster of the Crusade and have outlived his century by eight hundred years.

His greatest hymn is a Good Friday meditation addressed to the head of the crucified Christ, beginning in Latin with the words *Salve caput cruentatum* --- often translated as [[Hymn_0177_O_Sacred_Head_Now_Wounded|"O sacred Head, now wounded"]]. The hymn is a sustained act of devotion before the Passion, moving from grief at Christ's suffering to personal confession to grateful love. It is one of the most intimate and searching of all Passion hymns.

The hymn had an extraordinary journey across the centuries. Bernard wrote it in Latin in the twelfth century. Five hundred years later, the great Lutheran hymn writer [[Paul_Gerhardt]] translated it into German, deepening and enriching it. Gerhardt's German version was then translated into English by James W. Alexander of Princeton. And Johann Sebastian Bach, in the early eighteenth century, gave it the melody known as the Passion Chorale --- originally a rather frivolous German folk song, but transformed by Bach into one of the world's masterpieces of sacred music.

Philip Schaff described this remarkable story beautifully: "This classic hymn has shown in three tongues --- Latin, German and English --- and in three confessions --- Roman, Lutheran and Reformed --- with equal effect the dying love of our Saviour and our boundless indebtedness to Him."

Bernard also wrote a long Latin lyric poem called *De Nomine Jesu* ("On the Name of Jesus"), from which at least three more beloved English hymns have been drawn:
- "Jesus, the very thought of Thee" (translated by Edward Caswall)
- "O Jesus! King most wonderful" (also Caswall)
- "O Jesus, Joy of loving hearts!" (translated by Ray Palmer)

These hymns of Bernard's have a quality that is almost unique in medieval devotional writing: they are intensely personal and warm, addressed directly to Jesus with an intimacy that anticipates the best of Protestant evangelical devotion by four centuries.

## Legacy

The story of Christian Schwartz, a Lutheran missionary in India, dying in 1798 with his Indian pupils gathered around his bed singing the final verses of "O sacred Head" in their own Malabar tongue --- this story, recorded by Ryden, captures something essential about Bernard's hymn. It crossed every boundary of language, nationality, denomination, and time. It has done what the very best hymns do: it has gone wherever people carry the weight of their own mortality and the weight of Christ's love, and offered them a language for both.
