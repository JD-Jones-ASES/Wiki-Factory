# Christopher Wordsworth

Christopher Wordsworth did what almost no other Victorian hymn-writer attempted: he wrote a complete set of hymns for the entire Christian year — one or more for each Sunday, season, feast, and occasion in the Anglican calendar — and published them together in a single volume. The *Holy Year* (1862) is a monument of systematic hymnody.

## Life

Christopher Wordsworth was born on October 30, 1807 — a nephew of the poet William Wordsworth. He was educated at Trinity College, Cambridge, where he graduated in 1830 and was elected a Fellow. He served in various capacities in the Church of England, including as headmaster of Harrow School, before becoming a canon and eventually Bishop of Lincoln in 1869. He resigned the see in 1885, shortly before his death on March 20, 1885.

He was a scholar of note. Duffield describes him as "conspicuous by his Commentary upon the Old Testament in the Authorized Version, and upon the New Testament in the Greek," adding that his travel volumes on Greece and other regions also marked him as a man of broad learning.

## The Holy Year (1862)

All seven of Wordsworth's hymns covered by Duffield date from the *Holy Year* (1862), Wordsworth's single great contribution to English hymnody. The volume organized hymns by liturgical occasion and gave the Church of England a complete hymnological complement to its calendar.

**"Hark! the Sound of Holy Voices"** was written as the hymn for All Saints' Day, based on Revelation 5:6 — a "chorus of triumph" celebrating the company of heaven.

**"O Day of Rest and Gladness"** is the most widely used of Wordsworth's hymns in subsequent collections. As a Sunday hymn, it celebrates the day of resurrection with the richness of its accumulated typology — Creation, Exodus, Easter — woven through each stanza.

**"Gracious Spirit, Holy Ghost"** (Quinquagesima Sunday) is based on 1 Corinthians 13 — the great passage on charity — and brings the epistle text into congregational form. Duffield notes its scriptural grounding with approval.

**"See, the Conqueror Mounts in Triumph"** (Ascension) and **"The Day Is Gently Sinking to a Close"** (Evensong) represent the breadth of the *Holy Year* — hymns for specific moments in the liturgical day as well as the liturgical year.

**"O Lord of Heaven, and Earth, and Sea"** is an offertory hymn. Duffield quotes a Talmudic story about the Rabbi Tarphon as a commentary on it — evidence that Wordsworth's hymns invite theological commentary from unexpected sources.

**"Holy, Holy, Holy Lord — God of Hosts!"** has eight stanzas in the original *Holy Year*, though it is usually excerpted.

## Legacy

Christopher Wordsworth is the systematic hymnographer of Victorian Anglicanism — not a poet of intensity like [[Henry_Francis_Lyte]] or a lyricist of natural grace like [[Reginald_Heber]], but a careful craftsman who ensured that the Church calendar had a complete hymnological accompaniment. His *Holy Year* influenced Anglican hymnody throughout the rest of the century.

## Hymnological Context (Duffield)

Duffield (1886) covers 7 of Wordsworth's hymns, all from the *Holy Year* (1862). He treats Wordsworth respectfully as a scholar-bishop whose hymns serve the liturgy efficiently, while noting they lack some of the lyrical spontaneity of the greatest hymn-writers. For "The Day Is Gently Sinking to a Close," Duffield notes it "recalls the fine painting by Paul Delaroche — the vision of Christ walking upon the sea" — placing the hymn in the context of Victorian religious art as well as liturgy.
