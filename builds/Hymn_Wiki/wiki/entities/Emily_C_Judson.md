# Emily C. Judson

Emily Chubbuck was born at Eaton, a small town in central New York, August 22, 1817. Her parents were poor — at an early age she helped support the family by working in a woolen factory. Afterward she taught the village school, and at twenty was a welcome contributor to the poetical column of the village newspaper.

## From "Fanny Forester" to Missionary Wife

Having attracted the attention of the Misses Sheldon, who kept a well-known young ladies' school in Utica, she was given educational opportunities she gladly embraced. To continue supporting her parents, she began writing stories for children — later published as "Alderbrook" — and when N. P. Willis made her welcome to the columns of the *Evening Mirror*, she had, after a long struggle with poverty and adverse circumstances, made her way to a position of honor in the literary world. Her pen name "Fanny Forester" was well known.

Converted at eight years of age, she had from early on a sense that she would one day be a missionary. In January 1846, she met Dr. [[Adoniram_Judson]] at the home of Rev. A. D. Gillette in Philadelphia. They were married June 2, 1846, and within weeks embarked for Burma.

## At the Isle of St. Helena

Passing the island of St. Helena — where [[Sarah_B_Judson]] had been buried the previous September — Emily Chubbuck Judson wrote a tribute to the memory of her predecessor, beginning:

*Blow softly, gales! a tender sigh / Is flung upon your wing; / Lose not the treasure, as ye fly, / Bear it where love and beauty lie, / Silent and withering.*

*Mourn, Burma, mourn! a bow, which spanned / Thy cloud, has passed away; / A flower has withered on thy sand, / A pitying spirit left thy strand, / A saint has ceased to pray.*

*Weak, timid, frail, yet would my soul / Fain be to hers allied.*

This poem is one of the most affecting tributes in the Baptist missionary literary record — a new wife honoring a predecessor she never met, writing from the same ocean passage, bound for the same mission field.

## In Burma

The Judsons arrived at Maulmain, November 30, 1846. Emily devoted herself to learning the language and to preparing a biography of Sarah Boardman Judson. A daughter, Emily Frances, was born December 24, 1847.

Her health began to decline soon after. When Adoniram Judson was attacked by the disease that would kill him, and set sail from Maulmain on April 3, 1850, Emily was too ill to accompany him. She gave birth to a second child, Charles, on the very day news of his father's death arrived — and Charles died the same day he was born.

Emily eventually returned to the United States with her surviving daughter. She died at Hamilton, New York, June 1, 1854.

## Her Hymns and Poetry

In Dyer's *Psalmist* there is a hymn by Emily Chubbuck beginning

*Mother, has the dove that nestled*

The poem to Sarah Boardman Judson written off St. Helena, and the other verses composed in Burma (including the lines on "Angel Charlie" — the infant who died the day he was born), belong to a body of devotional poetry that emerged directly from the crucible of the Judson mission.

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Burrage's account of Emily C. Judson is illustrated with a portrait — one of only five portraits in the entire book — indicating how central the Judson family was to American Baptist identity. Together, the three Judson women (Ann, Sarah, Emily) represent the full human cost and the full devotional richness of the nineteenth-century Baptist missionary movement. Emily is the literary one among them, the professional writer who gave up a promising career to go to Burma; and her tribute to Sarah Boardman Judson remains one of the most beautifully written pieces in the Baptist canon.
