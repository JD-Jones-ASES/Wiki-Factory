# Robert Robinson

Robert Robinson was born in Norfolk, England, on September 27, 1735. Left fatherless at eight years of age, he was apprenticed to a barber, but was converted by the preaching of [[George_Whitefield]] and studied until he obtained a good education. He was ordained to the Methodist ministry. An "unsteadiness of mind," Butterworth-Brown note, "caused him to revise his religious beliefs too often for his spiritual health or enjoyment," and he passed successively through Methodist, Baptist, and Independent ministries before finally becoming a Socinian. He died on June 9, 1790.

## Come Thou Fount of Every Blessing

Robinson is supposed to have written this hymn in 1758 — in his early, ardent years of faith. The revivalist tune "Nettleton," composed by John Wyeth about 1812, became so closely associated with it that Butterworth-Brown note its "sing-song movement and accent" made it "the country favorite."

The hymn is remembered as much for the story attached to its author's later life as for its text. On a stagecoach journey, a fellow passenger began singing "Come, Thou Fount of Every Blessing" to relieve the monotony of the ride. Robinson said to her: "Madam, I am the unhappy man who wrote that hymn many years ago; and I would give a thousand worlds, if I had them, if I could feel as I felt then."

Butterworth-Brown present this simply, without commentary — but it is one of the most poignant moments in the literature of the hymns: a man confronted by his own younger self, captured in a song that still carried what he had lost.

## Musical and Thematic Context

The tune "Nettleton" was composed by John Wyeth (born Cambridge, Massachusetts, 1792; died Harrisburg, Pennsylvania, 1858), a musician and publisher who issued *Wyeth's Repository of Sacred Music*. The "more wavy melody of 'Sicily'" sometimes carried the verses but, Butterworth-Brown note, "never with the same sympathetic unction."

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Burrage situates Robinson firmly in the English Baptist mainstream. He was ordained pastor of the Baptist church in Cambridge on June 11, 1761 — his success there was remarkable: "Members of the University, and other hearers, who had never in their lives entered a Baptist meeting-house, became regular attendants." In 1764 a new building seating six hundred was built and paid for.

Robinson was an extensive literary man as well as a preacher. His works include a "Plea for the Divinity of our Lord Jesus Christ" (1776), translations of Saurin's Sermons, a "Plan of Lectures on the Principles of Nonconformity," and an unfinished history of the Baptists that left us his "History of Baptism" (1790) and "Ecclesiastical Researches" (1792). [[Charles_Spurgeon]], who began his ministry at the Cambridge church's successor in St. Andrews Street, occupied a pulpit with a direct succession from Robinson.

Burrage addresses the question of Robinson's alleged Unitarianism directly. He quotes a sermon Robinson preached in 1781 — after the date when he is supposed to have become Unitarian — in which Robinson speaks of Christ as "God" and as "a person infinitely lovely both as God and man." Burrage concludes the sermon is "throughout deeply evangelical in doctrine and spirit," and attributes the Unitarian charge to guilt by association with his friend Dr. Priestley. Robinson died at Birmingham, where he had gone to preach for Priestley — and Robert Hall prepared the epitaph calling him "the intrepid champion of liberty, civil and religious."

Burrage notes that Robinson authored a second hymn, *Mighty God, while angels bless thee* — written while holding a boy named Benjamin Williams (later a deacon in the Reading Baptist church) in his lap, beginning as a child's song and then kindling into a full-throated hymn of praise.
