# Samuel Medley

Samuel Medley was born at Cheshunt, Hertfordshire, England, on June 23, 1738. At eighteen he entered the Royal Navy, where, though he had been piously educated, he became dissipated and morally reckless. The turning point came in a sea fight off Cape Lagos: wounded and in dread of amputation, he prayed penitently through nearly a whole night, and in the morning the surprised surgeon told him his limb could be saved.

The voice of his awakened conscience was not immediately heeded, but eventually his vow to begin a religious life was sincerely kept. After teaching school for four years, he began to preach in 1766, with Watford in Hertfordshire as the first scene of his labors. He died in Liverpool on July 17, 1799, at the end of a faithful ministry there of twenty-seven years. A small edition of his hymns was published during his lifetime in 1789.

## O Could I Speak the Matchless Worth

This hymn of worshipping ardor and exalted Christian love — born from the experience of a man who knew exactly what it meant to have been saved, both literally and spiritually — is described by Butterworth-Brown as one of Medley's finest:

> O could I speak the matchless worth,
> O could I sound the glories forth
>   Which in my Saviour shine,
> I'd soar and touch the heavenly strings
> And vie with Gabriel while he sings,
>   In notes almost divine!

## Musical and Thematic Context

Butterworth-Brown note that "Colebrook" — a plain chorale with a noble movement by Henry Smart — is the English music for this hymn, while in America Dr. [[Lowell_Mason]]'s "Ariel" is the favorite. They describe "Ariel" with characteristic precision: it "justifies its name, for it has wings — in both full harmony and duet — and its melody feels the glory of the hymn at every bar."

Medley's other well-loved hymn, "Awake, My Soul, to Joyful Lays," is a simpler piece of devotional joy, one of the earliest examples of what would later become the popular revival tradition.

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Burrage adds important biographical detail. Medley's grandfather was deacon of the Baptist church in Eagle Street, London, under the pastoral care of Rev. Andrew Gifford, D.D. — the family Baptist connection that shaped Medley's life even through his years of wildness. After his wounding off Cape Lagos (August 18, 1759) he was carried to his grandfather's house; the pious old man's prayers were answered, and Medley united with the church in December 1760.

Burrage quotes Medley's dying words: "I am a poor shattered bark just about to gain the blissful harbor — and oh! how sweet will be the port after the storm! But a point or two more, and I shall be at my heavenly Father's home." The sailor's imagery, carried through a lifetime of ministry, returned to him at the end.

Medley published hymns between 1786 and 1790 as leaflets; a volume by request in 1789; a larger volume in 1794. An enlarged edition of 230 hymns appeared in 1800, the year after his death. Burrage notes that Rev. John Stanford's *Collection of Evangelical Hymns* (New York) took fifteen of Medley's hymns — probably their first introduction to American congregations.

Burrage also records the origin story of *Awake, My Soul, to Joyful Lays*, quoting Duffield (*English Hymns*): Medley was visiting at the home of a prominent London Baptist named Phillips, asked for paper and ink, retired to his room, and returned with the hymn written. "Betsey" Phillips, who witnessed this, was born in 1783, became Mrs. Dodds, died in America in 1861, and passed the story down through relatives in Washington, D.C.

Aside from *O Could I Speak* and *Awake, My Soul*, a third hymn particularly noted by Burrage begins *Dearest of names, our Lord, our King* — a meditation on death as a "friendly messenger" that has "angels' lovely form" for "every soul whom Jesus loves."
