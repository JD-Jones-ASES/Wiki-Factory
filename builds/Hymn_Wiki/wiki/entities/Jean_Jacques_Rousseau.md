# Jean-Jacques Rousseau

Jean-Jacques Rousseau was born in Geneva in 1712 and never knew his mother. Neither the affection nor the interest of his father or other relatives was, as Butterworth-Brown note with understated precision, "of the quality to insure the best bringing up of a child." He became the most influential philosopher of natural liberty and the general will in the Enlightenment, a novelist, a political theorist, and — incidentally — a composer. He died in July 1778.

Butterworth-Brown describe him without sentimentality: "that brilliant but erratic genius and freethinker, Jean Jacques Rousseau."

## The Tune "Greenville"

Rousseau's contribution to Christian hymnody is entirely inadvertent. The tune known as "Greenville" began as a love serenade — "Days of Absence, Sad and Dreary" — from his opera *Le Devin du Village*, written about 1752. It was widely known afterwards as "Rousseau's Dream." One legend says the melody came to him in a dream as "a song of angels."

Whatever its origin, the tune proved irresistible to the church he despised. "Greenville" became one of the most versatile and beloved tunes in congregational use — attached to [[Joseph_Hart]]'s "Come Ye Sinners Poor and Needy," to Charles Wesley's "Love Divine, All Loves Excelling" in a version called "Far from Mortal Cares Retreating," and to many other hymns.

Butterworth-Brown put the paradox memorably: the "unbelieving philosopher, musician, and misguided moralist builded better than he knew, and probably better than he meant when he wrote his immortal choral." He created, like Balaam, "a harmony dear to the church he despised, and softened the hearts of the Christian world towards an evil teacher who was inspired, like Balaam, to utter one sacred strain." Their final assessment: "It is almost a pardonable exaggeration to say that every child in Christendom knows 'Greenville.'"
