# William Billings

William Billings was a self-taught genius who became the first truly original voice in American church music --- and a fierce patriot whose tunes were heard from every fife in the New England Revolutionary ranks.

## The Self-Taught Patriot

Billings was born in Boston on October 7, 1746. He was a man of little formal education, but his genius for music spurred him to study the tuneful art unaided. He began to make tunes and publish them; his first book, the *New England Psalm-Singer*, was "a curiosity of youthful crudity and confidence" --- but it sold, and it was sung, and also laughed at. He went on studying and composing. A second work improved so much it got the name *Billings' Best*. A third singing-book followed, and finally a fourth entitled *Psalm Singer's Amusement*, both popular in their day.

## "Chester" --- The Song of the Revolution

Of all his compositions, "Chester" is the most historically significant. Both words and tune were his own. It became the freedom song of the Revolution:

> Let tyrants shake their iron rod,
>   And slavery clank her galling chains;
> We'll fear them not; we trust in God;
>   New England's God forever reigns.

One writer in Moore's *Cyclopedia of Music* recorded: "Chester, it is said, was frequently heard from every fife in the New England ranks. The spirit of the Revolution was also manifest in his 'Lamentation over Boston,' his 'Retrospect,' his 'Independence,' his 'Columbia,' and many other pieces."

His "Majesty" has "tremendous capabilities of sound," its movement fully equal to Nahum Tate's verses:

> And on the wings of mighty winds
>   Came flying all abroad.

## The Easter Anthem

Billings also composed an "Anthem for Easter" in A major (1785) that occupied several pages in the early collections of psalmody and was a fixture of colonial church music. In a style combining bold declamation with fugue, it proclaimed the Resurrection through alternating duet, trio, and full chorus --- from "He rose! He rose!" to the triumphant:

> Then, then *I* rose,
>   And seized eternal youth,
>   Man all immortal, hail!
> Heaven's all the glory, man's the boundless bliss.

Organs were scarce in those years, but beyond the viols of the village choirs, his anthem needed no instrumental accessories.

## Death and Legacy

William Billings died in 1800, and his remains lie in an unmarked grave in the old "Granary" Burying Ground in the city of his birth. He died poor. But the ruder voices he raised set the pitch of American sacred music, and were sung with native pride at fireside vespers and rural feasts alongside the adopted songs of Burns and Moore.

His patriotic ardor was recognized as one secret of his vast popularity. He taught a generation of home-born minstrels who in turn gave America the Hutchinson family and Philip Phillips, the "Singing Pilgrim."

## Musical Context (Metcalf)

Frank Metcalf's chapter on Billings in *American Writers and Compilers of Sacred Music* (1925) is one of the most detailed in the book, drawing on primary sources including the recently located Billings family Bible.

Metcalf confirms the birth date of October 7, 1746, in Boston, from the family Bible in the composer's own handwriting (the mother was Elizabeth, maiden name possibly Clark). Billings was a tanner by trade, married Mary Leonard on December 13, 1764, and then Lucy Swan on July 26, 1774. Lucy Swan had headed the treble section at Billings's singing school in Stoughton in 1774 --- the same school that became the germ of the Stoughton Musical Society, the oldest musical organization in the United States, formally constituted November 7, 1786. (Metcalf, p. 206)

The family had six children: Abigail Adams, Elizabeth Adams, Sarah, William, Peggy (Margaret), and Lucy. Peggy married Colonel William Pope and eventually went to Machias, Maine --- the same settlement where James Lyon had been minister.

Metcalf documents Billings's connection to Samuel Adams: "He and two of his brothers were intimate friends of Samuel Adams, the patriot, and together they liked to sing the 137th psalm, which had been put into political paraphrase." (Metcalf, p. 206) During the Revolutionary War, Billings ran singing schools in Wrentham, Massachusetts, and was involved in teaching the Brattle Street Church singers in 1778.

The death notice in the *Columbian Centinel* for September 27, 1800 reads simply: "William Billings, age 60, the celebrated music composer. His funeral will be held to-morrow, at 4 p.m. from the home of Mr. Amos Penniman, in Chamber Street, West Boston." His grave was in the common enclosure --- unmarked and now unknown.

Metcalf's survey of twenty-five modern hymnals found that Billings's tune "Coronation" --- actually by Oliver Holden, not Billings --- was in every one. Of Billings's own tunes, "Chester" was the most historically significant, and the six published music books represent the most prolific output of any American sacred composer of the eighteenth century.
