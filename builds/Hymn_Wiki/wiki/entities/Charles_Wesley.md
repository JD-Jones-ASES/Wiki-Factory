# Charles Wesley

With [[Isaac_Watts]], Charles Wesley holds the foremost place in English hymnody. No less than 6,500 hymns are said to have flowed from his pen --- a staggering output --- yet what is most remarkable is not the quantity but the quality. Many of them rise to real poetic excellence, and a few stand among the greatest sacred lyrics ever written.

## The Epworth Rectory

Charles was the next to youngest of nineteen children born to the Rev. Samuel Wesley and his remarkable wife Susannah. The family lived in the rectory at Epworth, and poverty and trouble descended on them with almost biblical regularity.

The most dramatic of their many disasters came in 1709, when the house caught fire. In the confusion, young John, only six years old, was left behind. The agonized father fell to his knees in prayer. A neighbor climbed onto another man's shoulders and snatched the boy from a second-story window just as the roof fell in. "A brand plucked from the burning" was how John Wesley would later describe himself --- a phrase that captures the family's sense of divine purpose even in catastrophe.

## Oxford and the Methodists

While studying at Oxford, John and Charles grew increasingly dissatisfied with the low spiritual temperature around them. They gathered a small group of friends committed to Bible study, prayer, fasting, and regular worship. Because of the strict methodical discipline they followed, others mockingly called them "Methodists." The name stuck, and eventually gave its name to one of the great renewal movements of Christian history.

## Conversion

Before either brother truly found peace with God, both had to travel a long, strange road. In 1735 they sailed for the colony of Georgia on a missionary voyage, sharing the ship with a band of Moravians. When a terrible storm struck and threatened to sink the vessel, the Wesley brothers were struck by the calm courage of the Moravians, who sang hymns amid the raging sea. The brothers made the painful discovery that they themselves lacked that deep assurance of salvation.

Back in London, they fell in with other Moravians and encountered Luther's teaching about grace. Charles came to faith during a severe illness. A week later, on May 24, 1738, John attended a meeting in Aldersgate Street where someone was reading Luther's preface to the Epistle to the Romans --- and for the first time, as he famously wrote in his journal, his heart was "strangely warmed."

This was the turning point for the Wesley movement, and it was out of this experience that the great flood of Methodist song began.

## The Hymns

Charles' soul seemed filled with music and poetry, and when the divine spark touched it, it burst into full flame. He wrote on horseback, in stage-coaches, on the decks of ships. Even on his deathbed, at the age of eighty, he dictated his last hymn to his devoted wife. The poem began: "In age and feebleness extreme."

The famous trio at the summit of his output --- "Hark! the herald angels sing," "Love divine, all love excelling," and "Jesus, Lover of my soul" --- has probably never been equaled by any single author. To these must be added:

- **"A charge to keep I have"**
- **"Arise, my soul, arise"**
- **"Christ, whose glory fills the sky"**
- **"Come, Thou long-expected Jesus"**
- **"Soldiers of Christ, arise"**
- **"Hail the day that sees Him rise"**

### "Jesus, Lover of My Soul"

This hymn, published in 1740, is generally recognized as Wesley's masterpiece --- all the more remarkable because it was one of the earliest he wrote. Henry Ward Beecher said of it: *"I would rather have written that hymn of Wesley's than to have the fame of all the kings that ever sat on the earth."* George Duffield called it "the hymn of the ages."

The story of how it was written is unforgettable. One day, while deeply troubled in spirit, Wesley noticed through his open study window a little song-bird pursued by a hungry hawk. The bird fluttered through the window and sheltered in Wesley's arms. Pondering this, the thought struck him that the soul fleeing to Christ is like that bird fleeing to safety --- and he took up his pen.

> Jesus, Lover of my soul,
>   Let me to Thy bosom fly,
> While the nearer waters roll,
>   While the tempest still is high!
> Hide me, O my Saviour, hide,
>   Till the storm of life is past;
> Safe into the haven guide:
>   O receive my soul at last!

## The End

Charles Wesley died on March 29, 1788, after fifty years of service. The night before he fell ill, he had preached in City Road Chapel, London. The hymn before his sermon had been Watts' "I'll praise my Maker, while I've breath." That evening, though already very sick, he sang it through with a strong voice. On the night of his death he tried again, but could only say "I'll praise --- I'll praise ---" and with the praise of his Maker on his lips, he went home to God.

His brother John survived him by three years, dying on March 2, 1791. The text of John's last sermon was: "Seek ye the Lord while He may be found."

## Legacy

The hymns of Charles Wesley were the secret weapon of the Methodist revival. While John preached --- holding no fewer than forty thousand preaching services in his lifetime --- Charles gave the movement its voice. Ryden puts it beautifully: *"The real secret of the success of the Wesleyan movement more likely must be sought in the sublime hymns written by his brother Charles."*

Of the 770 hymns in the Wesleyan Hymn Book, 623 are from Charles Wesley's pen. The number alone is testament to a life poured out in praise.

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

Butterworth-Brown note that "O for a Thousand Tongues to Sing" is believed to have been written May 17, 1739, for the anniversary of Wesley's own conversion — the impulse coming from a remark by the Moravian Peter Bohler: "Had I a thousand tongues I would praise Christ Jesus with them all." The third stanza (in some hymnals printed second) has "made language for pardoned souls for at least four generations." The natural tune for Wesley's characteristic flights, they observe, is not a slow movement of semibreves but something livelier; "a slow pace of single semibreves or quarters is not suited to Wesley's hymns. They are flights." Old "Azmon" by Carl Glaser (1734-1829), arranged by Lowell Mason 1830, is the nearest early companion.

On the literary assessment of Wesley's output, Butterworth-Brown quote Isaac Taylor: "There is no main article of belief … no moral sentiment peculiarly characteristic of the gospel that does not find itself … pointedly and clearly conveyed in some stanza of Charles Wesley's poetry." And from the *London Quarterly*: he "was, perhaps, the most gifted minstrel of the modern Church."

For "Lo! on a Narrow Neck of Land," they record that the hymn is said to have been composed at Land's End, Cornwall, with the British Channel and the broad Atlantic surging on both sides — the physical setting giving the eschatological image its visceral force: "A point of time, a moment's space, / Removes me to that heavenly place, / Or shuts me up in hell." The associated tune was Mason's "Meribah," borrowed from Lady Huntingdon's hymn since Mason was "not yet born" when Wesley wrote these words.

For "A Charge to Keep I Have," Butterworth-Brown note it was "often heard at the camp grounds, from the rows of tents in the morning while the good women prepared their pancakes and coffee," invariably sung to old "Kentucky" by Jeremiah Ingalls — a tune they judge "not field music" and unworthy of a hymn that merits something like "St. Thomas."

## Hymnological Context (Duffield)

Duffield (1886) covers 35 of Charles Wesley's hymns — placing him second in the volume only to Isaac Watts (133 entries). Duffield is particularly valuable on the textual histories and compositional circumstances of Wesley's most famous hymns.

On **"Oh, for a Thousand Tongues to Sing"** (written 1738), Duffield confirms it was written to commemorate Wesley's own conversion and that the original ran to eighteen stanzas — of which most hymnals print only a selection. Duffield is emphatic: "Charles Wesley wrote it to commemorate his own conversion, and it has been traced to its origin, and all the surrounding circumstances have been verified."

On **"Hark, the Herald Angels Sing"**, Duffield establishes that it was written and published in 1739 and appeared in revised form in 1743. The transformation of the opening line from its original form to the present "Hark, the herald angels sing" is attributed to Martin Madan (or, as others suggest, to John Wesley himself) around 1760.

On **"Jesus, Lover of My Soul"**, Duffield records the famous tradition that Wesley wrote it after a bird pursued by a hawk flew through his open window: "The baffled hawk did not dare to follow, and the poet took his pen and wrote this immortal song." Duffield notes that "few hymns have been more extensively blessed in the dying hours of believers" — a testimony to its power in the sickroom and at deathbeds.

On **"Love Divine, All Love Excelling"**, Duffield gives the unusual biographical detail that Charles Wesley was inspired while waiting for a boat — "almost from a veritable 'hole of the pit' was dug that remarkable genius by whom God has been pleased to bless both England and America."

On **"A Charge to Keep I Have"** (Short Scripture Hymns, 1762, No. 188), Duffield notes it was based on Leviticus 8:35 — a fact proven by James Grant in the *Christian Standard* about 1872, after the hymn had been the subject of Calvinist-Arminian controversy. Wesley intended it to be sung to the tune "Olney."

Duffield observes that Charles's work was "mainly original," while John Wesley rendered some forty German hymns and "wrote very little otherwise" — a useful distinction in assessing the brothers' respective contributions.
