# Isaac Watts

By universal consent the title "Father of English Hymnody" belongs to Isaac Watts. He was the man who broke the iron rule of psalmody that had governed the Reformed Church since the days of Calvin, and in its place gave English-speaking Christians a treasury of original sacred song so rich that it has never been exhausted.

## Early Life

Watts was born in Southampton, England, on July 17, 1674. His father was a "dissenter" --- a Nonconformist who held convictions strong enough to land him in prison twice. The future poet's mother often carried him in her arms when she went to visit her husband in jail. It was a beginning that taught young Isaac early that faith sometimes costs something.

As a boy he showed an irresistible gift for rhyme. His parents were charmed and a little exasperated by his constant versifying. When he grew up, a wealthy man offered to send him to university --- if he would agree to enter the Established Church. Watts declined, and prepared instead for the Independent ministry.

## The Challenge That Started Everything

The turning point came when young Watts, not yet twenty, grew impatient with the "wretched paraphrases of the Psalms" then in use at his father's church in Southampton. Others felt the same way --- Samuel Wesley, father of the famous Wesley brothers, called the versified Psalms of Sternhold and Hopkins "scandalous doggerel." When Watts voiced his complaints, a church officer shot back: "Give us something better, young man."

He accepted the challenge. His first hymn, written at eighteen, was sung at the very next Sunday evening service. Its opening stanza reads almost like a prophecy of what was to come:

> Behold the glories of the Lamb
>   Amidst His Father's throne;
> Prepare new honors for His Name,
>   And songs before unknown.

The congregation received it so warmly that Watts kept writing. Within two years he had produced nearly all the 210 hymns that would appear in his landmark collection, *Hymns and Spiritual Songs*, published in 1707 --- the first real hymn-book in the English language.

## The Great Works

Twelve years later Watts published his *Psalms of David*, a metrical version of the Psalter rendered, as he put it, "in the language of the New Testament, and applied to the Christian state and worship." The result was something extraordinary: the old Hebrew psalms were given so thoroughly Christian a dress that their Old Testament origin is often forgotten. His rendition of the seventy-second Psalm begins:

> Jesus shall reign where'er the sun
> Does his successive journeys run.

Among the immortal hymns Watts gave to the Church:

- **"When I survey the wondrous cross"** --- named by Matthew Arnold as the finest hymn in the English language, and ranked by hymnologist John Julian among the four greatest English hymns
- **"Joy to the world, the Lord is come!"** --- his paraphrase of Psalm 98, sung at virtually every Christmas service in the world
- **"O God, our help in ages past"** --- based on Psalm 90, indispensable at New Year's
- **"Before Jehovah's awful throne"** --- a majestic hymn of worship
- **"Alas, and did my Saviour bleed?"** --- a tender Lenten hymn
- **"Am I a soldier of the cross?"** --- a challenging call to faithful living
- **"There is a land of pure delight"**
- **"Come, Holy Spirit, Heavenly Dove"**

## The Long Guest

Always frail in health, Watts eventually wore himself out with intense study and was forced to give up his parish. He was invited to spend a week as a guest in the home of Sir Thomas Abney, an intimate friend. That week stretched into thirty-six years. The Abney family simply refused to let him go, and so the great hymnist lived out the rest of his days in their household. He died on November 25, 1748, and was buried at Bunhill Fields, London, near the graves of John Bunyan and Daniel Defoe. A monument was later placed in Westminster Abbey --- the highest honor England can bestow.

## The Children's Poet

One of the more endearing surprises in Watts' legacy is how deeply he loved children. The same pen that wrote "O God, our help in ages past" also gave us nursery rhymes like "How doth the little busy bee" and "Let dogs delight to bark and bite." He wrote a beautiful cradle-song:

> Hush, my dear, lie still and slumber;
>   Holy angels guard thy bed;
> Heavenly blessings without number
>   Gently falling on thy head.

## Watts and Wesley

Whether [[Charles_Wesley]] or Isaac Watts should hold first place among English hymnists has long been debated. The question may be unanswerable, but Dr. Breed's comparison is memorable: *"Watts is more reverential; Wesley more loving. Watts is stronger; Wesley sweeter. Watts appeals profoundly to the intellect; Wesley takes hold of the heart. Watts will continue to sing for the Pauls and Peters of the Church; Wesley for the Thomases and the Johns."*

The two complement each other perfectly. Watts writes from the mountaintop, contemplating the awesome majesty of God; Wesley writes from the valley, reaching up to embrace a Saviour. The Church needs both voices.

## Legacy

Two centuries after his death, hymns by Watts are sung every Sunday in churches around the world. His work changed the nature of Christian worship in the English-speaking world permanently and irrevocably. Before Watts, Reformed congregations sang the Psalms --- and little else. After Watts, the imagination of the Church was set free to sing new songs.

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

Butterworth-Brown supply distinctive musical details for three of Watts's best-known hymns.

For "Jesus Shall Reign Where'er the Sun" — which they call "one of Watts' sublimest hymns, this Hebrew ode to the final King" expanding the prophecy of Psalm 72 — they offer a remarkable missionary vignette: in 1862, one of the newly converted native kings in the South Sea Islands substituted a Christian for a pagan constitution, and five thousand of his subjects joined as with one voice in singing this hymn at the ceremonial. The appropriate tune, they suggest, is "Samson" from Handel's Oratorio, which appears in the *Evangelical Hymnal* — "Handel had no peer in the art or instinct of making a note speak a word."

For "Joy to the World!" (written 1719), they note that "Antioch" — the universal musical companion — is an adaptation from Handel's *Messiah*, an arrangement credited to Lowell Mason though "he seems to have taken it from an English collection by Clark of Canterbury." The tune's staying power they attribute to a kind of congregational gravity: despite its fugue, it "contrives to enlist the entire voice of a congregation, the bass falling in on the third beat as if by intuition. The truth is, the tune has become the habit of the hymn, and to the thousands who have it by heart, as they do in every village where there is a singing school, 'Antioch' is 'Joy to the World,' and 'Joy to the World' is 'Antioch.'"

For "Why Do We Mourn Departed Friends?" they record that the hymn was long paired with Timothy Swan's tune "China," which Lowell Mason called "a queer medley of melody" — composed by Swan at about age twenty-six (1784) and published in 1801 in the *New England Harmony*. It "may have sounded consolatory to mature mourners … but its wild and thrilling chords made children weep." Swan was a self-taught hatter from Northfield, Massachusetts, whose three-week "course of study" in a country singing school was his only formal training. "China" they judge a curio that "will be long kept track of as a curio, and preserved in replicates of old psalmody to illustrate self-culture in the art of song."

## Hymnological Context (Duffield)

Duffield (1886) gives Isaac Watts 133 entries — more than any other hymn-writer in the volume — placing him emphatically as the dominant figure in English hymnody. Duffield's entries provide the most comprehensive contemporary survey of Watts's hymn-by-hymn literary history available in the 19th century.

On **"When I Survey the Wondrous Cross"** (1709, based on Galatians 6:14), Duffield calls it "Watts's very finest hymn." He records that in George Eliot's *Adam Bede* there is a description — "reputed to be from life" — of Dinah Morris (whose real name was Evans) singing this hymn in a way that deeply moved onlookers. He passes over some anecdotes as "incongruous and unfit," but repeats Dr. John Hall's story of a Scotsman who sang it "most piously" while apparently having no personal relationship with its truth — a poignant illustration of the hymn's power over even the unregenerate conscience.

On **"Our God, Our Help in Ages Past"** (a version of Psalm 90), Duffield locates it precisely in Watts's Psalter and notes that Philip Doddridge famously reported to Watts that when a version of this Psalm was sung to "plain country folk in a large barn," the precentor "could scarcely utter the words, and many of the audience were in tears."

On **"Jesus Shall Reign Where'er the Sun"** (Psalm 72, Second Part, L.M., "Christ's Kingdom among the Gentiles"), Duffield records the remarkable missionary use: in 1862, five thousand natives of Tonga, Fiji, and Samoa sang it together when exchanging heathenism for Christianity. This is one of the great missionary anecdotes of Victorian hymnody.

On **"Alas! and Did My Saviour Bleed"**, Duffield gives it precise placement and notes its extraordinary effectiveness across denominational lines.

On **"Joy to the World"** (Second Part, C.M., "The Messiah's Coming and Kingdom"), Duffield confirms it has four stanzas and is specifically a Psalm paraphrase (Psalm 98), not an original composition — a useful corrective to those who treat it only as a Christmas carol.

Duffield's admiration for Watts is consistent and unqualified. He treats Watts as the standard against whom other hymn-writers are implicitly measured — the foundation on which all of English congregational song was built.
