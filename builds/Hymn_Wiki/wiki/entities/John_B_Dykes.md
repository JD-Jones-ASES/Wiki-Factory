# John Bacchus Dykes

John Bacchus Dykes was among the most gifted and prolific composers of congregational hymn tunes in the English tradition. His melodies are distinguished by harmonic richness, emotional warmth, and a gift for expressing the exact mood of the texts they accompany.

## Sailors' Hymns

Dykes's most famous tune for sailors is **"Melita"** --- composed in 1861 for "Eternal Father, Strong to Save," William Whiting's great nautical prayer. The name "Melita" is a tribute to the island of St. Paul's shipwreck, and the tune's strong, easy chords and moderate note range are described as "nobly suited to the devout hymn." The hymn is sung almost universally on English ships.

For Godfrey Thring's "The Ocean Hath No Danger," Dykes composed the tune **"Morlaix"** --- "simple, but a very sweet and appropriate harmony."

For Thring's other sailors' hymn, "Fierce Raged the Tempest on the Deep," Dykes composed **"St. Aelred,"** which includes an impressive *rallentando* of dotted semibreves to the refrain "Peace, be still," after the more rapid notes of the three-line stanzas.

## Other Notable Tunes

For Kipling's "God of Our Fathers, Known of Old" (the Recessional), Dykes composed "Magdalen" --- "a grave and earnest melody in D flat, with two bars in unison at 'Lord God of Hosts, be with us yet,' making the utterance of the prayer a deep and powerful finale."

For Faber's "Hark! Hark, My Soul!" Dykes composed **"Vox Angelica"** in B flat --- "a noble composition for choir or congregation."

For John Cawood's Christmas hymn "Hark! What Mean Those Holy Voices?" Dykes composed **"Oswald."**

For Sir John Bowring's funeral hymn "I Cannot Always Trace the Way," his **"Almsgiving"** has been used, though a tune by Howard M. Dow is considered more flexible.

For John Newton's New Year hymn "While with Ceaseless Course the Sun," his **"St. Edmund"** is sung in England, though Webbe's "Benevento" tends to be preferred in America.

## Two Masters of Sailors' Hymnody

The story of "Hark! Hark, My Soul!" brings together Dykes and Henry Smart in an illuminating comparison. Both masters wrote tunes for Faber's great pilgrimage hymn: Dykes's "Vox Angelica" and Smart's "Pilgrim." Some books print both on adjacent pages as optional selections. The authors suggest Smart's "Pilgrim" may have the more sympathetic melody, though "Vox Angelica" is the more frequently reprinted.

## Legacy

Dykes represents the best of Victorian Anglican hymn-tune composition: musically sophisticated, emotionally genuine, and spiritually suited to the texts. His tunes do not merely accompany hymns --- they interpret them. The music of "Melita" does not merely serve "Eternal Father"; it makes the prayer audible as prayer.

## Musical and Thematic Context

Butterworth-Brown single Dykes out as having made a decisive intellectual contribution to the art of hymn-tune writing: he was "perhaps the first to demonstrate that hymn-tune making can be reduced to a science without impairing its spiritual purpose." This insight shaped the great mid-Victorian flowering of English congregational music to which *Hymns Ancient and Modern* stands as monument.

His tune "Nicæa" for [[Reginald_Heber]]'s "Holy, Holy, Holy" is described by Butterworth-Brown as the supreme illustration of what congregational praise can be: Heber's "grand hymn did not come to its full grandeur of sentiment and sound in song-worship till the remarkable music of Dr. John B. Dykes was joined to it. None was ever written that in performance illustrates more admirably the solemn beauty of congregational praise." The name "Nicæa" means little to popular ears; the piece is everywhere known by the opening line.

Butterworth-Brown also cite Dykes's tune "Rivaulx" for [[Gerhard_Tersteegen]]'s "God Calling Yet" and "Keble" for Oliver Wendell Holmes's "Lord of All Being, Throned Afar" as examples of his characteristic gift for matching mood to text with technical precision.
