# Samuel Holyoke

Samuel Holyoke was born in 1762 and died in 1820. A member of the Essex (Massachusetts) Musical Association, he was one of the most prolific sacred music compilers of the early American republic.

## The Columbian Repository

His most important work was *The Columbian Repository of Sacred Harmony* (c. 1802) — the largest collection of sacred music gathered in America to that date. Published in Exeter, New Hampshire, by Henry Ranlet, it contained 472 pages and 734 tunes, dedicated to the Essex Musical Association. It was sold by subscription at three dollars per copy. Copyright records confirm entry date of April 7, 1802, resolving a century of conjecture about the publication date.

The *Columbian Repository* adapted every tune in Watts's Psalms and Hymns, plus tunes for the meters in Tate and Brady's and Dr. Belknap's collections. In the advertisement, Holyoke claimed it offered "a greater variety of style to be found than in any work of the kind yet appeared in the United States."

## Earlier Works

*Harmonia Americana* (1791) — 200 pages published by Thomas and Andrews in Boston — was his first collection. Its preface contained a principled rejection of fuguing tunes: "the parts, falling in one after another, each conveying a different idea, confuse the sense, and render the performance a mere jargon of words." This put Holyoke at odds with the Billings tradition but aligned him with the reforming spirit that would later characterize [[Lowell_Mason]].

He co-edited *The Massachusetts Compiler* (Boston, 1795, 72 pages) together with Hans Gram (organist of Brattle Street Church, Boston) and [[Oliver_Holden]]. Gram wrote the preface, and Holden, living in Charlestown, is thought to have done the larger part of the compiling.

*The Christian Harmonist* (Salem, 1804) — 195 pages — contained tunes adapted to Dr. Rippon's selection, Joshua Smith's collection, and Watts's psalms and hymns. He also composed music for the public funeral observance of Washington, performed at Newburyport on January 2, 1800. (Metcalf, p. 115-119)
