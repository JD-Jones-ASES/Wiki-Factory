# John Fawcett

John Fawcett, D.D., is always quoted as the example of his own most famous words, for he sacrificed ambition and personal interest to Christian affection — and then wrote the hymn about it.

He was born near Bradford, Yorkshire, on January 6, 1739, and converted under the preaching of Whitefield. He joined the Methodists but afterwards became a member of the new Baptist church in Bradford. Seven years later he was ordained over the Baptist Society at Wainsgate. In 1772 he received a call to succeed the celebrated Dr. Gill in London, and accepted.

But at the last moment, when his goods were packed for removal, the clinging love of his people — weeping their farewells around him — melted his heart. Their passionate regrets were more than either he or his good wife could withstand.

"I will stay," he said. "You may unpack my goods, and we will live for the Lord lovingly together."

It was out of this heart experience that the tender hymn was born:

> Our fears, our hopes, our aims are one,
>   Our comforts and our cares.

Dr. Fawcett died July 25, 1817.

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Burrage's account of Fawcett is notably detailed — Fawcett is one of only five people illustrated with a portrait in the entire book, and he has the honor of the frontispiece. Burrage confirms the birth-date ambiguity: old style January 6, 1739 = new style January 17, 1740. He also locates the birth at Lidget Green, near Bradford, not merely "near Bradford, Yorkshire" as usually given.

Fawcett's conversion under Whitefield's preaching at Bradford was from a text Fawcett never forgot: John 3:14, "And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of man be lifted up." He wrote: "As long as life remains I shall remember both the text and the sermon."

The detail about the London call is amplified by Burrage quoting Dr. Belcher: "He preached his farewell sermon... loaded six or seven wagons with his furniture, books, etc.... men, women, and children clung around him and his family in perfect agony of soul. The last wagon was being loaded, when the good man and his wife sat down on the packing cases to weep." His wife said, "'Oh John, John, I cannot bear this! I know not how to go!' 'Nor I, either,' said the good man: 'nor will we go; unload the wagons.'" A letter was sent to London declining the call; Fawcett continued on a salary of less than two hundred dollars a year.

Among Fawcett's hymns still found in best collections: *Religion is the chief concern*, *How precious is the book divine*, *Thy way, O God, is in the sea*, *Thus far my God hath led me on*, *With humble heart and tongue*. The degree of Doctor of Divinity was conferred by Brown University in 1811.

Burrage also notes that in 1793, after Dr. Caleb Evans' death, Fawcett was invited to succeed him as President of the Baptist Academy at Bristol — a second prestigious call he declined, preferring to remain with his congregation.

## Musical and Thematic Context

Butterworth-Brown describe "Blest Be the Tie That Binds" as "perhaps the best hymn-expression of sacred brotherhood," noting it "has had, and still has the endorsement of constant use." The tunes "Boylston" by [[Lowell_Mason]] and "Dennis," adapted by Mason from Johann Georg Nägeli, are the two most familiar settings. "Dennis" is described as "a soft and smoothly modulated harmony" that carries the deep feeling of sacred brotherhood with all its notes in sympathy.
