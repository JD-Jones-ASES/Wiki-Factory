# Theodulph of Orleans

Theodulph of Orleans wrote one of the most beloved hymns in the Christian tradition --- the great Palm Sunday processional "All glory, laud, and honor" --- while sitting in a prison cell.

## Imprisonment and Song

The details of Theodulph's imprisonment are not fully clear from Ryden's account, but the essential fact is striking: this hymn of triumphant praise, sung every Palm Sunday as congregations reenact Christ's entry into Jerusalem, was composed by a man who was himself a prisoner. It was probably written around 821 AD.

There is a beautiful irony in the contrast: the hymn celebrates a king entering his city in triumph, welcomed with palm branches and hosannas, while its author sat in confinement, stripped of his freedom and his episcopal dignity. The song transcends the circumstances of its composition. It was written in chains, but it has the sound of jubilee.

The Latin original begins *Gloria, laus et honor tibi sit*, and has been translated and sung in various versions across many centuries and traditions. The familiar English translation most widely used today was made by [[John_Mason_Neale]].

## Place in the Medieval Tradition

Theodulph was a leading scholar in the court of Charlemagne --- a key figure in the Carolingian Renaissance, the brief cultural flowering of the early ninth century. His imprisonment came later in life, after Charlemagne's death, during a period of political instability.

He belongs to the period that Ryden identifies as the beginning of the [[Golden_Age_of_Latin_Hymnody]]: the late eighth and early ninth centuries when the Carolingian church was producing some of the finest Latin sacred verse since Prudentius. His contemporary Rhabanus Maurus wrote the *Veni, Creator Spiritus* around this same era.

## Significance

The Palm Sunday processional is one of the fixed points of the Christian year, and Theodulph's hymn has been the song of that moment for twelve centuries. It is one of those hymns so perfectly fitted to its liturgical occasion that it is almost impossible to imagine the occasion without it.
