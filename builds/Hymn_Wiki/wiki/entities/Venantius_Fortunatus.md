# Venantius Fortunatus

Venantius Fortunatus wrote some of the most beautiful hymns about the Cross that the Christian Church has ever known. He lived in the twilight of the great Ambrosian era of Latin singing, and his hymns have the quality of a glorious sunset --- brilliant, warm, and shot through with a light that outlasted the age that produced them.

## A Pilgrimage That Changed Everything

Fortunatus was born around 530 AD at Ceneda, near Treviso in northern Italy, and converted to Christianity as a young man. While studying at Ravenna, he suffered a severe eye condition that nearly left him blind. He recovered his sight --- an event he experienced as a miracle --- and in gratitude made a pilgrimage to the shrine of St. Martin at Tours in Gaul (modern France). That journey changed the course of his life.

He never returned home. He spent the rest of his years in Gaul, eventually becoming Bishop of Poitiers, the same see that had once been held by [[Hilary_of_Poitiers]], the first Latin hymn writer. In this he was, symbolically and geographically, both heir and fulfillment of that earlier tradition.

## The Singer of the Cross

Fortunatus wrote on many subjects, but nothing he wrote surpasses his hymns about the Cross. Ryden says of them that "no one has ever sung of the Cross with such deep pathos and sublime tenderness." That is high praise, and it is deserved. His two great Passion hymns --- *Vexilla Regis prodeunt* ("The royal banners forward go") and *Pange lingua gloriosi* ("Sing, my tongue, the glorious battle") --- have been sung in Holy Week liturgies for fourteen centuries.

The first of these contains the famous lines:

> Faithful Cross! above all other,
> One and only noble tree!
> None in foliage, none in blossom,
> None in fruit thy peer may be;
> Sweetest wood and sweetest iron!
> Sweetest weight is hung on thee.

There is something startling about calling iron "sweet" and a Roman instrument of execution "noble" --- but that is exactly the paradox of the Cross, and Fortunatus captures it with a poet's instinct for the right word in the right place.

His second great Passion hymn, *Pange lingua*, also gave rise to one of the Easter hymns that survives in modern use:

> Praise the Saviour, now and ever!
> Praise Him all beneath the skies!
> Prostrate lying, suffering, dying,
> On the Cross, a Sacrifice;
> Victory gaining, life obtaining,
> Now in glory He doth rise.

## The Easter Singer

Fortunatus was not only a poet of the Cross; he was also a poet of the Resurrection. His Easter hymn, "Welcome, happy morning! age to age shall say," rings with the confidence of Easter triumph. Its opening stanza has been sung on Easter Day in many traditions for generations:

> Welcome, happy morning! age to age shall say,
> Hell today is vanquished, heaven is won today.
> Lo, the Dead is living, God for evermore!
> Him, their true Creator, all His works adore.

He also wrote hymns for Ascension Day and Whitsunday (Pentecost), showing a poet who was at home across the entire sweep of the Christian year.

## The End of an Era

Fortunatus was one of the last voices of the [[Ambrosian hymnody]] tradition that had flourished since the fourth century. Around the time of his death, Pope [[Gregory_the_Great]] was completing his reform of church music, replacing the lively congregational style of Ambrosian singing with the more austere Gregorian chant. The era of the people's hymn was giving way to the era of the trained choir.

In this light, Fortunatus is a poignant figure --- the last great songwriter of an age that was ending, who left behind hymns so beautiful that they refused to die with the tradition that produced them. Many of his finest hymns survived through the medieval period in liturgical use and were eventually translated by [[John_Mason_Neale]] and others in the nineteenth century for use in English-speaking Protestant churches.

## Significance

Ryden describes Fortunatus as "the most important Latin poet of the sixth century" and "one of the last hymnists of the Ambrosian school." Both descriptions are accurate, and both hint at his importance. He represents the culmination of one great era of Latin hymnody, and his hymns about the Cross and Resurrection are among the enduring masterpieces of Christian sacred poetry in any language.
