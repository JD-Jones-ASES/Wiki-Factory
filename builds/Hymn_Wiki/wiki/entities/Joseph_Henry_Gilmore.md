# Joseph Henry Gilmore

Joseph Henry Gilmore was born in 1834 and became a professor at the University of Rochester. He was also an ordained Baptist minister.

## He Leadeth Me

The story of "He Leadeth Me, O Blessed Thought" is one of the most charming in hymn history. Butterworth-Brown record the details: Gilmore had been preaching a Wednesday-evening service at the First Baptist Church in Philadelphia, speaking on the Twenty-Third Psalm. After the service, a group of friends gathered in the parlor of Deacon Watson, and the conversation continued. In that parlor, impressed by the thought that God's guidance was itself sufficient comfort — that the *mode* of leading mattered less than the fact of being led — Gilmore "jotted down" the words of the hymn.

He gave no further thought to them. But his wife, who saw something in the lines her husband had dismissed, sent them to the *Watchman and Reflector* without telling him.

Years later, Gilmore went to Rochester, New York, to candidate at the Second Baptist Church — a pulpit he hoped to fill. He opened the hymnal in the pew and found "He Leadeth Me" already there, already in use. He had no idea it had ever been published.

> He leadeth me! O blessèd thought!
> O words with heavenly comfort fraught!
> Whate'er I do, where'er I be,
> Still 'tis God's hand that leadeth me.

## Musical and Thematic Context

The tune for "He Leadeth Me" was composed by [[Robert_Lowry]], who set it with the refrain that has made it one of the most recognizable hymns in the American tradition. Lowry's music gave the hymn its staying power, adding a chorus that drives home the devotional point with memorable simplicity.

Gilmore also taught for many years as a professor of logic and rhetoric, and his ability to distill a complex theological truth into simple devotional language owes something to that discipline. "He leadeth me" is not a theological argument — it is a statement of trust, and Gilmore found the words for it in a quiet parlor on a weeknight, without any sense that he was writing a hymn at all.

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Burrage supplies the institutional detail of Gilmore's career that earlier accounts condense. Born in Boston on April 29, 1834, he was the son of a future governor of New Hampshire. He graduated from Phillips Academy in Andover, then entered Brown University in 1854, graduating with the highest honors of his class in 1858. He commenced theological study at Newton Theological Institution in the same year and graduated in 1861, spending the following year as an instructor in Hebrew there. He was ordained June 19, 1862 and took the pastorate of the Baptist church in Fisherville, New Hampshire.

In 1863-64, while his father served as governor of New Hampshire, Gilmore was his private secretary and also edited the Concord Daily Monitor. In 1865 he accepted the call to Second Baptist Church, Rochester, New York — the very church where he would later discover his own hymn in the pew. In 1867 he served as acting professor of Hebrew at Rochester Theological Seminary, and from January 1, 1868, occupied the professorship of logic, rhetoric, and English literature at the University of Rochester.

Burrage prints Gilmore's own account of the hymn's composition, more complete than most retellings: he had spoken at the Wednesday evening lecture of First Baptist Church, Philadelphia, about the Twenty-third Psalm, "especially impressed with the blessedness of being led by God, of the mere fact of his leadership altogether apart from the way in which he led us." After the service, at Deacon Thomas Wattson's home, the conversation continued. "During the conversation... the blessedness of God's leadership so grew upon me that I took out my pencil, wrote the hymn just as it stands to-day, handed it to my wife, and thought no more of it." His wife sent it, without his knowledge, to the Watchman and Reflector. When he went to Rochester and opened the hymnal in the pew — and found it there — "that was the first time I knew my hymn had found a place among the songs of the church."

Burrage also adds that [[William_Bradbury]] probably found the text in the Watchman and Reflector columns, and composed for it "the very appropriate music with which Professor Gilmore's words have ever since been associated." Burrage quotes Rev. S. Dryden Phelps noting that "He Leadeth Me" was a general favorite in the McAll Mission in Paris, and provides the opening of its Swedish refrain ("Han leder mig! Han leder mig!") as evidence of the hymn's international reach.
