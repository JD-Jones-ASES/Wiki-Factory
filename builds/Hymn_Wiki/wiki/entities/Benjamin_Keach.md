# Benjamin Keach

Benjamin Keach did something quietly revolutionary in English church history: he introduced hymn-singing to English Baptist worship, against fierce resistance, and lived to see it accepted by his congregation. Without that foothold, the entire tradition of Baptist hymnody in England — including everything that followed from [[John_Rippon]]'s great collection onward — would have been impossible.

## Life and Persecution

Keach was born at Stokehaman, Buckinghamshire, on February 29, 1640. He was converted at fifteen and united with a neighboring Baptist church, beginning to preach three years later.

In 1664, he published *The Child's Instructor, or a New and Easy Primer* — a religious primer for children. For this he was indicted at the Aylesbury Assizes before Lord Chief Justice Hyde (afterward Lord Clarendon), who directed the jury to find him guilty. The sentence was humiliating: two weeks in prison; two separate public appearances in the pillory at Aylesbury and Winslow; the burning of his book by the common hangman; a fine of twenty pounds; and a demand that he renounce his doctrines publicly.

"I hope," said Keach to his lordship, "I shall never renounce the truth which I have written in that book." That part of the sentence was quietly dropped.

In 1668, Keach accepted the pastorate of a small Particular Baptist church meeting in a private house in Tooley Street, London. After the Declaration of Indulgence (1672), a meeting-house was built at the corner of Goat Street, Horsley-down, Southwark. His congregations grew so large that the building had to be enlarged repeatedly.

## The Introduction of Hymn-Singing

Up to this point, English Baptists were opposed to singing as part of worship. The controversy was genuine and deep-rooted — there were serious theological objections about whether congregations should sing composed human poetry in the presence of God.

Keach began cautiously. With his congregation's consent, he introduced the practice of singing a hymn at the Lord's Supper. Then hymns on Thanksgiving Days. Finally, about 1690, the church voted — with only a few dissenting — to sing a hymn every Lord's Day after the sermon, adding the generous provision that those who opposed this could "go freely forth."

In 1691, Keach published a full defense of the practice: *The Breach Repaired in God's Worship, or Singing of Psalms, Hymns, and Spiritual Songs proved to be a holy ordinance of Jesus Christ.*

He also published, that same year, a collection of hymns entitled *Scriptural Melody*, containing nearly three hundred hymns. According to Burrage, none of these are now in use — Keach's hymns did not outlast their generation. But his act of introducing the practice did.

## His Writings

Keach was a prolific author. His two most popular works were *Tropologia, or a Key to Open Scripture Metaphors* and *Gospel Mysteries Unveiled, or an Exposition of the Parables*. He also published *Zion in Distress, or the Groans of the Protestant Church* (1666) and *Distressed Zion Relieved, or the Garment of Praise for the Spirit of Heaviness*.

He died in 1704.

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Burrage treats Keach as the pivotal figure in the introduction of Baptist hymn-singing, preceding his full account of the great hymn writers who followed. The chain is direct: Keach establishes the practice at his Southwark church; the tradition develops through the eighteenth century; [[John_Rippon]] compiles the first great Baptist hymnal in 1787; and by the time [[Charles_Spurgeon]] takes the pulpit of what had been Rippon's own congregation at New Park Street in 1854, the singing of hymns is the heart of Baptist corporate worship.

Burrage notes that the hymn

*The Lord, he is our sun and shield, / Our buckler and safeguard*

(from Psalm 84) is representative of Keach's style — solid, doctrinally grounded, but not particularly musical by the standards of what came after. His lasting contribution was institutional rather than aesthetic: he opened the door.

## The Singing Controversy (Benson)

Benson's account in *The English Hymn* (1915) adds essential institutional detail to Keach's story, situating him precisely within the denominational conflict over hymn-singing that divided English Baptists for a generation.

According to Benson, Keach's congregation was composed of Particular Baptists — the Calvinist wing of English Baptist life, in contrast to the General Baptists who rejected not just hymns but often any form of congregational singing at all. When Keach introduced hymn-singing at the Lord's Supper around 1673, he was moving against the strong instinct of his own tradition. His church's practice was "promiscuous singing" — all members singing together — which the most conservative element regarded as spiritually dangerous, mixing the voices of the regenerate and unregenerate in a common act of worship.

A significant minority of Keach's own congregation dissented throughout his ministry. Isaac Marlow, one of the dissenting members, led a sustained theological opposition through a series of publications — *Hints of the Fatal Tendency of Unguided Singing* and others — to which Keach responded with equal energy. The controversy spread beyond Keach's church. Benson traces how it agitated the entire Particular Baptist community for over a decade, and how Keach's patient pastoral approach — introducing the practice gradually, tolerating dissenters, insisting only on the rightness of the thing itself — eventually won the argument within his own congregation.

By 1691, when Keach published *The Breach Repaired in God's Worship* — his formal theological defense — the practice had been adopted not only by his Southwark congregation but was spreading through other Particular Baptist churches. Benson regards Keach's perseverance through this controversy as decisive for the future of Baptist hymnody: had the conservative faction prevailed, it is possible that English Baptists might have remained, like the General Baptists, a denomination without congregational singing.
