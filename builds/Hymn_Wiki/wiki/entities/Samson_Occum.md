# Samson Occum

Samson Occum was born in Mohegan, New London County, Connecticut, probably in 1722. Converted from paganism in 1740 (possibly under the preaching of Whitefield, who was in New England at that time), he desired to become a missionary to his people and entered the school of Eleazer Wheelock. After four years of study, he began to teach and preach among the Montauk Indians, and in 1759 the Presbytery of Suffolk County, Long Island, ordained him to the Christian ministry — the first Native American to receive ordination in America.

## His Service to Dartmouth College

Butterworth-Brown give Occum full credit for a remarkable act of institutional sacrifice. The Indian school at Lebanon, Connecticut — "Moore's Indian Charity School," which had educated him — was chronically underfunded. In 1765 Occum crossed the Atlantic to raise funds, remaining four years in England and Scotland, lecturing and preaching nearly four hundred sermons. He raised ten thousand pounds. The donation was placed under a Board of Trustees chaired by Lord Dartmouth, and when the school was moved to Hanover, New Hampshire, it was named "Dartmouth" — while, as Butterworth-Brown note with dry precision, "the names of Occum, Moore and Wheelock retired into the encyclopedias."

Occum died in 1779 while laboring among the Stockbridge Indians of New York.

## Awaked by Sinai's Awful Sound

This revival hymn, set to the "stentorian tune of 'Ganges'" (attributed to S. Chandler, circa 1790), was described by Butterworth-Brown as "a tremendous sermon in itself to old-time congregations." Its stark Calvinist theology — the sinner confronting God's law, trembling, finding no relief until the grace of Christ intervenes — represents the preaching tradition in which Occum was formed:

> Awaked by Sinai's awful sound,
> My soul in bonds of guilt I found,
>   And knew not where to go.

The rugged original has been "so often and so variously altered and 'toned down'" that only accurate aged memories can recall it in its original form. It was sung in revival meetings throughout New England but went gradually out of use from about 1860 onward.

## Musical and Thematic Context

Occum also wrote "An Account of the Customs and Manners of the Montauks" — an ethnographic document as well as a missionary's witness. His hymn stands as one of the earliest surviving examples of Native American Christian verse, and its fierce doctrinal simplicity is inseparable from the tradition that formed its author: a tradition in which conversion was a matter of cosmic consequence, and the law and grace of God were not abstract ideas but life-and-death realities.
