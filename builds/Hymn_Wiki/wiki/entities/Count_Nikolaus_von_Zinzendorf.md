# Count Nikolaus von Zinzendorf

## The Nobleman Who Gave Everything

Nikolaus Ludwig, Count von Zinzendorf, is remembered for two things that are rarely found together in the same person: a world-changing commitment to foreign missions, and an extraordinary outpouring of hymns. Both grew from the same root --- a passionate, personal love for Jesus Christ that he first articulated as a ten-year-old boy and never abandoned.

His is also the story of Herrnhut, the remarkable community he sheltered on his estates that became one of the most influential missionary centers in the history of Christianity.

## Biographical Sketch

Zinzendorf was born in 1700 into the Saxon aristocracy. As a boy of ten he was sent to study at the famous Pietist school of August Hermann Francke in Halle (see [[Pietism]]). Even there, among children, his missionary zeal found expression: he organized his classmates into a society called "The Order of the Grain of Mustard Seed" to pray for the world's conversion.

A few years later, viewing a copy of Sternberg's famous painting "Ecce Homo" --- Christ wearing His crown of thorns --- he read the inscription beneath it: *"This have I done for thee; What hast thou done for Me?"* From that moment he took as his life motto: "I have but one passion, and that is He and only He."

On his wedding day in 1722, he and his young bride resolved to renounce their rank and dedicate their lives entirely to winning souls for Christ.

## Herrnhut

That same year, a band of Protestant refugees from Moravia arrived at Zinzendorf's large estates in Saxony, seeking refuge from Roman Catholic persecution. They were remnants of the Bohemian Brethren, the heroic evangelical body that had traced its spiritual lineage back to the martyred Jan Huss and had survived three centuries of persecution as "the hidden seed."

Zinzendorf welcomed them. They established a colony they called "Herrnhut" (literally "The Lord's Watch"). As word spread that religious freedom existed on Zinzendorf's estates, people from across Europe poured in. It quickly became a flourishing community.

Though Zinzendorf was himself a Lutheran, he helped the Moravians draft a statement of faith based on the Augsburg Confession while allowing them to maintain their distinct identity. In 1737 he accepted ordination as a bishop of the Brethren, becoming their formal leader.

## Missions

Zinzendorf immediately turned his characteristic fire toward foreign missions. Two of the earliest Moravian missionaries, David Nitschmann and Leonard Dober, sailed to the island of St. Thomas in the West Indies to preach the Gospel to enslaved Africans. When the slaves refused to listen, Dober made the extraordinary decision to sell himself into slavery and share their condition. He died as a result of this sacrifice --- but his story moved the heart of Prime Minister Wilberforce of England and helped ignite the movement that eventually abolished slavery throughout the British Empire.

The Moravian Church at Herrnhut became one of the most remarkable missionary centers in Christian history. By the time William Carey sailed to India, the Moravians already had 165 missionaries in the pagan world. Today, despite being a small denomination, the Moravians maintain a missionary ratio of approximately one foreign missionary for every 58 members at home.

## Hymns

Zinzendorf began writing hymns as a twelve-year-old and continued until his death in 1760. He is credited with authorship of more than 2,000 lyrics --- an astonishing output, though not all are of equal quality. His most famous hymn is **"Jesus, Still Lead On"** (also known as **"Jesus, Lead the Way"**), written in 1721:

> *Jesus, still lead on,*
> *Till our rest be won,*
> *And although the way be cheerless,*
> *We will follow, calm and fearless.*
> *Guide us by Thy hand*
> *To our Fatherland!*

The hymn is a pilgrimage song, perfectly suited to a community that regarded itself as having no permanent home in this world and ready at any moment to be sent anywhere.

John Wesley, who was greatly influenced by Zinzendorf and the Moravians (they traveled on the same ship to America and it was Moravian singing during a storm that first impressed Wesley with their faith), translated another of his hymns: **"Jesus, Thy Blood and Righteousness."**

The celebrated English hymn-writer James Montgomery was himself a Moravian.

The Moravian hymnody that Zinzendorf inspired was rich, voluminous, and not without excess (see [[Moravian_Hymnody]]). At its best, however, it expressed an intimacy with Christ that was electrifying and that deeply influenced the Wesleyan revival.

## Significance

Zinzendorf was an unusual figure: an aristocrat who gave up his wealth and status for the Gospel, a Lutheran who became the bishop of a different church body, a visionary who inspired the modern Protestant missionary movement. His [[Moravian_Hymnody|Moravian hymn]] tradition was enormously influential, reaching John Wesley in England and spawning hymnists like James Montgomery.

He also deeply valued the earlier German tradition: he included no fewer than 79 hymns by the Catholic convert and mystic Johann Scheffler (Angelus Silesius) in his Moravian collection, and his own hymns echo the warm, personal devotion of [[Paul_Gerhardt]].

The colony of Herrnhut that he sheltered still exists. The Moravian Church, though small, has never lost the missionary fire he kindled.
