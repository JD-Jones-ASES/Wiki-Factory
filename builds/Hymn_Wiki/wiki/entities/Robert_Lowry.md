# Robert Lowry

Robert Lowry was an American Baptist minister and composer who became one of the most prolific and influential hymn-tune writers of the nineteenth century. He was closely associated with the revival song tradition and produced some of its most durable melodies.

## Major Tunes

Lowry composed the tune for [[Joseph_Henry_Gilmore]]'s "He Leadeth Me" — the music that transformed a jotted-down meditation into one of the best-loved hymns in the American tradition.

He also composed the tune for [[Annie_Sherwood_Hawks]]'s "I Need Thee Every Hour" (1872), adding the chorus that greatly enhanced the hymn's effectiveness. The hymn was first sung at a National Baptist Sunday School Association meeting in Cincinnati in 1872. Butterworth-Brown note that "one instance, at least, of a hymn made doubly impressive by its chorus will be attested by all who have sung or heard the pleading words and music of Mrs. Hawks' and Dr. Lowry's 'I need Thee, Oh, I need Thee.'"

Lowry also set [[S_Dryden_Phelps]]'s "Saviour, Thy Dying Love" — admiring the hymn and giving it a tune "perfectly suited to its metre and spirit." It has never been sung to any other music. The usual title of the tune is "Something for Jesus."

## Musical and Thematic Context

Lowry represents the tradition of the pastor-composer: a man of genuine literary and theological formation who nonetheless served popular, congregational music with warmth and skill. His tunes are not sophisticated in the academic sense, but they are profoundly suited to the devotional character of the words they carry. He understood that the purpose of a revival tune is not aesthetic sophistication but spiritual accessibility.

He collaborated with William Howard Doane on numerous publications, and the hymns he set became fixtures of the late-nineteenth-century Sunday school and prayer-meeting repertoire.

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Burrage gives a thorough account of Lowry's pastoral career, which is more extensive than usually noted in hymn histories. Born in Philadelphia on March 12, 1826, Lowry came to Baptist convictions through independent Bible study, even though his parents were Associate Presbyterians. He was baptized by Dr. George B. Ide and joined First Baptist Church, Philadelphia. Encouraged by Dr. Ide to enter ministry, he attended Lewisburg (now Bucknell) University, graduating with valedictory honors in 1854. That same year he was ordained and began five years at First Baptist, West Chester, Pennsylvania.

His subsequent pastorates trace the arc of a distinguished career: Bloomingdale Baptist Church, New York (1858); Hanson Place Baptist Church, Brooklyn (1861); then from 1869 a combined professorship of belles-lettres at his alma mater and pastorate of Lewisburg Baptist Church. In 1875 he settled at Plainfield, New Jersey, where he organized and served Park Avenue Baptist Church for nine years. The D.D. was conferred by Lewisburg University.

It was at the Hanson Place Baptist Church in Brooklyn, during an oppressively hot July afternoon in 1864, that "Shall We Gather at the River" was composed. Burrage preserves Lowry's own account: lying exhausted on a lounge, barely capable of physical movement, his mind wandered to the imagery of Revelation — the throne, the heavenly river, the gathering saints. He wondered why hymnists had said so much about "the river of death" and so little about "the pure river of water of life, clear as crystal." The words came first as a question — "Shall we gather?" — then broke into the chorus as an answer of faith: "Yes, we'll gather." Words and music arrived together; he could never say which came first. He called them twins. He sprang up, sat at his organ, played it through, and wrote it out. When the Brooklyn Sunday School Union used it the following spring, forty thousand children sang it on parade.

Burrage recounts one of the hymn's most memorable incidents: during the military campaign in Egypt, a British soldier lay dying of fever in an Alexandria hospital. A visiting lady sat by him, laid her hand on his burning brow, and sang "Shall We Gather at the River." Other sick men raised their heads to listen. When she finished, they asked her to sing it again — and turning to the dying soldier she asked, "Will you be there?" "Yes," he said faintly, "I shall be there soon" — and shortly after, he was gone.

On the death of [[William_Bradbury]], the music publishing business Bradbury had built was continued by Biglow and Main, who engaged Lowry to prepare Sunday school books. He was at first reluctant, fearing interference with his ministerial duties, but eventually entered this work and continued it for the rest of his life. His music books include "Bright Jewels" (1869, half a million copies in four years), "Pure Gold" (1871, over a million copies sold), and many others. Among his other hymns: "Shall We Know Each Other There," "One More Day's Work for Jesus," "Weeping Will Not Save Me," and "Where Is My Boy Tonight."

## Methodist Hymnal Context (Nutter)

Nutter and Tillett describe Lowry as "a well-known American Baptist minister" for whom "music was his avocation." He served as musical editor of several successful books for use in Sunday schools. Three tunes by Lowry appear in the Methodist Hymnal: Something for Jesus, One More Day's Work, and I Need Thee Every Hour. Nutter's brief but respectful entry acknowledges his pastoral career alongside his musical work, noting his role in both composing and editing.
