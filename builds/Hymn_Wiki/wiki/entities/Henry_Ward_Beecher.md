# Henry Ward Beecher

Henry Ward Beecher was the most celebrated American preacher of his age, and his contribution to American hymnody was not the hymns he wrote — he wrote very few — but the transformation he effected in how Americans sang in church. Before Beecher, congregational singing in most American churches was a choir's performance while the congregation listened. After the Plymouth Collection of 1855, the ideal became something quite different: the whole congregation, thousands of voices, singing together.

## The Man and His Ministry

Beecher was born in 1813, the son of the great Calvinist preacher Lyman Beecher and brother of Harriet Beecher Stowe. He accepted the pastorate of Plymouth Congregational Church in Brooklyn in 1847, and it became one of the most famous pulpits in America — indeed, in Benson's telling, one of the most famous in the English-speaking world. Beecher's preaching attracted enormous crowds; people came from across New York and beyond to hear him. His theology moved steadily in a liberal, broadly evangelical direction, increasingly influenced by the Romantic Movement.

He was also a brilliant showman with a genuine love of popular culture, and this shaped his approach to church music.

## The Plymouth Collection (1855)

In 1855 Beecher published *The Plymouth Collection of Hymns and Tunes for the Use of Christian Congregations* — a landmark in American hymnodic history. It was massive: 1,374 hymns, with tunes, designed to be placed in the hands of every member of the congregation. The idea of a combined hymns-and-tunes book for congregational use was novel at the time; Beecher's was one of the first American churches to adopt it.

The Collection was not a work of scholarship. Beecher gathered hymns from every denomination without much care for textual accuracy or attribution. The ruling motive was homiletical — he wanted hymns that could "excite pious emotions" — rather than literary or liturgical. But the effect was extraordinary.

Benson describes the result: Plymouth Church became famous for its congregational singing. "The hearty singing of the vast congregation became almost as much of an attraction as his preaching." The fame spread, encouraging countless congregations to emulate it, and carrying the Collection itself into Baptist, Presbyterian, and Congregational churches far beyond Brooklyn.

## Significance: The New Church Hymnal

The Plymouth Collection marks two transitions of lasting importance in American church music.

First, it represented the shift from choir-dominated to congregation-dominated worship. Before Beecher, in most American churches, the music was the choir's domain; the congregation, without tunes before them, participated minimally. Beecher's conviction that the people should sing — and his provision of the musical tools to make that possible — transformed the model.

Second, the Plymouth Collection established what Benson calls "the new type of church hymnal" — the combined words-and-music book, arranged for congregational use rather than choir performance, designed to give everyone the means to participate. This model became standard in American churches for the rest of the century.

## Shortcomings Noted by Benson

Benson's account, while acknowledging Beecher's transformative impact, is not uncritical. The size of the collection (1,374 hymns was far too many for any congregation to know) reflected the prevailing homiletical conception — the minister wanted an index of hymns on every possible sermon topic, not a compact treasury of great hymns. The format, in which tunes were printed at the top of pages with multiple hymns to be sung to them, was a poor model that many later compilers copied without improving upon.

The Plymouth Collection's motive was "homiletical rather than liturgical in either a larger or narrower sense" — it was a minister's tool for sermon illustration rather than the congregation's act of devotion. This distinction, which Benson traces as central to the whole history of American hymnody, remained a limitation of the tradition Beecher established.

## Influence Beyond Plymouth

The success of the Plymouth Collection — its eventual adoption in hundreds of Congregational, Baptist, and Presbyterian churches — made Beecher's model of congregational singing the American standard for a generation. He did not write great hymns; he created the conditions in which great hymn-singing became normal.

Together with the *Sabbath Hymn Book* (1858) of the Andover professors Park and Phelps — which brought scholarly rigor to what Beecher had pioneered in popular enthusiasm — the Plymouth Collection marks what Benson calls "the transition from the hesitations and limitations of the compromise era of 'Psalms and Hymns' to the free and catholic-hearted use of available resources that characterizes modern Hymnody."
