# Heinrich Christopher Zeuner

Heinrich Christopher Zeuner was born in Eisleben, Saxony, on September 20, 1795. He came to the United States in 1827 and became organist at Park Street Church, Boston, and for the Handel and Haydn Society — two of the most prestigious musical posts in American church life at that time. In 1854 he moved to Philadelphia, where he served as organist at St. Andrew's Church and Arch Street Presbyterian. He became insane in 1857 and died by his own hand in November of that year.

## The Missionary Chant

Butterworth-Brown are emphatic: the tune for "Ye Christian Heralds, Go Proclaim" is "the 'Missionary Chant,' and no other." Zeuner's composition is described as "remarkably spirited and vigorous," and his work as a tune-maker was "much in demand during his life, and is sure to continue, in its best examples, as long as good sacred music is appreciated."

The "Missionary Chant" has a bold, forward-moving energy — appropriate for a hymn that Butterworth-Brown describe as "one of the most stimulating in Missionary song-literature." The combination of Zeuner's march-like tune and the text's call to global advance gave the piece its unique character in early nineteenth-century missionary meetings.

## Musical Context (Metcalf)

Metcalf's *American Writers and Compilers of Sacred Music* (1925) devotes a full chapter to Zeuner, drawn from the *Musical Cyclopedia* of John W. Moore and other sources.

Zeuner was born September 20, 1795, in Eisleben, Saxony — the same town where Martin Luther was born in 1483. He was baptized Heinrich Christopher Zeuner; no record explains why, on coming to America (around 1824), he adopted the name Charles. Before leaving Germany he had been a court musician. After settling in Boston he was regarded as one of the best-educated organists in the country. He served as president of the Handel and Haydn Society in 1838-39, but his temper made it impossible to keep harmony among the singers and he was asked to resign. His temperament was volatile: when a churchgoer in Philadelphia complained that his inspired fugal improvisation sounded like a malfunctioning organ, he "strode from his interrogator and never went near that stately church again either professionally or otherwise."

The year 1832 marked the climax of his work. *The American Harp* appeared that year — an almost entirely original composition, departing from the era's practice of reprinting older tunes. Both "Missionary Chant" and "Hummel" (named for his early teacher) appeared in this volume. The same year he contributed three pieces to [[Lowell_Mason]]'s *Lyra Sacra* and some pieces for N.D. Gould's *National Church Harmony*.

When Mason asked Zeuner why "Missionary Chant" was so popular, the composer answered: "I was sitting on one of these seats on Boston Common on a most beautiful moonlight evening, all alone, with all the world moving about me, and suddenly 'Missionary Chant' was given me. I ran home as fast as ever I could and put it on paper before I should forget. That is what makes it please." (Metcalf, p. 220-225)

His oratorio "The Feast of Tabernacles" was presented at the Odeon by the Boston Academy for eight evenings, but the result was a financial failure. Incensed, Zeuner broke into the Academy, tore up and burned all the manuscript and printed score he could find. One copy survived at the Library of Congress.

His second major collection, *The Ancient Lyre* (copyrighted 1836), went through at least twenty editions. Only "Missionary Chant" and "Hummel" survived into common use from his large output.

## Published Works

Zeuner published an oratorio, "The Feast of Tabernacles," and two popular books: *The American Harp* (1832) and *The Ancient Lyre* (1836). These collections helped establish standards for American church music in a formative period. The tragic end of his life — mental collapse and self-destruction — does not diminish the vigor and originality of his contributions to American sacred music.
