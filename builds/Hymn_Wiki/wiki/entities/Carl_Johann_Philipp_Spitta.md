# Carl Johann Philipp Spitta

## The 19th-Century Voice of German Evangelical Hymnody

Carl Johann Philipp Spitta was the greatest German hymn-writer of the nineteenth century --- the heir of [[Paul_Gerhardt]]'s warm personal piety and the voice of the spiritual renaissance that swept Germany after the Napoleonic wars.

## Biographical Sketch

Spitta was born August 1, 1801, in Hannover. His father, descended from a Huguenot family that had fled France during the Catholic persecutions, died when Carl was only four. His mother --- a Christian Jewess --- raised him, and Spitta's most famous hymn, "O Happy Home," is widely understood as a tribute to the home she created for him.

He was a frail child. His mother hoped he would enter the ministry, but his health seemed to rule it out, and a younger brother was sent to school in his place. The brother died, and now Carl was given his chance. He completed his theological studies in 1824, taught school for four years in Lüne, and was ordained to the Lutheran ministry in 1828.

During his university years he formed a close friendship with Heinrich Heine, the brilliant poet and prose writer. The friendship ended sharply when Heine visited Spitta at Lüne and scoffed at holy things in front of Spitta's students. Not long after, Spitta passed through a deep spiritual experience that transformed his work. He wrote to a friend in 1826: "In the manner in which I formerly sang, I sing no more. To the Lord I dedicate my life, my love, and likewise my song. He gave to me song and melody. I give it back to Him."

## The "Psalter und Harfe"

Spitta's hymn collection "Psalter und Harfe" (Psalter and Harp) was first published in 1833. A second, larger edition followed the next year. Then a new edition appeared every year: by 1889, the book had gone through fifty-five editions. A second collection published in 1843 had passed through forty-two editions by 1887. No German hymn collection of the nineteenth century approached this popularity.

The book also spread internationally. A large number of Spitta's hymns were translated into English and found their way into American and British hymnals.

## His Hymns

Spitta's child-like faith and fervent love for the Saviour are visible in every hymn. His most famous is **"O Happy Home"** (*O selig Haus*), a meditation on the Christian family as a dwelling place of Christ:

> *O happy home, where Thou art loved the dearest,*
> *Thou loving Friend and Saviour of our race,*
> *And where among the guests there never cometh*
> *One who can hold such high and honored place!*

> *O happy home, whose little ones are given*
> *Early to Thee in humble faith and prayer,*
> *To Thee, their Friend, who from the heights of heaven*
> *Guides them, and guards with more than mother's care.*

Other well-known hymns include:
- **"I Know No Life Divided"** --- a declaration of union with Christ
- **"O Come, Eternal Spirit"**
- **"By the Holy Hills Surrounded"**
- **"I Place Myself in Jesus' Hands"**
- **"We Are the Lord's: His All-Sufficient Merit"**
- **"How Blessed from the Bonds of Sin"**

## Significance

Spitta stands at the crest of the German spiritual renaissance that Ryden describes as the reaction to the desiccating influence of Rationalism. He restored to German hymnody the personal warmth and evangelical directness of [[Paul_Gerhardt]] while giving it a distinctly nineteenth-century lyrical elegance. He died suddenly at his desk on September 28, 1859 --- a quiet and fitting end for a man whose life had been largely a song.
