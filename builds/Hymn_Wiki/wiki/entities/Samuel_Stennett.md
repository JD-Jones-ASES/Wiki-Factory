# Samuel Stennett

Rev. Samuel Stennett D.D. came of a remarkable ministerial dynasty. He was the son of Rev. Joseph Stennett, and grandson of Rev. Joseph Stennett D.D., who wrote "Another six days' work is done, / Another Sabbath is begun." All three were Baptist ministers. Samuel was born in 1727 at Exeter, England. At the age of twenty-one he became his father's assistant and subsequently his successor over the church in Little Wild Street, Lincoln's Inn Fields, London.

## A Life of Influence

Samuel Stennett was one of the most respected and influential ministers of the Dissenting persuasion, and a confidant of many of the most distinguished statesmen of his time. The celebrated prison reformer John Howard was his parishioner and intimate friend. His degree of Doctor of Divinity was bestowed upon him by Aberdeen University.

Besides his theological writings, he composed and published thirty-eight hymns, among them:

- "On Jordan's stormy banks I stand"
- "When two or three with sweet accord"
- "Here at Thy table, Lord, we meet"
- "'Tis finished, so the Saviour cried"

He died in London on August 24, 1795.

## Majestic Sweetness Sits Enthroned

Stennett's most celebrated hymn began as the third stanza of his longer hymn "To Christ the Lord let every tongue." In its independent form it became one of the standard hymns of Christian praise:

> Majestic sweetness sits enthroned
>   Upon the Saviour's brow;
> His head with radiant glories crowned,
>   His lips with grace o'erflow.

> To Him I owe my life and breath
>   And all the joys I have;
> He makes me triumph over death,
>   He saves me from the grave.

> Since from His bounty I receive
>   Such proofs of love divine,
> Had I a thousand hearts to give,
>   Lord, they should all be Thine.

## Musical and Thematic Context

For fifty or sixty years the tune "Ortonville" by [[Thomas_Hastings]] was linked with this hymn and maintained its fitting fellowship. The tune was composed in 1830 and is, as Butterworth-Brown note, "almost as well-known and as often sung as his immortal 'Toplady.'" The natural bond of Stennett's warm, adoring words and Hastings' graceful melody made them a lasting pair in American and British hymnals alike.

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Burrage establishes Samuel Stennett's place within one of the most distinguished Baptist ministerial dynasties in England. He was not merely the grandson but the great-grandson of Rev. Edward Stennett, making him fourth-generation Dissenting ministry. The family line runs: Edward Stennett → Joseph Stennett (the hymnist of "Another six days' work is done") → Joseph Stennett D.D. → Samuel. Samuel was born in 1727 at Exeter, where his father was pastor, but at age ten moved to London when his father accepted a call to Little Wild Street, Lincoln's Inn Fields. His education was thorough: first under Rev. John Hubbard at Stepney, then under the celebrated linguist Dr. John Walker at the Academy at Mile End.

Burrage quotes a tribute from Rippon's Register that captures Stennett's intellectual standing: "He was formed by nature and grace for the distinguished figure he afterward made. To the strength of natural faculties, vigor of imagination, and acuteness of judgment, of which he was possessed, he had added, from his earliest years, so close an attention to reflection and study that there was scarcely a topic in science or literature, in religion or even politics, but he seemed to have investigated."

He was ordained successor to his father at Little Wild Street on June 1, 1758, and served there for the rest of his life. When in 1767 he received a call from the Sabbatarian Baptist church (of which his grandfather had been pastor), he did not accept the position — but as a gesture of family loyalty he preached for that congregation every Saturday morning for twenty years.

Burrage notes that thirty-nine of Stennett's hymns appear in [[John_Rippon]]'s Selection (1787) — including "Majestic Sweetness Sits Enthroned" as the lead hymn. His scholarly publications included several works defending Baptist principles against infant baptism and immersion controversies. King's College, Aberdeen conferred the D.D. on him in 1763. He also enjoyed the personal friendship of King George III., and — like his grandfather — could have held high position in the Church of England had he been willing to abandon his Nonconformist principles.

The most touching detail Burrage preserves is John Howard's letter from Smyrna (August 11, 1786). Howard, the great prison reformer who worshipped regularly at Little Wild Street, wrote to Stennett: "No man ever entered more into my religious sentiments, or more happily expressed them... Oh, sir, how many Sabbaths I ardently long to spend in Little Wild Street... I bless God for your ministry. I pray God reward you a thousand-fold."

At his death in August 1795, Burrage records that "the death of his wife greatly afflicted him, and seemed to deaden him to the world. He appeared to have no further desire to live in it. Just before he was confined to his bed, he prayed earnestly in his family that God might give him an easy passage out of life; and God granted him that which he requested." He was buried in Bunhill Fields — the great Nonconformist burial ground where [[John_Bunyan]], Isaac Watts, and other luminaries of English Dissent also rest.
