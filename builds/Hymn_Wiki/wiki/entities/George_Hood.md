# George Hood

George Hood wore many hats: music teacher, music historian, Presbyterian minister, and composer. Of these, it is the historian who has proven most durable.

## Life

Hood was born on February 10, 1807, in Topsfield, Massachusetts --- just a few days older than Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, as Metcalf noted with some pleasure. He devoted his early career to music, teaching first in public schools and then in a female seminary. He later entered the Presbyterian ministry and served several churches until his death on September 24, 1882.

## The History

In 1846, when he was not yet forty, Hood published *A History of Music in New England* --- a work of 259 pages that represented ten years of research. It was a pioneering effort: almost nothing had been written before it on the subject. He gathered information about early American psalmody, the tune books, the composers, and the singing schools that had shaped New England worship. Metcalf, who built on Hood's work a generation later, acknowledged it as an essential foundation.

At the end of his book, Hood described all the music books available to him --- a list drawn from his own library and supplemented by Lowell Mason's large collection.

## Musical Productions

Hood also composed a modest amount of music. In the same year his history appeared, he published *The Southern Melodist*, intended for use in the South, with shaped notes and figured bass; it contained two tunes under his own name and two by his brother Jacob. In 1864 he published a *Musical Manual* as an instruction book.

His lasting contribution was not the music he composed but the history he preserved --- making it possible for later researchers to trace the arc of American sacred song from the Puritan singing schools to the great revival era.
