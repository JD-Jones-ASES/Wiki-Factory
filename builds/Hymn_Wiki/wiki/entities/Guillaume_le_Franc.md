# Guillaume le Franc

Guillaume le Franc — William Franck or William the Frenchman — was born in Rouen, France. The authorship of "Old Hundred," the most universally sung tune in all Christian worship, has been contested: some have claimed it for Luther, others for Louis Bourgeois (editor of the French Genevan Psalter), but Butterworth-Brown write that "the weight of evidence seems to indicate" that le Franc was the composer.

Le Franc founded a music school in Geneva in 1541 and served there as Chapel Master. He subsequently removed to Lausanne, where he played in the Catholic choir and wrote the tunes for an edition of Marot's and Beza's Psalms. He died in Lausanne in 1570.

## Old Hundred

"Old Hundred" is the great Gregorian harmony that has been the vehicle of Christian praise across every denomination and language. Butterworth-Brown describe it as the supreme representative of the Puritan or Genevan school of church music — the counterpart to "Tallis' Evening Hymn" from the Anglican school. Whereas the Anglican tradition preserved something of the ornate warmth of Italian psalmody, the Genevan tradition stripped sacred music to its most austere and universal voice.

The two traditions, Butterworth-Brown note, "were gradually reconciled, and together made the model after which the best sacred tunes are built."

## Musical and Thematic Context

"Old Hundred" is so thoroughly identified with [[Thomas_Ken]]'s great doxology "Praise God from whom all blessings flow" that the pairing feels ancient and inevitable. Butterworth-Brown also note that it has served as the tune for Charles Wesley's and Isaac Watts's doxologies and for Martin Madan's florid anthem "Denmark." But beyond any single text, "Old Hundred" represents the spirit of congregational praise at its most austere and universal — the voice of the people gathered simply to declare the glory of God.
