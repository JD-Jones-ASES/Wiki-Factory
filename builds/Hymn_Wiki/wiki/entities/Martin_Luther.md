# Martin Luther

## The Man Who Gave the Church Its Voice

Martin Luther was not only the father of the Protestant Reformation --- he was the father of evangelical congregational hymnody. Before Luther, the people in the pew sat in silence while trained clergy sang the liturgy in Latin. After Luther, the whole congregation sang. That single change altered the sound of Christian worship forever.

Philip Schaff called Luther "the Ambrose of German hymnody," crediting him with giving the German people "the Bible, the Catechism, and the hymn book, so that God might speak directly to them in His word, and that they might directly answer Him in their songs."

## Biographical Sketch

Luther was born in 1483 and grew up with an extraordinary love for music. As a student in Magdeburg and Eisenach he sang for alms in the streets --- it was his sweet voice that first attracted the attention of the benevolent Ursula Cotta, who gave him shelter during his school years. The flute and the lute were his favorite instruments, and he played the lute to accompany his own singing.

His close friend and collaborator John Walther remembered: "I spent many a delightful hour with him in singing; and ofttimes I have seen the dear man wax so happy and merry in heart over the singing that it is well-nigh impossible to weary or content him therewithal."

Luther had no patience with the iconoclasts who wanted to strip music from worship. He wrote: "I am not of the opinion that all sciences should be beaten down and made to cease by the Gospel... I would fain see all the arts, and music in particular, used in the service of Him who hath given and created them." He went further: "If any man despises music, as all fanatics do, for him I have no liking; for music is a gift and grace of God, not an invention of men. Thus it drives out the devil and makes people cheerful."

## The Spark That Lit the Fire

The moment that unlocked Luther's own gift for hymn-writing was tragic. In 1523, two young Augustinian monks --- Heinrich Voes and Johannes Esch --- were burned at the stake in Brussels for refusing to recant their Lutheran faith. As the flames rose around them, witnesses heard them singing the ancient hymn "Lord God, we praise thee."

When news reached Luther, the poetic gift in his soul burst into flame. He wrote a ballad commemorating the first Lutheran martyrs, beginning with the words "Ein neues Lied wir heben an" (A new song we lift up). His lyre was tuned.

## The First Lutheran Hymnal

In 1524 Luther published the "Achtliederbuch" --- the first Protestant hymnal --- containing just eight hymns: four by Luther, three by [[Paul_Speratus]], and one likely by Justus Jonas. The little books flew across Europe. Luther's enemies lamented that "the whole people are singing themselves into his doctrines." A second volume, the "Erfurt Enchiridion," appeared the same year with twenty-five hymns, eighteen of them Luther's. The nightingale of Wittenberg had begun to sing.

By the time of Luther's death, more than sixty collections of hymns had been published.

## "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God"

Of Luther's thirty-six hymns, none has achieved the fame of "Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott" --- "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God." Heinrich Heine called it "the Marseillaise of the Reformation." Frederick the Great called it "God Almighty's grenadier march." It has been translated into nearly every language on earth, with some eighty English versions alone.

The date of composition is uncertain --- it cannot have been written later than around 1529, as it appeared in a hymn-book before Luther allegedly sang it at the Diet of Augsburg in 1530. Luther wrote both the words and the magnificent chorale melody. The combination proved irresistible: Mendelssohn used the theme in his Reformation Symphony, Meyerbeer wove it into "Les Huguenots," and Bach built a beautiful cantata around it.

Stories of the hymn in battle are vivid. When Gustavus Adolphus faced Tilly's Catholic army at Leipzig on September 7, 1631, he led his troops in singing "Ein feste Burg" before charging --- and won the day. When Melanchthon fled Wittenberg after Luther's death, pursued by Spanish armies, he heard a girl singing the hymn as he entered Weimar. "Sing on, my child," he exclaimed, "thou little knowest how thy song cheers our hearts."

Even in Africa, a missionary heard 2,000 native believers sing it, and his native interpreter remarked of Luther: "That man must have been a powerful man --- one can feel it in his hymns."

## Luther's Broader Hymn Output

Most of Luther's hymns are not wholly original --- many are paraphrases of the Psalms or recastings of older Latin, Greek, and German hymns. But he imbued every one with his fervent faith and militant spirit, making them shine with a new luster.

His principal hymns still in use include:

- **"A Mighty Fortress Is Our God"** --- the battle hymn of the Reformation
- **"Come, Thou Saviour of Our Race"** --- based on the ancient Latin "Veni Redemptor gentium"
- **"Good News from Heaven the Angels Bring"** --- a Christmas carol
- **"In Death's Strong Grasp the Saviour Lay"** --- an Easter hymn on the resurrection
- **"Come, Holy Spirit, God and Lord"** --- a Pentecost hymn
- **"Lord, Keep Us Steadfast in Thy Word"** --- originally written in 1541 against the Turkish invasion (in its original form, the first stanza invoked God to "restrain the murderous pope and Turk")
- **"Dear Christians, One and All Rejoice"** --- a joyful proclamation of salvation
- **"Out of the Depths I Cry to Thee"** --- a paraphrase of Psalm 130
- **"We All Believe in One True God"** --- a creedal hymn

## Significance

Luther understood that music was not merely decorative --- it was theological. The act of a congregation singing the Gospel together was itself a proclamation of the priesthood of all believers. In restoring congregational song, he did more than change worship; he gave ordinary Christians a voice and a vocabulary for faith.

Thomas Carlyle, who admired Luther as "perhaps the most inspired of all teachers since the Apostles," described "Ein feste Burg" as something with "the sound of Alpine avalanches, or the first murmur of earthquakes, in the very vastness of which dissonance a higher unison is revealed to us."

The tradition Luther began --- robust, Scripture-drenched, congregationally sung hymnody --- is the foundation on which every figure in the [[Reformation_Hymnody]] story stands.

## Hymnological Context (Duffield)

Duffield (1886) devotes his most extensive single entry to "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God," providing a full biographical sketch of Luther's life alongside multiple translations and a remarkable collection of historical anecdotes about the hymn's use.

Duffield gives precise biographical dates: Luther born at Eisleben, November 10, 1483; died at the same place, February 17, 1546. He traces Luther's path from Erfurt University (entering 1501) through the decision to become an Augustinian monk after the death of a friend by lightning (July 17, 1505) through his ordination (1507), his visit to Rome (1511), the posting of the ninety-five theses (October 31, 1517), the burning of the papal bull (December 12, 1520), and the famous Diet of Worms declaration: "Here I stand, I cannot do otherwise."

On the hymn itself, Duffield notes that it may have been composed either on the road to Worms (1521) or later (1529), and that the first Wittenberg hymn-book (1524) contained four of Luther's hymns.

Duffield's anecdotes are remarkable. He records that in 1532 the hymn was sung at Schweinfurth in defiance of the Roman Catholic priest. After Wittenberg's capture in 1547, Melanchthon, fleeing to Weimar, heard a child singing it: "Sing, dear daughter, sing," said Melanchthon, "you know not what great people you are now comforting." The hymn was also sung by Gustavus Adolphus before the battle of Leipzig (1631) and before Lützen, where he died.

Duffield also describes a Moravian revival of 1720, where a congregation seized by police struck up the stanza "And were the world with devils filled" in defiance — twenty heads of families were arrested, including David Nitschmann, who later escaped to Herrnhut and accompanied the Wesleys on their 1735 expedition to Savannah, Georgia.

Luther himself, Duffield records, used the hymn as a personal resource when dangers thickened: "Come, Philip," he would say to Melanchthon, "let us sing the 46th Psalm" — and they would sing it in this "characteristic version."

Duffield also provides his own translation of the hymn (1873), noting it was made for use among German immigrants in the United States.

The rendering by F.H. Hedge (translated 1853, published in *Hymns for the Church*) Duffield identifies as the most popular English version alongside Carlyle's "A Safe Stronghold."
