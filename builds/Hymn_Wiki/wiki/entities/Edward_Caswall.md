# Edward Caswall

When the Church of England and the Church of Rome have agreed on anything in hymnody, it has often been the translations of Edward Caswall. An Anglican clergyman who became a Roman Catholic and then an Oratorian priest, Caswall devoted his life to rendering the Latin hymns of the medieval church into English verse — and his translations have found their way into Protestant hymnals as readily as Catholic ones.

## Life

Edward Caswall was born on July 15, 1814, at Yately, Hampshire, England. He was educated at Brasenose College, Oxford, where he graduated in 1836. He was ordained as a deacon in the Church of England in 1838 and as a priest in 1840. He served as perpetual curate of Stratford-sub-Castle, near Salisbury, until 1847.

In 1847 Caswall was received into the Roman Catholic Church. He was ordained a Catholic priest in 1852 and entered the Oratory of St. Philip Neri at Edgbaston, Birmingham — the community founded by John Henry Newman. He served there for the rest of his life, dying on January 7, 1878.

## The Translations

Caswall's principal work was the translation of Latin hymns, and his collections were gathered into *Lyra Catholica* (1849), *Masque of Mary* (1858), and his collected *Hymns and Poems* (1873).

His most celebrated translation is **"Jesus, the Very Thought of Thee"** — Caswall's rendering of St. Bernard of Clairvaux's *Jesu, dulcis memoria*. According to Duffield, this is "Caswall's almost unapproachably fine translation of St. Bernard's hymn," which ranks among the supreme achievements of English sacred verse. The companion hymn **"O Jesus! King Most Wonderful"** is drawn from the same original Latin poem.

**"When Morning Gilds the Skies"** is his translation of the German "Gelobt sei Jesus Christ" — a rendering that quickly became standard in both Catholic and Protestant use. Duffield notes that despite extensive research, the German original's source remains uncertain, making Caswall's English version more widely known than the original.

**"Holy Spirit! Lord of Light"** (1848, from *Lyra Catholica*) is Caswall's "best rendering" of the *Veni Creator*, according to Duffield, surpassing his other attempts at this most difficult of medieval liturgical texts.

**"Come, O Creator Spirit, Blest"** — another *Veni Creator* translation — reflects the rich tradition of rendering this Pentecost hymn that runs from the Reformation through the Victorian era.

**"The Sun Is Sinking Fast"** is notable for a different reason: despite extensive effort, no source for this hymn has been found. Biggs, in his *Annotations to Hymns, Ancient and Modern*, states that "every effort has been made to discover the original of this hymn, but in vain." Caswall may have composed it freely rather than translated it.

## The Caswall Contribution

What distinguishes Caswall's translations from those of his contemporaries is their combination of scholarly fidelity and poetic grace. He worked not from English intermediaries but from the Latin originals, often consulting multiple manuscript traditions. His conversion to Rome gave him access to Catholic liturgical materials that most Victorian translators lacked, while his Oxford education gave him the critical tools to assess them.

Duffield's treatment of Caswall in 1886 — covering 12 of his hymns with warm appreciation — is evidence that his work had already crossed the denominational divide. In a Protestant American handbook of English hymnody, Caswall stands alongside John Mason Neale as the preeminent translator of the medieval Latin tradition into English.

## Hymnological Context (Duffield)

Duffield (1886) treats 12 of Caswall's hymns, making him the most-covered Catholic translator in the volume. Duffield consistently praises the quality of Caswall's work while noting its specific textual history.

He notes that **"Days and Moments Quickly Flying"** combines material from two separate original hymns — one on the "Swiftness of Time" and another a "Warning" — both by Caswall, dating from 1858, drawn from the *Masque of Mary*.

For **"He Who Once in Righteous Vengeance"**, Duffield tracks the variant between the five stanzas in *Lyra Messianica* (1865) and the six-stanza version in *Annus Sanctus* (1885), noting that the fuller version aligns more closely with the original Latin.

For **"On the Fount of Life Eternal"** (from Cardinal Peter Damiani's *Ad perennis vitae fontem*), Duffield quotes his own earlier work on Latin hymnody, calling the original "the most melodious of all the medieval Church songs" — high praise from a scholar who had surveyed the entire field.
