# Ambrose of Milan

If you have ever sung a hymn in a Christian congregation, you owe something to Ambrose of Milan. He is the man who brought congregational singing to the Western Church --- who first insisted that the whole people of God, not just trained choirs or solo cantors, should lift their voices together in worship. For that reason alone, he deserves to be remembered with gratitude.

## An Unlikely Bishop

Ambrose's path to the bishopric reads like something out of a novel. His father was prefect of the Gauls, and Ambrose was trained for a career in government, not the church. By 374 AD he had been appointed Consular of Liguria and Aemilia --- a senior Roman official.

Then came the election crisis. The bishopric of Milan had fallen vacant, and the church was bitterly divided between orthodox Christians and Arians. To prevent a riot, Ambrose went to the church where the election was taking place to restore order. According to a story that became famous in the early church, a child's voice suddenly rang out in the assembly: "Ambrosius!" The crowd, taking this as a sign from God, immediately began calling for Ambrose to be their bishop.

There was just one problem: Ambrose had not yet been baptized. He protested, fled the city, and tried every means he could think of to avoid the appointment. He was eventually persuaded to return, was baptized, and was ordained bishop --- moving through all the degrees of holy orders in a single week.

It was the beginning of one of the most remarkable lives in Christian history.

## The Man Who Taught the Church to Sing

Once he became bishop, Ambrose threw himself into his new role with total commitment. He sold his possessions, devoted himself to the poor and the sick, and took the spiritual life of his congregation with complete seriousness.

One of his first great projects was the introduction of congregational singing. Before Ambrose, in the Western churches, worship music had been largely the domain of individuals or small groups of clerics. Ambrose changed all of that. He introduced a new kind of church music --- hymns with a rhythmical accent, rich modulation, and a musical flow that made a powerful emotional appeal, yet were simple enough that ordinary people could quickly learn and love them. He also introduced antiphonal (responsive) singing, where the congregation alternated phrases back and forth, keeping everyone actively engaged.

The results were extraordinary. Augustine, who later became Ambrose's most famous convert, described the effect of hearing Ambrose's congregation sing: "How mightily I was moved by the overwhelming tones of Thy Church, my God! Thy voices flooded my ears, Thy truth melted my heart, the sacred fires of worship were kindled in my soul, my tears flowed, and a foretaste of the joy of salvation was given me."

Ambrose himself seems to have been quietly pleased by the response: "They say that people are transported by the singing of my hymns," he wrote. "And I confess that it is true."

## The Siege of Milan

The most dramatic episode in Ambrose's musical ministry came during his standoff with Empress Justina, who favored the Arians and tried to force Ambrose to hand over the Milan basilica for their use. When soldiers surrounded the church to enforce the imperial command, Ambrose and his congregation simply stayed inside --- and sang. They sang through the night. The effect on the soldiers outside was so powerful that eventually the troops themselves began joining in the anthems. The empress abandoned her plan.

This story is more than a colorful anecdote. It illustrates something Ambrose understood deeply: the congregation's voice, raised in worship, is not a passive thing. It is a form of presence, of solidarity, of witness. A church that sings together cannot be easily dispersed.

## Ambrose and Augustine

Among the people drawn to Milan by the fame of Ambrose's preaching was a young professor of rhetoric named Augustine, who came in 384 AD --- skeptical, brilliant, and deeply restless. Augustine came at first for the eloquence; he stayed because the Word of God began to grip him. On Easter Sunday, 387 AD, Augustine received baptism from Ambrose's own hands. The two men reportedly recited the *Te Deum* together afterward, though Ryden notes this beautiful tradition is probably legend rather than history.

Their relationship stands as one of the great teacher-and-student stories in Christian history.

## The Defender of Orthodoxy

Like [[Ephrem_the_Syrian]] in the East, Ambrose in the West was deeply committed to the full divinity of Christ against the Arians. Many of his hymns center on the Trinity and the deity of Christ. He was also a man of extraordinary moral courage: when Emperor Theodosius ordered a massacre of civilians at Thessalonica, Ambrose met him at the church door and refused him admittance until he had done public penance. The emperor complied.

## His Hymns

At least twelve Latin hymns can be ascribed to Ambrose with certainty. His greatest hymn is *Veni, Redemptor gentium* ("Come, Thou Saviour of our race"), an Advent hymn that Martin Luther prized highly and was one of the first Latin hymns Luther translated into German. Another Advent hymn sometimes credited to Ambrose is "Now hail we our Redeemer."

The style he created --- simple, metrical, singable by ordinary people --- became known as "Ambrosian hymnody" and dominated the Western Church for three centuries, until [[Gregory_the_Great]] replaced it with the more austere Gregorian chant.

Ambrose died peacefully on Easter evening, 397 AD, a death so gentle it seemed an answer to his own prayer:

> Grant to life's day a calm unclouded ending,
> An eve untouched by shadows of decay,
> The brightness of a holy deathbed blending
> With dawning glories of the eternal day.

## Significance

Philip Schaff called Luther "the Ambrose of German hymnody" --- meaning that what Ambrose did for the Latin West in the fourth century, Luther would do for the German-speaking world in the sixteenth. The highest possible tribute. Ambrose invented the congregational hymn as we know it. Every church that sings together is, in some measure, his heir.

### From Lorenz (1938)

Lorenz treats Ambrose (340--397) in Chapter X as the pivotal figure in Latin hymnody. He had been a lawyer, not a product of the ecclesiastical system, bringing to his office "a freshness of insight and of resources." When besieged for days in his cathedral, the value of song in supporting his followers' spirits suggested its wider use. Lorenz credits Ambrose with three transformative contributions: he transformed the mere reading hymn confined to the clergy into a singing hymn for the congregation; he abandoned artificial classical meters for the simplest lyrical form --- four lines of four iambic measures, which became Long Meter; and he introduced the free use of rhymes. Lorenz calls Ambrose "not only a learned man of great ability" but also "a man of great piety and devotion" who sought to make worship "genuine and heartfelt as against the formalists." He notes that if Hilary of Poitiers is the grandfather of Western hymnody, Ambrose is its father. Despite opposition from the Roman See and Charlemagne's efforts to destroy Ambrosian hymns, the "Ambrosiani" still keep a small place in the Roman Breviary.
