# William Williams of Pantycelyn

If Wales has a hymn-writer who stands above all others, it is William Williams of Pantycelyn --- the man his people called "the Sweet Singer of Wales" and "the Watts of Wales." In an era when the mountain provinces were awakening to faith under the preaching of Howell Harris and Daniel Rowland, Williams gave that awakening its songs. He wrote nine hundred and sixteen hymns.

## Early Life and Calling

Williams was born in 1717 at Cefncoed Farm, near Llandovery, in South Wales. Three years younger than Harris, the great revival preacher, and educated only at a village school and the academy at Llwynllwyd, Williams was the song protagonist of the holy campaign while Harris was its champion preacher. The partnership was providential: one man's words kindled the crowd; the other's melodies carried those words in every Welsh heart for generations.

He made his home at Pantycelyn after his marriage, and that farmstead name clung to his memory so closely that even today he is never simply "William Williams" but always "Williams of Pantycelyn."

## The Hymn-Writer of the Revival

From first to last Williams wrote nine hundred and sixteen hymns. Some are still heard throughout the church militant; others survive in local use and affection. He published his first collection under the fitting title *The Hallelujah* --- all in Welsh, all full of adoration for the Redeemer.

His was the genius to take the popular airs of the Welsh streets and markets and set sacred words to them. Like Luther and like Charles Wesley among the Cornwall sea-robbers, he caught up the country ballad-refrains, and his wife --- who had a voice so full and melodious that the people she and her husband visited were often more moved by her hymnody than by his exhortations --- sang his words to those familiar tunes. Many of these old Welsh airs were minor-mode melodies, but they had the swing that caught a Welsh multitude.

One evening the two put up at Bridgend Tavern in Llangefin, Anglesea, and a mischievous crowd, wishing to plague the "Methodists," sent a fiddler to ask if the guests would "have a tune." Williams cheerfully agreed --- "Nancy Jig or anything else" --- and the moment the fiddler started, Mrs. Williams struck in with one of her husband's Welsh hymns in the same metre, singing the sacred words in her sweet, clear voice, note for note after the player, until he stopped ashamed and took himself off with all his gang.

## His Greatest Hymn

Among all his songs, the one most universally sung across the world is a Welsh hymn now known in English as:

> Guide me, O Thou Great Jehovah,
> *Arglwydd arwain trwy'r anialoch.*

This hymn --- with its imagery of pilgrimage, the fiery cloudy pillar, bread of heaven, and crossing Jordan's flood --- became the anthem of Welsh Christianity and has since passed into hymnbooks of every English-speaking denomination. In the great revival of 1904--5, when ten percent of the entire Welsh population entered the church in one breathtaking harvest season, this was among the most cherished and oftenest chosen hymns.

## His Other Hymns

Williams also published two books of English hymns, *Hosanna* (1759) and *Gloria* (1772). Among his Welsh hymns that were translated into English are hymns of atonement, of surrender, of covenant joy, and of yearning for cleansing. One Welsh hymn antedates by many years the famous advice Dr. Malan gave to Charlotte Elliott --- "Come just as you are":

> Take me as I am, O Saviour,
>   Better I can never be;
> Thou alone canst bring me nearer,
>   Self but draws me far from Thee.

Another recalls Bunyan's Pilgrim in sight of the Cross:

> I'll cast my heavy burden down,
>   Remembering Jesus' pains;
> Guilt high as towering mountain tops
>   Here turns to joyful strains.

## Death and Legacy

William Williams died on January 11, 1791, at Pantycelyn. He had poured out the songs of the Welsh church for half a century, filling its revival meetings and prayer-gatherings with melodies that turned minor-mode lament into paradoxical joy. He fills so large a space in the hymnology and religious history of Wales that Butterworth and Brown wrote of him with wonder: "The province soon became a land of hymns."

In Calvinistic Wales, during the great revival of 1904--5, the most cherished hymns were those of William Williams and [[Ann_Griffiths]], of Charles Wesley, and Isaac Watts --- but it was Williams who gave Wales its own voice.
