# Joachim Neander

## The Paul Gerhardt of the Calvinists

Joachim Neander holds a unique place in German hymnody: he was the first great hymn-writer produced by the Reformed, or Calvinist, branch of the Protestant Church. Where [[Paul_Gerhardt]] had done for Lutheran hymnody, Neander did --- in a much shorter life --- for the Reformed tradition. He died at thirty years of age, yet his influence on the subsequent hymnody of his Church earned him the title "the Gerhardt of the Reformed Church."

His most famous hymn, "Praise to the Lord, the Almighty, the King of Creation," has become one of the great *Te Deums* of all Christendom.

## Background: Why the Reformed Church Lagged Behind

It is worth understanding why the Reformed Church had almost no hymnody of its own for more than 150 years after the Reformation.

Calvin and Zwingli were deeply suspicious of human creativity in worship. Calvin held that God could be worthily praised only by divinely inspired words --- meaning the Psalms of the Old Testament. Hymns composed by human beings, however beautiful, were in Calvin's view improper for public worship. This gave rise to the practice of *psalmody* --- versifying the Psalms in metrical form --- as distinct from *hymnody*, the Lutheran practice of original congregational songs.

The great Genevan Psalter (1562), begun by Clement Marot and completed by Theodore de Beza, became the standard hymn-book of Reformed churches across Europe. Its musical editor, Louis Bourgeois, composed the melody now universally known as the "Old Hundredth." In Germany, a translation by Ambrosius Lobwasser became the Reformed standard.

For more than 150 years, Lutheran hymn-writers poured out a great river of song --- while the Reformed Church largely maintained its Psalm-singing discipline. Then came Neander, who broke the dam.

## Biographical Sketch

Neander was born in Bremen, Germany, in 1650, the latest in a remarkable family of pastors: his father, grandfather, great-grandfather, and great-great-grandfather had all been clergymen and all borne the name Joachim Neander.

As a young student at the Academic Gymnasium of Bremen he led, by his own later account, a careless and profligate life. Then, in 1670, at age twenty, he chanced to attend St. Martin's Church in Bremen, accompanying two other students who planned to scoff at the sermon. They had not reckoned with the preaching of the new pastor, Theodore Under-Eyck. The burning words caught Neander off guard. He who went to scoff came away to pray. Under-Eyck became his spiritual mentor and lifelong friend, and guided the young man to faith in Christ.

## Years of Service and Trouble

The following year Neander became tutor to five young students, accompanying them to Heidelberg. In 1674 he became rector of the Latin school in Düsseldorf, under a Reformed pastor named Sylvester Luersen. For a time the two men worked well together. But Neander came under the influence of separatist groups, began absenting himself from regular worship and the Lord's Supper (on grounds that he could not partake with the unconverted), and began holding independent prayer meetings.

In 1676 the Düsseldorf church council investigated and dismissed him. Within two weeks, however, Neander signed a declaration promising to conform, and was reinstated.

## Neander's Cave

Legend holds that during his suspension from service, Neander spent long hours in a beautiful limestone gorge near Mettmann on the Rhine, known as the Neanderthal, writing hymns in a cave there. Whether strictly accurate or not, it is certain that Neander loved this place and returned to it often. One of his hymns, written "in this cave" according to tradition, contains the lines:

> *Thee all the mountains praise;*
> *The rocks and glens are full of songs of Thee!*
> *They bid me join my lays,*
> *And laud the mighty Rock, who, safe from every shock,*
> *Beneath Thy shadow here doth shelter me.*

The gorge he loved still bears his name, and of course it is now also the site where, in 1856, a Neanderthal skull was found --- giving the ancient hominid its name.

## His Hymns

Neander's hymns are above all hymns of praise. They are jubilant, smooth in rhythm, and almost irresistible to sing. They quickly found their way into Lutheran hymnals and from there to the entire Protestant world.

**"Praise to the Lord, the Almighty"** (*Lobe den Herren, den mächtigen König der Ehren*) --- written in 1680, the year of his death, this is Neander's crowning achievement and one of the grandest hymns of adoration in Christian history. A paraphrase of Psalms 103 and 150, it opens with the famous call:

> *Praise to the Lord, the Almighty, the King of creation!*
> *O my soul, praise Him, for He is thy health and salvation!*

**"Heaven and Earth, and Sea and Air"** --- a nature hymn urging all creation to praise its Maker

**"Here Behold Me, as I Cast Me"** --- a penitential hymn that found favor throughout Christendom

**"All My Hope on God Is Founded"** (attributed) --- one version of a hymn in this tradition

## Death at Thirty

In 1679, Neander's spiritual friend Pastor Under-Eyck invited him to come to Bremen as his assistant at St. Martin's Church. Despite a salary of only 40 thalers and a free house, Neander joyfully accepted. The following year, however, he fell seriously ill.

During his illness he experienced severe spiritual struggles, but found peace in Scripture. On the day of his death, May 31, 1680, he asked that Hebrews 7:9 be read to him. When asked how he felt, he replied: "The Lord has settled my account. Lord Jesus, make also me ready." His last words were: "It is well with me. The mountains shall be moved, and the hills shall tremble, yet the grace of God shall not depart from me, and His covenant of peace shall not be moved."

He was thirty years old.

## Significance

Neander's brief life produced a body of praise hymns that broke open the door of hymnody for the Reformed tradition. His legacy to the entire church is one of the most exuberant calls to worship ever written --- sung today in churches of nearly every denomination. The Neanderthal gorge, named for the man who retreated there to write songs of praise, became famous for an entirely different reason centuries later --- giving us the somewhat jarring image of "Neanderthal Man" being named, however indirectly, after a hymn-writer.

In the lineage of German hymnody, he is the bridge between the Lutheran tradition exemplified by [[Paul_Gerhardt]] and the broader Protestant world, demonstrating that the spirit of evangelical praise knows no denominational walls.
