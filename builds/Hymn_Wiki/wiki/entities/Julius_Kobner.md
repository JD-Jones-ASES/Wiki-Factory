# Julius Kobner

Julius Kobner was born June 11, 1807, at Odense, capital of the island Fühnen, Denmark — next to Copenhagen the most important place in that country. As the son of the head rabbi he was brought up in all the traditions of Jewish learning and received careful instruction in a good school.

## From Rabbi's Son to Baptist Leader

Having become an engraver, Kobner entered into a marriage engagement with a young lady of noble birth. Since such a union was difficult in Denmark, the young couple left and settled in Wandersbeck, Germany, where they renounced Judaism, entered the state church, and were married by a special act of grace from the Danish king.

At this point Kobner was not especially interested in religious matters. While in Lübeck he was on friendly terms with Dr. Geibel, pastor of the Reformed Church; afterward he earned a living as a playwright in Hamburg. But in Hamburg he encountered John G. Oncken, who "made known to him the way of life so clearly that soon he could say of the Messiah, 'Rabbi, thou art the Son of God, thou art the King of Israel.'"

On May 7, 1836, Kobner was baptized by Oncken. Until 1852 he assisted Oncken as pastor of the Hamburg Baptist church and as a missionary. An earnest scholar, he made himself familiar with Greek, Latin, and English in addition to Hebrew, Danish, and German — six languages in all. From 1852 to 1866, he was pastor of the Baptist church in Barmen, Rhenish Prussia, a church of which he was himself the founder. In 1866, he went to Copenhagen, where he labored until 1879. He then returned to Barmen, and in 1883 became pastor of the Baptist church in Berlin — where he closed his work, dying February 2, 1884, one month after Oncken's death and nearly two years after the death of [[Gottfried_Lehmann]].

## His Standing and His Hymns

Burrage says plainly: "Kobner unquestionably was the ablest of the leaders of the German Baptist movement inaugurated by Oncken. Somewhat of a mystic, he was the most eloquent preacher the Baptists in Germany have produced." His poetical faculties were of a high order, and he was prolific.

His hymn book, the *Glaubensstimme*, which he compiled, became the standard German Baptist hymnbook. He also compiled the *Harfentöne* and published a collection of his own hymns entitled *Liederstrauss*. His other poetical works included *Die Waldenser* and *Das Lied von Gott* — the latter in dramatic form with fine lyrical passages. President Hovey of Newton Theological Institution gave an extended notice of *Das Lied von Gott* in the *Baptist Quarterly Review* for July 1877. Hovey translates a passage on the sufferings of Christ in Gethsemane:

*In dark Gethsemane he wept; / To him the cup of death was given; / Though perfectly the law he kept, / His soul with pangs of hell was riven.*
