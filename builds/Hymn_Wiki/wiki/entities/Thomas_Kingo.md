# Thomas Kingo

Denmark's first great hymnist hailed from Scotland. His grandfather, who also bore the name Thomas, emigrated from Scotland to Denmark in the late 17th century to become a tapestry weaver for Christian IV. From this unlikely beginning came the man who would give the Danish church its own singing voice.

## The Scottish-Danish Bard

Kingo was born in Slangerup, Denmark, on December 15, 1634. At six years old he entered the Latin school of his native city; at sixteen he became a student at the school in Frederiksborg, where the principal Albert Bartholin recognized unusual gifts in the boy and took him into his own home. After completing theological studies at the university, Kingo returned to Slangerup in 1668 as a Lutheran parish pastor.

He began attracting attention as a secular poet, but it was not until 1673 that his first collection of hymns appeared under the title *Spiritual Songs, First Part*. The impression it created was profound. In 1677 he was elevated from his obscure parish to the bishopric of the diocese of Fyen.

## "The Soul of the Danes"

When Kingo dedicated his *Spiritual Songs* to King Christian V, he made a striking argument for Danish hymnody. In his address to the king, he deplored the fact that the Danish people had depended so largely in worship on hymns of foreign origin:

*"The soul of the Danes is not so bound and impoverished but that it can soar as high toward heaven as that of other peoples, even if it be not upborne by strange and foreign wings."*

He also defended the practice of writing hymns to popular folk melodies: *"If a pleasing melody set to a song of Sodom delights your ear, how much more, if you are a true child of God, should not that same melody delight your soul when sung to a song of Zion!"*

## The Battle for the Hymn-Book

In 1683, Kingo received the royal appointment to prepare an official hymn-book for the Church of Denmark. The king's decree stated specifically that Kingo should include his own hymns, while making few changes to the old traditional hymns and under no circumstances altering the meaning of Luther's hymns.

The first part of the new book appeared in 1689 --- and met with a storm of disapproval. Of 267 hymns, 136 were Kingo's own. Members of the Danish court who had been objects of his merciless satire in earlier years now found their opportunity for revenge. The book was rejected, published at Kingo's own expense, and a rival editor was appointed. That rival's work was also rejected. A commission was finally appointed; Kingo came back into favor. The new hymn-book, officially approved in 1699, was based largely on his work and contained 85 of his original hymns.

Although Kingo lived to see his life's work crowned with success, he never fully recovered from the indignity and humiliation he had suffered. He died on October 14, 1703. The day before his death, he exclaimed: *"Tomorrow, Lord, we shall hear glorious music."*

## The Poet of Easter-Tide

Kingo has been called "the poet of Easter-tide." A biographer declares that he was "in love with the sun" and regarded light as the "true element." This is reflected above all in his morning hymns, which are among the finest songs of praise ever written. A Danish hymnologist named Brandt pointed out that the three great Danish hymnists each had a characteristic symbol: Kingo's was the sun, Brorson's the rose, and Grundtvig's the bird. Kingo extols Christ as the risen, victorious Saviour --- the Sun that breaks through the dark shades of sin and death.

His morning hymn begins:

> The sun arises now
>   In light and glory,
> And gilds the rugged brow
>   Of mountains hoary;
> Be glad, my soul, and lift
>   Thy voice in singing
> To God from earth below,
> Thy heart with joy aglow
>   And praises ringing.

But Kingo could also dwell on Christ's passion with gripping pathos:

> On my heart imprint Thine image,
>   Blessed Jesus, King of grace...

His Lenten hymns, and especially "Over Kedron Jesus treadeth," are among the most soul-stirring hymns in the Northern tradition.

## Legacy

Kingo gave the Danish Church something it had never had: its own voice. Before Kingo, Danes worshiped largely with hymns from other nations. After Kingo, there was a Danish hymnody --- and 85 of its foundational hymns bore his name. His successors, [[Hans_Adolph_Brorson]] and [[N_F_S_Grundtvig]], would build upon the foundation he laid.
