# John Playford

John Playford holds a peculiar place in the history of English hymnody: he was the first person to seriously attempt introducing hymns into the worship of the Church of England, he failed, and then his name became synonymous with the very Psalter tradition he had tried to displace. Both the attempt and the failure are important to understanding how long and how stubbornly the Church of England resisted hymns before the Oxford Movement finally changed it.

## Background

Playford — identified by Benson as John Playford the Younger, the son of the more famous music publisher Henry Playford — was parish clerk of the Temple Church in London, one of the most prominent Anglican parishes. His access to the musical life of the Church and his family's publishing connections gave him unique standing to attempt a reform of Anglican congregational song.

## The Attempted Reform (1671)

In 1671, Playford published *Psalms and Hymns in Solemn Musick* — a collection that included both the metrical psalms customary in Anglican worship and a selection of hymns of human composition. The inclusion of original hymns alongside the psalms was a deliberate attempt to introduce hymn-singing into Anglican parish worship, anticipating by decades what the Evangelical Revival and then Watts would accomplish.

The attempt failed. The Church of England was not ready. The Calvinist heritage, the legal framework of the Prayer Book, and the deep conservatism of the parish system combined to ensure that Playford's hymns found no welcome. They were admired in some quarters but not adopted.

## The Standard Psalter (1677)

Playford's response to this failure was practical: he gave the Church of England what it would accept. In 1677 he published *The Whole Book of Psalms*, a new musical setting of the metrical psalter that quickly became the standard version for Anglican churches. Benson's account makes clear that this book dominated Anglican congregational song for approximately a century — from its publication until well into the era of the Oxford Movement.

The irony is complete. Playford began by trying to bring hymns into the Church of England; he ended by providing the Psalter that made hymn-singing even harder to introduce, simply by giving congregations a more competent and pleasing alternative than they had had before.

## Significance in Hymnodic History

Benson identifies Playford as an important figure precisely because his 1671 collection documents the earliest systematic effort to change Anglican worship practice. Before Watts, before the Wesleys, before Heber and the Oxford Movement, there was Playford — writing and publishing at a moment when the Church of England showed no appetite for what he was offering.

His career illustrates the central argument of Benson's book: that English hymnody did not emerge smoothly from an obvious consensus but was the product of struggle against institutional inertia. The Church of England resisted hymns for approximately two centuries after the Reformation, and Playford's failure in 1671 is part of that story.

## The Standard Psalter's Long Reign

The dominance of the 1677 Psalter meant that for most of the eighteenth century — the very period when Watts was transforming Nonconformist worship and when the Wesleys were transforming popular religion — the typical Anglican church remained locked into a musical tradition of metrical psalms set to Playford's tunes. When Reginald Heber finally obtained permission in the 1820s to publish a proper Anglican hymnal, he was breaking a mold that had been set by Playford's publishing enterprise 150 years earlier.
