# Adoniram Judson

There is no name dearer to American Baptists than that of Adoniram Judson. He was a man who changed denominations by honest conviction, suffered imprisonment and the loss of two beloved wives for the sake of the gospel, translated the entire Bible into Burmese, and wrote the hymns that arose from that crucible. His story is inseparable from the founding of American Baptist foreign missions.

## Early Life and Conversion to Baptist Views

Judson was born August 9, 1788, in Malden, Massachusetts, where his father was pastor of the Congregational church. He graduated from Brown University in 1807 with the highest honors of his class. The year following, while teaching in Plymouth, he accepted views hostile to Christianity — under the influence of a skeptical college friend. But the sudden death of that friend, encountered under peculiar circumstances on a subsequent journey, changed the current of his thoughts. He returned home, entered Andover Theological Seminary as a special student, and came to a hearty acceptance of Christ. He united with his father's Congregational church at Plymouth, May 28, 1809.

In February 1810 he resolved to consecrate himself to foreign missions. There was no American foreign missionary society. He wrote to the London Missionary Society and received an invitation to visit England. At the meeting of the American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions in September 1811, Judson and his associates were accepted as missionaries under that Board's support.

On the voyage to Calcutta in 1812, knowing he would meet the Baptist missionaries at Serampore, Judson commenced a careful study of baptism. The result was complete: his views changed both as to the subjects and the act of baptism. On September 6, 1812, he and his wife Ann were baptized by Rev. William Ward at Serampore. Having resigned their appointment under the American Board, the Judsons appealed to American Baptists for support. The appeal, as Burrage notes, "thrilled the hearts of Baptists in all parts of the land," and the Baptist Triennial Convention was organized May 18, 1814.

## Burma: The Long Mission

Driven from India by the East India Company's hostility to missions, Judson entered Burma, reaching Rangoon on July 14, 1813. He studied the language for years before his first convert, Moung Nau, was baptized on June 27, 1819.

War between Burma and the British East India Government overshadowed the mission in 1824. Dr. Judson — he received the D.D. from Brown University in 1823 — was arrested at Ava and thrown into the death prison. For eleven months he remained there, nine months in three pairs of fetters, and two months in five pairs. Only the daily visits of Ann Judson, who brought food, prevented starvation. He was then moved to the still more wretched prison at Oung-pen-la, where Ann followed him.

The continued success of English arms eventually prevented the prisoners' execution, and they were released. But Ann Judson, exhausted by her heroic labors, died at Amherst, October 24, 1826.

Judson completed his translation of the New Testament. On January 31, 1834, he completed his translation of the entire Bible into Burmese. He married his second wife, Sarah B. Boardman (widow of missionary George Dana Boardman), on April 10, 1834 — see [[Sarah_B_Judson]].

Sarah Judson died on shipboard in the harbor of St. Helena, September 1, 1845, in the forty-second year of her age. Judson returned to the United States, and on June 2, 1846, married Miss Emily Chubbuck of Utica, N.Y. — see [[Emily_C_Judson]]. They returned to Burma together.

Judson's health failed in 1849. In April 1850 he set sail for the Isle of France on a sea-voyage recommended for his health. He continued to grow weaker, and on April 12, 1850 — nine days after embarkation — he died at sea, and was buried in the ocean, latitude thirteen degrees north, longitude ninety-three degrees east.

## His Hymns and Poetry

Judson only occasionally gave his thoughts a poetical dress, but what he wrote carries the weight of his extraordinary circumstances.

His **baptismal hymn**, composed in the death prison at Ava, is the best known:

*Come, Holy Spirit, Dove divine, / On these baptismal waters shine.*

This hymn appears in *The Psalmist* and other collections. Another baptismal hymn begins:

*Our Savior bowed beneath the wave.*

He also composed in the death prison a versification of the Lord's Prayer — "in fewer words than the original Greek, and containing only two more than the common translation" — which appears in *The Psalmist* (807):

*Our Father, God, who art in heaven, / All hallowed be thy name; / Thy kingdom come, thy will be done / In earth and heaven the same.*

After his release from prison, "oppressed by the loss of his wife and daughter," he wrote "The Solitary's Lament," beginning *Together let us sweetly live, / Together let us die.* He also wrote tender lines addressed to an infant daughter in the condemned prison at Ava, and verses to his children on a subsequent sea voyage.

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Burrage's account of Judson opens the American Baptist section of the book with appropriate grandeur — he is the pivotal American Baptist figure. The story of Judson's conversion to Baptist views by independent Bible study on the voyage to India was enormously important for American Baptist identity: here was a man who had not been raised Baptist, who examined the Scriptures for himself and changed his views at great personal cost.

The organizational consequence — the founding of the Baptist Triennial Convention in 1814 — made American Baptists a self-conscious missionary denomination, and the stream of Judson-related hymnody (from his own hymns to those of his wives) is one of the most distinctive features of the American Baptist tradition.
