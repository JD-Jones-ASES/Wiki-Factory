# Frances Ridley Havergal

To read the story of Frances Ridley Havergal's life is not only an inspiration, Ryden writes, but it "discloses at once the secret of her beautiful hymns. She lived her hymns before she wrote them."

She was one of those rare people who are so utterly in earnest that their earnestness becomes a light.

## Little Quicksilver

Frances was born at Astley, Worcestershire, on December 14, 1836 --- the daughter of a Church of England minister who was himself a hymn-writer of no mean ability. She was such a bright, happy, and vivacious child that her father called her "Little Quicksilver." He was also a gifted musician, and this quality too was inherited by his daughter. She became a brilliant pianist and passionately fond of singing. But she would sing nothing but sacred songs, looking upon her talents as gifts from God to be used only in His service.

Her naturally sunny nature became even more radiant following a deep spiritual experience at the age of fourteen. She wrote of it: *"I committed my soul to the Saviour, and earth and heaven seemed brighter from that moment."*

At eighteen she was confirmed, and she wrote in her manuscript book:

> Oh! Thine for ever, what a blessed thing
>   To be for ever His who died for me!
> My Saviour, all my life Thy praise I'll sing,
>   Nor cease my song throughout eternity.

## The Painting and the Hymn

Four years later, while studying in Düsseldorf, Germany, she chanced to see Sternberg's celebrated painting *Ecce Homo*, with the inscription beneath it: *"This have I done for thee; What hast thou done for me?"* She was deeply moved. Immediately she seized a piece of scrap paper and a pencil and wrote a response:

> I gave My life for thee,
>   My precious blood I shed,
> That thou might'st ransomed be,
>   And quickened from the dead.
> I gave My life for thee:
> What hast thou given for Me?

She thought the verses so poor after reading them over that she tossed them into a stove. The paper fell out untouched by the flames. When she showed the words to her father a few months later, he was so touched that he immediately composed a tune for them.

## Take My Life

Havergal's most famous hymn, "Take my life, and let it be," was written in 1874. The circumstances are moving. She was spending a few days in a home where ten people were gathered, some unconverted and others half-hearted Christians who seemed to derive no joy from their religion. A great desire came upon her to be the means of bringing them all to true faith. Her prayer was wonderfully answered. On the last night of her stay her heart was so filled with joy and gratitude that she could not sleep. Instead, she spent the night writing the consecration hymn:

> Take my life, and let it be
> Consecrated, Lord, to Thee.
> Take my moments and my days;
> Let them flow in ceaseless praise.

Her prayer, "Take my silver and my gold; not a mite would I withhold," was not an idle petition. In August 1878, she wrote to a friend: *"The Lord has shown me another little step, and of course I have taken it, with extreme delight. 'Take my silver and my gold' now means shipping off all my ornaments to the Church Missionary House --- nearly fifty articles are being packed up. I don't think I ever packed a box with such pleasure."*

## The Woman Behind the Hymns

Havergal was a brilliant linguist, having mastered several modern languages. She was also proficient in Greek and Hebrew. Her sister records that she always kept her Hebrew Bible and Greek New Testament at hand when reading the Scriptures.

She was able to repeat from memory the four Gospels, the Epistles, Revelation, and all the Psalms --- and in later years added Isaiah and the Minor Prophets. She began her Bible reading at seven o'clock in summer mornings. On cold days, when her sister urged her to sit near the fire, she would answer: *"But then, Marie, I can't rule my lines neatly. Just see what a find I've got. If one only searches, there are such extraordinary things in the Bible!"*

She and [[Fanny_Crosby]], the American blind hymnist, never met but were ardent admirers of each other. Havergal sent Crosby a greeting that captures something of her own spirit:

> Dear blind sister over the sea,
> An English heart goes forth to thee.
> We are linked by a cable of faith and song...
> Sister! What shall our meeting be,
> When our hearts shall sing, and our eyes shall see!

## The Death

Frances Ridley Havergal was only forty-two years old when she died on June 3, 1879. When her attending physician told her that her condition was serious, she replied: *"If I am really going, it is too good to be true!"*

She had placed at the foot of her bed her favorite text, where she could see it: *"The blood of Jesus Christ His Son cleanseth us from all sin."* She asked that these words be inscribed upon her coffin and her tombstone.

*"Splendid! To be so near the gates of heaven!"* she exclaimed. And again: *"So beautiful to go! So beautiful to go!"*

She died while singing:

> Jesus, I will trust Thee,
>   Trust Thee with my soul...

## The Hymns

Among her hymns besides those already mentioned:

- **"O Saviour, precious Saviour"**
- **"I am trusting Thee, Lord Jesus"**
- **"Thou art coming, O my Saviour"**
- **"Lord, speak to me, that I may speak"**
- **"Singing for Jesus, our Saviour and King"**

## Legacy

Frances Ridley Havergal is the consecration poet --- the woman who asked God to take everything, and then proved by her life that she meant it. She is proof that the most fruitful lives are often the shortest ones, and that a surrendered life makes better music than a long one.

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

Butterworth-Brown note that the "Ecce Homo" in the Düsseldorf gallery that inspired "I Gave My Life for Thee" had already exerted the same influence on the young Count Zinzendorf — he too had stood before the same painting, read the same motto ("All this I have done for thee; What doest thou for me?"), and had his lifelong motto formed by it. The sight "seems to have had much to do with the gifted girl's early religious experience, and indeed exerted its influence on her whole life." They observe that "writing is *praying* with me," was Havergal's own summary of her work.

They also note that the tune making "I Gave My Life for Thee" familiar in America was composed by Philip P. Bliss — a pupil of George F. Root, born in Rome, Pennsylvania in 1838, who perished in the Ashtabula railroad disaster on December 29, 1876, along with his wife Lucy Young Bliss. The tune is named from the hymn's first line. Butterworth-Brown correct a biographical detail: though originally christened "Philipp Bliss," he himself changed the spelling, preferring to let the third P stand alone as a middle initial.

## Hymnological Context (Duffield)

Duffield (1886) treats 12 of Havergal's hymns. He was writing just seven years after her death (1879) and draws on her published Poems for his source citations, noting the precise page numbers.

On **"O Saviour, Precious Saviour"**, Duffield observes that "Miss Havergal's poetical compositions were singularly dependent upon her moods" — making this hymn of pure adoration exceptional in its freedom from the self-examination that marks her more typical work.

On **"Tell It Out among the Nations"**, Duffield records that Ira Sankey composed the tune for this hymn and that the verses were written for a specific occasion. This is an early and reliable attribution of the Sankey-Havergal collaboration.

On **"Thou Art Coming, O My Saviour"** (Advent Hymn, seven stanzas, from Poems p. 52), Duffield notes that "Thou Art Coming at Thy Table" is the second part of the same poem — clarifying a frequent confusion between the two closely related hymns.

On **"I Am Trusting Thee, Lord Jesus"**, Duffield gives the date as within her active period and locates it precisely in her Poems (p. 255) under the title "Trusting Jesus."

On **"Master, Speak! Thy Servant Heareth"**, Duffield records it from her Poems (p. 214) in nine stanzas, titled "Master, say on" — Havergal's own title expressing the prayer for divine instruction that the hymn voices.

On **"Standing at the Portal"**, based on Isaiah 41:10, Duffield gives the composition date as 1873 — a useful anchor for her later output.

Duffield's testimony is notably warm. Though brief in his entries, he consistently situates Havergal within the devotional mainstream of English hymnody, which by 1886 was already treating her work as classic.
