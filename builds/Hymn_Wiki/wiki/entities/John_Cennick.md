# John Cennick

John Cennick was born at Reading, Berkshire, England, on December 12, 1718. Like [[Joseph_Hart]], he was led to Christ after a reckless boyhood and youth, through the work of the Holy Spirit working independently of any direct outward influence. Sickened of his cards, novels, and playhouse pleasures, he had begun a sort of mechanical self-reform when one day, walking in the streets of London, he suddenly seemed to hear a text spoken to him: "I am thy salvation!" His consecration began at that moment.

He studied for the ministry and became a preacher, first under the direction of the Wesleys, then under Whitefield, but afterwards joined the Moravians (the "Brethren"), in whose tradition he spent the remainder of his ministry. He died in London on July 4, 1755.

## Thou Dear Redeemer, Dying Lamb

Butterworth-Brown describe this hymn as having been "familiarized to the public" through a revival tune so closely linked with it that "few religious people now past middle life could enjoy singing it to any other." The tune "Rhine" (in some collections called "Emmons") was composed by Frederick Burgmüller — a young German musician born in 1804 who died in 1824 at only twenty years old. Butterworth-Brown describe it as a "bright legato melody" from one of Burgmüller's marches, with "a clear, bold gladness sure to infect a meeting with its own spiritual fervor":

> Thou dear Redeemer, dying Lamb,
>   I love to hear of Thee;
> No music like Thy charming name,
>   Nor half so sweet can be.

## Jesus My All to Heaven Is Gone

This earlier Cennick hymn was linked with the revival tune "Duane Street" (long-metre double) composed by the Rev. George Coles in 1835, and later appeared in the *New Methodist Hymnal*. Butterworth-Brown recall "the hearty unison with which prayer-meeting and camp-meeting assemblies used to 'crescendo' the last stanza":

> Then will I tell to sinners round
> What a dear Saviour I have found.

## Musical and Thematic Context

Cennick is one of those transitional figures who connect several streams of the evangelical revival: Wesleyan, Calvinistic, and Moravian. His hymns carry the emotional directness of the revival tradition — personal, Christ-centered, and shaped by an acute awareness of human unworthiness and divine grace. His early death at thirty-six meant that his output was relatively small, but what he left behind has retained its place in the hymnal.
