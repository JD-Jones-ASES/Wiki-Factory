# Julia Ward Howe

Julia Ward Howe is the author of the one American hymn that lifted a war to the level of prophetic vision. The "Battle Hymn of the Republic" was written before dawn in a Washington hotel room in November 1861, and it has never stopped marching.

## The Birth of the Battle Hymn

After a visit to the Federal camps on the Potomac in the autumn of 1861, Howe returned to her lodgings in Washington, fatigued by a long, cold drive, and slept. She woke before daybreak and, lying still in the dark, "began to twine the long lines of a hymn which promised to suit the measure of the 'John Brown' melody." She wrote out the lines in the dark, returned to sleep, and sent the poem to the *Atlantic Monthly*, which published it in February 1862 with the title "Battle Hymn of the Republic."

The tune had been a camp-meeting melody composed in 1855 by John William Steffe of Richmond, Virginia, for a fire company; it was then taken up by soldiers at Fort Warren in Boston Harbor who improvised the "John Brown Song." Mrs. Howe's poem made the rustic original sublime.

## The Hymn's Vision

The hymn is a prophetic text in the Old Testament sense --- a poet's vision of God's righteousness moving through history. It sees the marching armies in the light of the grapes of wrath, the fateful lightning, the righteous judgment, and ultimately the lilies of Christ's birth. Its closing stanza --- "As He died to make men holy, let us die to make men free" --- fused the cause of abolition with the language of atonement in a way that reverberated for generations. Butterworth-Brown call it "a career that promises to run till battle hymns cease to be sung."

## Her Life and Work

Julia Ward was born in New York City on May 27, 1819. In 1843 she became the wife of Samuel Gridley Howe, the philanthropist and champion of liberty, and with him edited the anti-slavery paper *The Boston Commonwealth* until the Civil War closed its mission. She was active throughout the war in philanthropic work and remained so for the rest of her long life --- an advocate for peace, justice, and women's suffrage. Her four surviving children all became eminent in the scientific and literary world.

Her "great hymn first brought her prominently before the public," Butterworth-Brown note, but "her many other writings would have made a literary reputation" in any case.
