# John Newton

In an old English church there is a memorial tablet that tells the most dramatic story in all of hymnody. Newton wrote his own epitaph, and it reads:

> *John Newton, clerk, once an Infidel and Libertine, a servant of slavers in Africa, was, by the rich Mercy of our Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ, preserved, restored, pardoned, and appointed to preach the Faith he had long labored to destroy.*

The man who wrote "How sweet the Name of Jesus sounds" and the immortal "Amazing Grace" had earned the right to every word of that inscription.

## A Life Gone Wrong

Newton was born in London on July 24, 1725. His mother was a deeply pious woman who found her greatest joy in teaching her boy Scripture passages and hymns --- he could read the Catechism at age four. But she died when he was only seven, and he was largely left to fend for himself. On his eleventh birthday he joined his father, a sea captain, at sea.

Through the influence of evil companions and infidel literature, he fell into a completely godless life. He was pressed into the navy, deserted, was caught and flogged at the mast, and then degraded in rank. From the navy he somehow fell into the hands of an unscrupulous slave-dealer in Africa and found himself in conditions little better than slavery himself. Ryden describes his state plainly: *"In his misery he gave himself up to nameless sins."*

And yet the memory of his mother, and the truths she had planted in him as a child, gave his conscience no peace. The reading of *The Imitation of Christ* by Thomas à Kempis moved him deeply. A terrifying storm at sea, and then a bout with malignant fever in Africa, finally brought the prodigal to his knees.

## Six Years as a Slave-Ship Captain

Even after his initial turn toward God, Newton spent six more years as the captain of a slave ship. He passed through severe spiritual struggles during this period, trying to find peace through strict moral living. It was not until his final voyage, when he met a pious captain who helped him toward a deeper faith in Christ, that the light fully broke in.

## Olney: The Great Friendship

For nine years in Liverpool, Newton studied the Scriptures in Hebrew and Greek and associated with Whitefield and the Wesleys. In 1764 he was ordained as curate of the little village of Olney, where he formed the remarkable friendship with the poet [[William_Cowper]] that would change hymnody forever.

Newton proposed a project: the two of them would write a hymn-book together. The *Olney Hymns*, published in 1779, contained 349 hymns --- 66 by Cowper and the rest by Newton. It is one of the great collections in English hymnody, and from it came some of the most beloved hymns in the language.

Newton's own contributions include:

- **"How sweet the Name of Jesus sounds"** --- a hymn of surpassing tenderness, ranked among the finest in English
- **"Amazing grace! how sweet the sound"**
- **"Glorious things of thee are spoken"**
- **"Come, my soul, thy suit prepare"**
- **"Approach, my soul, the mercy-seat"**
- **"May the grace of Christ our Saviour"**
- **"Though troubles assail us, and dangers affright"**
- **"Safely through another week"**

## The Old African Blasphemer

In 1779 Newton moved to London as rector of St. Mary Woolnoth, where he served for twenty-eight years. In 1805, when his eyesight began to fail and friends urged him to stop preaching, he answered: *"What! shall the old African blasphemer stop while he can speak?"*

Near the end, when his memory was fading, an assistant had to stand in the pulpit and help him read his manuscript. One Sunday Newton twice read the words "Jesus Christ is precious." The assistant whispered: "You have already said that twice; go on." Newton turned to him and said quietly, "John, I said that twice, and I am going to say it again." Then the old preacher shouted: *"Jesus Christ is precious!"*

Newton died in London in 1807. Among his converts were numbered the missionary Claudius Buchanan and the Bible commentator Thomas Scott.

## "Amazing Grace"

Newton's life was the living text of his most famous hymn. The man who had been an infidel, a libertine, a servant of slavers, wrote these words out of the depths of personal experience:

> Amazing grace! how sweet the sound
>   That saved a wretch like me!
> I once was lost, but now am found ---
>   Was blind, but now I see.

There is no hymn in the English language more autobiographical than this one.

## Legacy

Newton stands as one of the most remarkable testimonies to the power of God's grace in hymnody. He represents the truth at the heart of the gospel: that no one is too far gone to be found. His epitaph, which he wrote himself, remains one of the most honest and moving inscriptions in Christian history.

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

Butterworth-Brown's portrait of Newton is admirably compressed: "the son of a sea-captain, he became a sailor, and for several years led a reckless life. Converted, he took holy orders and was settled as curate of Olney, Buckinghamshire, and afterwards Rector of St. Mary of Woolnoth, London, where he died, Dec. 21, 1807." They note that the defiance to doubt in "Begone Unbelief" is "the blunt utterance of a sailor rather than the song of a poet."

For the tune, they observe that old "Hanover," by William Croft (1677-1727), "carries Newton's hymn successfully, but Joseph Haydn's choral of 'Lyons' is more familiar — and better music." "Hanover" also often accompanies Charles Wesley's "Ye servants of God, your Master proclaim."

## Hymnological Context (Duffield)

Duffield (1886) covers 30 of Newton's hymns, making Newton the third most-represented author in the volume after Watts and Charles Wesley. Duffield provides precise placement of each hymn within the *Olney Hymns* (1779), which he divides into three books: Book I (On Select Texts of Scripture), Book II (On Occasional Subjects), and Book III (Applying to the State and Experience of Christians).

On **"Amazing Grace"** (Olney Hymns, Book I, No. 41, titled "Faith's Review and Expectation"), Duffield notes its scriptural text is 1 Chronicles 17:16-17 and that it ran to six stanzas in the original. He confirms the Moravian Hymn Book (1789) includes it independently, calling it "the transcript of his own experience."

On **"How Sweet the Name of Jesus Sounds"**, Duffield suggests it may be "an echo, or paraphrase, of the great Latin hymn of St. Bernard of Clairvaux" — a significant textual observation placing Newton in continuity with the medieval Latin tradition.

On **"Glorious Things of Thee Are Spoken"** (Olney Hymns, Book II, No. 24, titled "Zion, or the City of God"), Duffield notes that the hymn originally opened differently: the current first line was not Newton's original opening word.

Duffield observes that Newton knew the Westminster Catechism by heart and was instrumental in the conversion of the missionary Claudius Buchanan. He also recounts that Newton was "much depends on the way we come into trouble — Paul and Jonah were both in a storm" as Newton's own saying, which illuminates the theology behind "Though Troubles Assail."

According to Duffield, the parting hymn "For a Season Called to Part" was written specifically in November 1776 when Newton was leaving Olney for London — a precise biographical date not found in other sources.
