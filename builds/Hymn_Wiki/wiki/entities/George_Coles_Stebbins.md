# George Coles Stebbins

George Coles Stebbins was the quiet craftsman behind many of the most beloved tunes of the Gospel Hymns era. Where [[Ira_Sankey]] was the celebrated soloist and [[Philip_Paul_Bliss]] the inspired poet-composer, Stebbins was the faithful musician who set other men's words and gave them wings.

## Early Life and Musical Education

Stebbins was born on February 26, 1846, in East Carlton, Orleans County, New York. He was educated at common school and an academy in Albany, then turned his full attention to music, studying in Rochester, Chicago, and Boston.

His musical career began in Chicago as chorister at the First Baptist Church. While holding the same position at Clarendon Street Church in Boston (1874-76), he entered on a course of evangelistic work with D.L. Moody as gospel singer and composer.

## The Moody-Sankey Missions

Stebbins became a co-editor with [[Ira_Sankey]] and James McGranahan of the *Gospel Hymns* series. He harmonized and arranged many pieces, including the well-known revival hymn "Throw Out the Life-line" (words by Rev. E.S. Ufford), which he prepared for *Winnowed Songs* (1890). Dr. Theodore Cuyler said to Sankey of that hymn: "There is more electricity in that song than in any other I ever heard."

## His Tunes

Among Stebbins's most characteristic compositions:

- The popular tune for **"We Speak of the Realms of the Blest"** (words by Mrs. Elizabeth Mills) --- described as "fluent and easily learned and rendered by rote," capturing both the pathos and the exaltation of that deathbed hymn.
- Tunes for **"There Is a Green Hill Far Away"** (words by Mrs. Cecil Frances Alexander) --- Butterworth-Brown note that of all the composers who set this gentle Passion hymn, Stebbins's composition "comes nearest to being the favorite, if one judges by the extent and frequency of its use."
- A tune for Horatius Bonar's **"Beyond the Smiling and the Weeping"** --- which, in some localities, superseded Bradbury's earlier setting.

## Legacy

Stebbins lived to an extraordinary age, dying in 1945 at ninety-eight. He outlived the entire era he helped create. His musical contribution was not brilliance but reliability: tunes that served the words, carried the feeling, and gave congregations what they needed to sing their faith. In this he was a faithful craftsman in the tradition of all the great hymn composers.
