# Paul Gerhardt

## The Prince of Lutheran Hymnists

Paul Gerhardt is, by nearly every measure, the greatest hymn-writer the Lutheran Church has ever produced --- and one of the greatest in all Christian history. More of his beautiful lyrics have found their way into the English language than those of any other German writer. He was born in suffering, lived through unspeakable trial, and responded with songs of transcendent joy. Beneath his portrait in the Lutheran church at Luebden hangs this Latin inscription: *Theologus in cribro Satanae versatus* --- "A divine sifted in Satan's sieve."

## Biographical Sketch

Gerhardt was born March 12, 1607, in Graefenhaynichen, a village near Wittenberg. His father, the village mayor, died before Paul reached maturity. At twenty-one he began studying theology at Wittenberg --- in the very years the [[Thirty_Years_War_Hymns|Thirty Years' War]] was turning Germany into a wasteland.

Because of the difficulty of finding a parish during the war years, Gerhardt served for many years as a private tutor in the home of lawyer Andreas Barthold. Barthold's daughter Anna Maria became his bride in 1655 --- a long and patient courtship. It was during this period that his poetic gifts blossomed, stimulated in no small part by contact with the great musician Johann Crueger, cantor of St. Nicholas Church in Berlin. In 1648 many of Gerhardt's hymns appeared in Crueger's *Praxis Pietatis Melica*, which brought him wide recognition.

In 1651 he was ordained as Lutheran provost at Mittenwalde. Six years later he accepted the position of third assistant pastor at St. Nicholas Church in Berlin, where his fame as a preacher and hymn-writer grew rapidly.

## A Faith Tested by Fire

The joy in Gerhardt's hymns is all the more remarkable because his life was shadowed by loss. He and Anna Maria lost four children; then came the political crisis that would cost him his pulpit.

The Calvinist Elector Friedrich Wilhelm of Prussia sought to impose religious unity by requiring all clergy to sign a document promising silence on doctrinal differences. Though Gerhardt had always been moderate in his public statements, he believed that signing would compromise the integrity of the Gospel. Summoning the other Lutheran clergy of Berlin to his bedside (he was ill at the time), he urged them to stand firm. The Elector promptly deposed him from office and even prohibited private worship in his own home.

To his suffering Gerhardt responded with characteristic fortitude: "This is only a small Berlin affliction; but I am also willing and ready to seal with my blood the evangelical truth, and, like my namesake, St. Paul, to offer my neck to the sword."

His wife died during this period of turmoil, leaving him with one surviving child, a six-year-old boy. In 1669 he was called to the church at Luebden, where he labored faithfully until his death on June 7, 1676.

## His Hymns

Gerhardt wrote 123 hymns in all. They mark a turning point in the history of Lutheran hymnody: where Luther's hymns have the quality of a trumpet call or a fortress wall, Gerhardt's have the warmth of a heart laid open. Sixteen of his hymns begin with the first-person pronoun "I." This is not weakness but intimacy --- a subjective focus that never loses sight of the great objective truth of justification by faith alone. He is the bridge between the militant Reformation hymn and the devotional personal lyric.

His hymns on nature have never been surpassed. In them, the beauty of created things is always pointing upward to the Creator.

**Principal hymns include:**

- **"O Sacred Head, Now Wounded"** (*O Haupt voll Blut und Wunden*) --- based on a medieval Latin poem attributed to Bernard of Clairvaux; one of the most beloved Passion hymns in all Christendom. Johann Sebastian Bach used the chorale in his St. Matthew Passion.
- **"All My Heart This Night Rejoices"** --- a jubilant Christmas carol
- **"Now Rest Beneath Night's Shadow"** (*Nun ruhen alle Wälder*) --- the famous evening hymn, beloved by Schiller who first heard it as a cradle song from his mother. Bach and Mozart both revered its melody (composed by Heinrich Isaak ca. 1490) as one of the sublimest of all chorales.
- **"O How Shall I Receive Thee"** --- an Advent hymn of tender consolation
- **"If God Himself Be for Me"** (*Ist Gott für mich, so trete*) --- based on Romans 8; Catherine Winkworth translated a portion as "Since Jesus Is My Friend." One stanza captures the spirit of the whole: *"Though earth be rent asunder, Thou'rt mine eternally; Not fire, nor sword, nor thunder, Shall sever me from Thee."*
- **"Commit Thou All Thy Griefs"** --- a hymn of trust in God's providence
- **"Holy Ghost, Dispel Our Sadness"** --- a Pentecost hymn
- **"Beside Thy Manger Here I Stand"** --- a Christmas devotional
- **"Go Forth, My Heart, and Seek Delight"** --- a spring hymn praising God in nature
- **"A Pilgrim and a Stranger"** --- a pilgrimage hymn

## Significance

Ryden writes: "The greatest Lutheran hymnist of the seventeenth century, and perhaps of all time, was Paul Gerhardt. Not even the hymns of Martin Luther are used so generally throughout the Christian world as those of Gerhardt."

The genius of Gerhardt is the combination of doctrinal solidity and personal warmth. He never lets the reader forget that the trust he sings of is grounded in objective Gospel truth --- the atonement of Christ --- but he makes that truth feel as close and personal as a friend's hand in the dark. His influence on [[Pietism]] and on later German hymnody was profound, and his hymns continue to be sung in churches around the world nearly four centuries after his death.

He stands in direct lineage with [[Martin_Luther]] as the supreme representatives of the German Lutheran hymn tradition, and his influence is visible in every hymn-writer of the generation that followed him.

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

Butterworth-Brown present Gerhardt's biography in the context of "Commit Thou All Thy Griefs" — calling it "the Hymn of Trust" and noting that Gerhardt "was a graduate of that school when he wrote it." Their biographical narrative: he was born at Grafenheinchen, Saxony, in 1606; "through the first and best years of manhood's strength (during the Thirty Years' War), a wandering preacher tossed from place to place, he was without a parish and without a home." After the peace of Westphalia he settled at Mittenwalde aged forty-four, married four years later, then moved to a Berlin church — where he "buried his wife, and four of his children, was deposed from the ministry because his Lutheran doctrines offended the Elector Frederick, and finally retired as a simple arch-deacon to a small parish in Lubben, where he preached, toiled, and suffered amid a rough and uncongenial people till he died." Their summary verdict: "Few men have ever lived whose case more needed a 'Hymn of Trust' — and fewer still could have written it themselves."

The associated tune is "Schumann" — named for Robert Schumann (1810-1876), born in Zwickau, Saxony, a music director, conservatory teacher, and "master-mind of the pre-Wagnerian period," happy in the cooperation of his wife, herself a skilled musician. Butterworth-Brown find the pairing entirely right: "the great 'Hymn of Trust' could have found no more sympathetic interpreter than the musician of Gerhardt's own land and language."

Catherine Winkworth's translation of "Commit Thou All Thy Griefs" is the one in standard English use — the same translator who rendered Altenburg's "Fear Not O Little Flock" and who contributed extensively to the *Lyra Germanica* series.
