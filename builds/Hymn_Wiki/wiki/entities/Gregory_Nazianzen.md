# Gregory Nazianzen

Gregory Nazianzen is described by Ryden as "the greatest name among the Greek hymnists" of the early church period --- a man whose life was marked by political turbulence and theological controversy, but who found his deepest peace in writing sacred poetry.

## A Life of Reluctant Service

Gregory was born in 325 AD, the son of a bishop. His father was determined that Gregory would enter the priesthood, and when Gregory was thirty-six years old he was effectively compelled to be ordained. He threw himself into his work with genuine zeal, however, and his gifts of preaching and theology eventually brought him to one of the highest positions in the Eastern Church: he was enthroned by the Emperor's own hand as Patriarch of Constantinople.

But church politics were brutal. The Arian faction --- those who denied the full divinity of Christ --- maneuvered against Gregory constantly, and eventually he was forced to resign his office and return to his hometown of Nazianzus. It was an unhappy end to his public career, and a bitter one.

## The Gift of Enforced Retirement

The forced retirement turned out to be a grace in disguise. Free from the pressures of church administration and theological controversy, Gregory devoted the last years of his life to what he apparently did best: writing sacred poetry. Ryden describes this poetry as being of "singular beauty and lofty spirit."

None of Gregory's hymns appear to have survived in direct use in English-language hymnody, but his influence on the Greek tradition of sacred poetry was profound. He stands as one of the master craftsmen of the era Ryden calls [[Greek_and_Syriac_Hymnody]].

## Significance

Gregory Nazianzen represents the Eastern Christian tradition at its most intellectually refined. A man caught between the competing demands of theological controversy and contemplative poetry, he ultimately chose the pen over the pulpit, and the church is richer for it. His life is also a reminder that forced retreat from public life can become the condition for a person's deepest and most enduring contribution.

### From Lorenz (1938)

Lorenz discusses Gregory of Nazianzus (327--389) in Chapter IX as the most prominent figure among the early Greek hymn writers. Called to Constantinople by Emperor Theodosius to lead the orthodox forces against the Arians, Gregory was appointed court preacher, Patriarch of the Eastern Church, and president of the Ecumenical Council of Constantinople. However, Lorenz notes that "the pious, gentle monk, while a great preacher and a fertile hymn writer (it is said that he wrote thirty thousand hymns), was not fitted for the strife and intrigue rampant in the Capital," and within a few years he returned to his cell at Nazianzus in Cappadocia. Lorenz rates his hymns very highly and quotes Dr. Brownlee's version of his "Evening Hymn" as an example of their quality.
