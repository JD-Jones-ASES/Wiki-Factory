# Joseph the Hymnographer

Joseph the Hymnographer is the last significant figure in the great tradition of [[Greek_and_Syriac_Hymnody]]. He lived and worked at Constantinople in the ninth century, and the title by which history knows him --- "the Hymnographer" --- suggests that hymn writing was understood to be his defining contribution.

## The Hymn on Angels

Ryden singles out one hymn of Joseph's in particular: a hymn for the feast of St. Michael and All Angels, which begins with the vivid image:

> Stars of the morning, so gloriously bright,
> Filled with celestial resplendence and light,
> These that, where night never followeth day,
> Raise the "Thrice Holy, Lord!" ever and aye.

The image is striking: angels as morning stars, blazing with a light that knows no night, whose endless occupation is the worship of God. It has an otherworldly grandeur that characterizes Eastern Christian hymnody at its best.

## The End of an Era

After Joseph, Ryden notes, hymnody in the Eastern church assumed an increasingly liturgical character. The great popular tradition of Greek sacred song --- which had begun with [[Clement_of_Alexandria]]'s "Shepherd of Tender Youth" in the second century and reached its finest expression with [[John_of_Damascus]]'s Easter hymns in the eighth --- gradually gave way to more formal, clerical liturgical forms.

Joseph the Hymnographer, fittingly named, stands as the final figure in a tradition that had lasted seven centuries. His hymns are a worthy close to a magnificent era.
