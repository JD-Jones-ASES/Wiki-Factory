# Giovanni Palestrina

The full name was Giovanni Pietro Aloysio da Palestrina, named for the town twenty miles from Rome where he was born. He is the man who saved sacred choral music when the Church was on the verge of banning it altogether.

## The Council of Trent

By the mid-sixteenth century, sacred music in European churches had become, in the opinion of many churchmen, dangerously secularized. Polyphonic settings were elaborate, theatrical, even frivolous. The Council of Trent commissioned a select board of cardinals and musicians to address the degeneration of church song. Palestrina was consulted as one of the leading experts.

His proposal was elegant: apply polyphonic technique to the reverence of plain-song. Preserve the solemn beauty of the ancient chant but add the charming chords of counterpoint. He wrote three masses for the Commission, one of them his famous "Requiem," and had them performed under his direction before the cardinals. The result, Butterworth-Brown write, revealed "to the censors the possibilities of contrapuntal music in sanctuary devotion and praise." The sanction was given, and "part-song harmony became permanently one of the angel voices of the Christian church."

## Father of the Choral Tune

Palestrina died in 1594, but his influence extended far beyond his death. Butterworth-Brown describe him as "the father of the choral tune" and note that "hymn-tunes adapted from his motets and masses are sung today." He lived to see musical instruments and congregational singing introduced in public worship, and to see how richly, in popular assemblies during the Protestant Reformation, his art had multiplied the creation of spiritual hymns.

## The Two Schools

Butterworth-Brown describe a musical division that Palestrina's work helped create. Contemporary with him in England was Thomas Tallis, who developed the Anglican school of church music. The extreme Protestants formed what Butterworth-Brown call "the Puritan or Genevan School," represented by "Old Hundred," while the Anglican is represented by "Tallis' Evening Hymn." The two traditions gradually reconciled, together forming "the model after which the best sacred tunes are built."

## Musical and Thematic Context

Butterworth-Brown connect Palestrina's work directly to the Protestant Reformation, noting with evident pleasure that this devout Roman Catholic composer "perhaps with secret pleasure, though he was a Romanist" lived to see how richly his helpful art multiplied sacred songs in popular Protestant assemblies. The "Sunday School" tune "Balerma," noted by Butterworth-Brown, "resembles rather one of Palestrina's chorals than the tripping melodies that took its place."
