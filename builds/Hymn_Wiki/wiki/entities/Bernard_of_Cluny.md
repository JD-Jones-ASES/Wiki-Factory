# Bernard of Cluny

Bernard of Cluny shares with his more famous contemporary [[Bernard_of_Clairvaux]] the title of foremost Latin hymn writer of the medieval period. The two men are often confused, and their names are sometimes used interchangeably --- but they were different people, from different places, with different personalities and different contributions to the hymnody of the Church.

## The Monk of Cluny

Very little is known about Bernard of Cluny's personal life. He was born in Morlaix in Brittany of English parents, probably in the early twelfth century. He entered the Abbey of Cluny, which at the time was the wealthiest and most luxurious monastery in all of Europe. This background is significant, because it shaped what he wrote.

## A 3,000-Line Critique

While living at Cluny, Bernard spent his leisure hours writing a massive poem called *De contemptu mundi* --- "On Contempt for the World." It runs to 3,000 lines and is, in essence, a scathing satire against the vices, corruptions, and follies of his age. Bernard did not mince words. He was an insider at one of the most powerful institutions of his day, and what he saw there inspired a poem of fierce, sustained moral indignation.

This is somewhat ironic, given that the three hymns derived from his poem are among the most serene and beautiful descriptions of heaven in the entire hymnological tradition. But that is exactly the structure of *De contemptu mundi*: the darkness of the present age, set against the luminous beauty of the world to come. The more vividly Bernard painted the corruption of earthly life, the more radiantly he could paint the glory of the heavenly Jerusalem.

## The Three Hymns

From this 3,000-line poem, [[John_Mason_Neale]] extracted three hymns in the nineteenth century, and they have been loved ever since:

**"Jerusalem the golden"** is the best known. It is a hymn of longing for heaven, painted in images of gold, milk and honey, and the communion of saints. Its second stanza has been particularly beloved:

> They stand, those halls of Zion,
> All jubilant with song,
> And bright with many an angel,
> And all the martyr throng.

**"Brief life is here our portion"** draws on the same well of longing, acknowledging the brevity and sorrow of earthly existence while pointing to the rest that awaits.

**"For thee, O dear, dear country"** is an apostrophe to heaven itself --- a love song to a home not yet reached.

All three hymns have in common a poignant yearning for a better world than the one we inhabit. They are hymns for people who feel the weight of mortality and imperfection, who sense that this life is not the whole story.

## Significance

Bernard of Cluny is a reminder that great devotional poetry does not always come from peaceful, settled spirits. It can come from a man burning with righteous anger at the corruption of his world --- anger that, when it reaches for something better, produces not rant but song. His three hymns, rescued from a vast Latin poem by Neale's translations, have become part of the permanent treasury of English hymnody.
