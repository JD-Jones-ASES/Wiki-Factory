# Phebe Hinsdale Brown

Phebe Hinsdale Brown was born in Canaan, New York, in 1783. Her father died in her early childhood, and she was left an orphan at two years of age in the hands of a relative who kept the county jail — giving her a childhood of what Butterworth-Brown describe as "the bitter fare and ceaseless drudgery of domestic slavery." She grew up with a crushed spirit and "was a timid, shrinking woman as long as she lived." She married Timothy H. Brown, a house-painter of Ellington, Connecticut, and passed her days there and in Monson, Massachusetts.

## I Love to Steal Awhile Away

The story of this hymn is one of the most touching in hymn history. In her humble home in Ellington, Connecticut, caring for four children and a sick sister, Phebe Brown had established a quiet devotional habit: whenever she could find a moment at sunset, she would slip away from her burdens to rest and commune with God in a wealthy neighbor's large and beautiful flower garden.

A servant reported her visits to the mistress of the house, who called the "intruder" to account: "If you want anything, why don't you come in?" — followed by a plain hint that no stealthy person was welcome.

Wounded by the ill-natured rebuff, the sensitive woman sat down the next evening — it was August 1818 — with her baby in her lap, half-blinded by her tears, and wrote "An Apology for my Twilight Rambles." The result was nine stanzas of humble, tender verse that she sent to the neighbor herself.

The Rev. Dr. Charles Hyde of Ellington obtained a copy and, assisting Dr. Nettleton in compiling the *Village Hymns*, judged the verse worthy of a place. Hyde and his daughter Emeline gave it a few touches of rhythmic amendment. The original line:

> I love to steal awhile away
>   From little ones and care,

became:

> I love to steal awhile away
>   From every cumb'ring care.

## Musical and Thematic Context

Butterworth-Brown observe with characteristic warmth that "the poor who are 'remembered for what they have done' may have a larger place in history than many rich who did nothing." Phebe Brown would have "rated her history with 'the short and simple annals of the poor'" — but her brief, embarrassed poem found its way into the *Village Hymns* and from there into constant use. Crude and simple as it was, it "contained a germ of lyric beauty and life."

It is one of the clearest examples in hymn history of a hymn written not for publication but from personal necessity — the overflow of a wounded, devotional spirit that had nowhere else to go but into verse.
