# Anna Steele

Miss Anna Steele was born in 1706 at Broughton, Hampshire, in her father's parsonage, and in her father's parsonage she spent her entire life, dying there in November 1778. She was the daughter of a Baptist minister. She was for many years a severe sufferer from bodily illness, and bore a lasting grief: the loss of her intended husband, who was drowned the day before their appointed wedding. Butterworth-Brown note that her hymn "Father, Whate'er of Earthly Bliss" is said to have been written under the recent sorrow of that loss.

Butterworth-Brown note with amusement that for many years the surname "Steele" in hymnbooks mislead readers, who placed her in the same category as the essayist Sir Richard Steele — giving the knight "a credit he never sought." Only with the publication of stories of the hymns did many people learn that "Steele" meant a "very retiring English lady, a Baptist minister's daughter."

## Her Hymns

In 1760 and 1780 volumes of her works in verse and prose were published under her pen-name "Theodosia," and reprinted in 1863 as *Hymns, Psalms, and Poems, by Anna Steele*. "Father, Whate'er of Earthly Bliss" is estimated as her best, though some rank her "Dear Refuge of My Weary Soul" equally high.

The tune "Federal Street" owes its existence to a stanza of Anna Steele. Henry Kemble Oliver, the American composer, wrote the tune while reading Steele's line "So Fades the Lovely Blooming Flower," and named his composition for the street where he lived.

## Musical and Thematic Context

The "true musical mate" of "Father, Whate'er of Earthly Bliss," Butterworth-Brown note, "came to it probably about the time of its hundredth birthday; but it came to stay." That mate was [[Lowell_Mason]]'s "Naomi" — described as blending with it "like a symphony of nature."

Anna Steele exemplifies the tradition of the devout, hidden life that produces enduring devotional verse: a woman who never left her father's house, who suffered much and published modestly under a pseudonym, and whose hymns outlasted both her obscurity and her grief.

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Burrage corrects one detail supplied by earlier sources: Steele was born not in 1706 but in 1716, dying in November 1778 at the age of sixty-one. Her father was not merely a minister but William Steele, a timber merchant who served for thirty years as deacon and occasional preacher in the Broughton Baptist church, then for an equal period as its beloved unsalaried pastor. Anna became a church member in early life.

Burrage cites her father's diary entries showing how anxiously William Steele watched Anna's first publication take shape: "I entreat a gracious God, who enabled and stirred her up to such a work, to direct in it, and bless it for the good of many... I pray God to make it useful, and keep her humble." This is a touching window into Baptist family piety.

Dr. Hatfield (*Poets of the Church*) is quoted calling Steele "the female 'Poet of the Sanctuary'" and noting that "No one of the gentler sex has so largely contributed to the familiar hymnology of the church as the modest and retiring, but gifted and godly, Anne Steele." Burrage notes that more than one hundred of her hymns are found in modern compilations — more than any other Baptist hymn writer of any gender.

Her closing hours are described by Dr. Evans: "Though her feeble body was excruciated with pain, her mind was perfectly serene. She took a most affectionate leave of her weeping friends, and at length, the happy moment of her dismission arriving, she closed her eyes, and with these words upon her dying lips, 'I know that my Redeemer liveth,' gently fell asleep in Jesus."

Her tombstone in Broughton churchyard reads: *Silent the lyre, and dumb the tuneful tongue / That sung on earth her great Redeemer's praise; / But now in heaven she joins the angel's song, / In more harmonious, more exalted lays.*

## Hymnological Context (Duffield)

Duffield (1886) covers 20 of Steele's hymns — the largest single-author count for any woman in the book, a fact Duffield draws attention to explicitly. He ranks her "fourth or fifth in the list of contributors to English hymnody, being outnumbered, usually, by Watts, Doddridge, and Charles Wesley; and, occasionally, by Newton." This is a remarkable standing for a woman who published under a pseudonym and never left her father's house.

On **"Father, Whate'er of Earthly Bliss"**, Duffield had access to an original copy of the 1760 *Poems by Theodosia* and notes that in that edition the hymn begins differently: "When I survey life's varied scene / Amid the darkest hours." The common familiar version is a later editorial reshaping. The hymn is based at p. 134 of the 1760 volume.

On **"Dear Refuge of My Weary Soul"**, Duffield calls it "so excellent that very little of it" was cut from modern collections — the second, sixth, and seventh stanzas being the main omissions. Its original title in *Poems by Theodosia* is "God the Only Refuge of the Troubled Mind."

On **"Lord, When My Raptured Thought Surveys"** (fourteen stanzas in the original), Duffield quotes Steele's own diary voice: *"I enjoy a calm evening on the terrace walk,"* giving a rare glimpse into the quiet domestic world from which her meditative verse emerged.

On **"The Saviour! Oh, What Endless Charms!"**, Duffield acknowledges a criticism that her hymns are "occasionally like the songs of the daughters of Huzzab, who went 'tabering on their breasts' with dove-like lamentation" — but dismisses it: "these hymns cannot be spared." The defense captures the tension that has always surrounded Steele: her emotional register reads as excessive to some, as genuine to others.

Duffield's portrait is consistent: Steele is a woman of unusual output, unusual quality, and unusual obscurity — whose entire literary life happened within the radius of a country parsonage, and who nevertheless outlasted more celebrated contemporaries.
