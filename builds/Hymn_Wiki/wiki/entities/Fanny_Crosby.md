# Fanny Crosby

Blindness is not always an affliction. If it serves to give the soul a clearer vision of Christ and of His redeeming love, as it did with Fanny Crosby, it may rather be regarded as a blessing.

That is how Ryden opens his account of America's most famous hymn-writer. It is also how Fanny Crosby herself regarded her condition.

## Born into Darkness

Frances Jane Crosby was born of humble parents at Southeast, New York, on March 24, 1823. She was only six weeks old when, through the application of a poultice to her inflamed eyes, her sight was forever destroyed. Such a disaster would have cast a perpetual gloom over most lives, but not so with Fanny Crosby.

At eight years old she gave evidence not only of her happy optimism but also of her poetic genius when she wrote:

> O what a happy soul am I!
>   Although I cannot see,
> I am resolved that in this world
>   Contented I will be.
>
> How many blessings I enjoy,
>   That other people don't;
> To weep and sigh because I'm blind,
>   I cannot, and I won't!

When a Scotch minister later told her that it was "a great pity that the Master, when He showered so many gifts upon you, did not give you sight," she answered: *"Do you know that, if at birth I had been able to make one petition to my Creator, it would have been that I should be made blind? Because, when I get to heaven, the first face that shall ever gladden my sight will be that of my Saviour."*

## The New Mission

At fifteen she entered the Institution for the Blind in New York City, where she began to develop her remarkable talent for verse. At first she wrote secular songs --- one, "Rosalie, the Prairie Flower," brought her nearly $3,000 in royalties.

Strangely, it was not until she was forty-one years old that her first hymn was written. In 1864 she met the composer W. B. Bradbury, who asked her to try writing a hymn. She felt she had found her real mission in life and declared she was *"the happiest creature in all the land."*

For a long time she was under contract to furnish her publishers, Biglow & Main, with three hymns every week. By the time of her death in 1915, she had written an estimated 8,000 hymns and sacred songs.

## The Making of a Hymn

Most of her hymns were composed in a few minutes; often the lines came as rapidly as they could be dictated. This prolificacy led to criticism. The English hymnologist John Julian declared, with characteristic candor, that her hymns were "with few exceptions very weak and poor." Dr. S. W. Duffield compared her output to an oyster diver: more credit was due for occasionally finding a pearl than for bringing up so many shells.

But Duffield later revised his opinion, writing that he believed *"her talent will stand beside that of Watts or Wesley."*

And certainly there are pearls among the 8,000. The story of how "Safe in the arms of Jesus" was written illustrates her method. In 1868, the hymn composer William H. Doane came to her with a tune he had composed and asked her to write words for it. She listened as he played, then exclaimed: *"That says 'Safe in the arms of Jesus!'"* She went immediately to her room and within half an hour the words were written.

## The Hymns

Among her most beloved:

- **"Safe in the arms of Jesus"**
- **"Blessed assurance, Jesus is mine"**
- **"Pass me not, O gentle Saviour"**
- **"Rescue the perishing"**
- **"All the way my Saviour leads me"**
- **"Jesus is tenderly calling thee home"**
- **"I am thine, O Lord"**
- **"Jesus keep me near the Cross"**
- **"To God be the glory"**

Many of her hymns carry tender allusions to her blindness. "All the way my Saviour leads me" suggests how much a guiding hand means to the sightless. And the constant refrain of hope in "Some day the silver cord will break":

> And I shall see Him face to face,
> And tell the story --- Saved by grace.

## The Prayer

It was the prayer of Fanny Crosby that she might win a million souls for Christ. Many believe her prayer has been more than realized.

At a summer conference in Northfield, Massachusetts, when D. L. Moody asked her for a testimony, she rose quietly and recited what she called her "soul's poem" --- words she had never published:

> Some day the silver cord will break,
>   And I no more as now shall sing:
> But O, the joy when I shall wake
>   Within the palace of the King!
> And I shall see Him face to face,
> And tell the story --- Saved by grace.

The sight of her uplifted face, with its wistful expression, moved the vast audience to tears.

## Marriage and the Long Life

In 1858, Fanny Crosby married Alexander Van Alstyne, a blind musician. She is sometimes referred to as Mrs. Frances Jane Van Alstyne. She died on February 12, 1915, having lived ninety-two years in cheerful darkness. The first face that had ever delighted her sight was, at last, the face of her Saviour.

## Legacy

Fanny Crosby is the most productive hymn-writer in Christian history, and no one in American hymnody has been more loved or sung. Her hymns lack the poetic refinement of Watts or the theological depth of Wesley, but they possess something equally important: they are winsome, personal, and direct. They go straight to the heart, which is precisely where the gospel is meant to go.

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

Butterworth-Brown supply complementary details on two Crosby hymns.

For "Jesus Keep Me Near the Cross" (inspired by Col. 1:29), they note that the composer of the tune was William Howard Doane — born in Preston, Connecticut, February 3, 1831, trained at Woodstock Academy, holder of a Doctor of Music degree from Denison University (1875). They observe that Doane had "a mechanical as well as musical gift" and "patented more than seventy inventions," yet his interest in church music "never abated." He "is well known as a trainer of choirs and composer of some of the best modern devotional tunes," with his home in Cincinnati.

For "Speed Away! Speed Away!" — the missionary song — they record that it was written in 1890 to Woodbury's popular melody, "at the request of Ira D. Sankey." The key-word and refrain were adapted from Woodbury's original 1848 song, but "in substance and language the three hymn-stanzas are the new and independent work" of Crosby. Butterworth-Brown also note that Crosby began her association with sacred music through George F. Root, who was at one time musical instructor at the New York Institution for the Blind — supplying words to his popular song-tunes, including "Rosalie, the Prairie Flower" and "There's Music in the Air."
