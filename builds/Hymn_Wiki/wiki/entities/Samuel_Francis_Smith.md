# Samuel Francis Smith

Nearly a century after it was written, the national hymn "America" retained its hold on the hearts of the American people. Samuel Francis Smith will always be remembered as the author of *My country, 'tis of thee* --- but the New England pastor who gave his country its most beloved patriotic song also gave the Christian Church some of the choicest gems in her hymnody.

## A Student and a Winter's Day

Both of Smith's most famous compositions were written in the winter of 1832, when he was only twenty-four years old and a student at Andover Theological Seminary.

The circumstances of "America" are well known: Smith found a German tune among a collection of music sent by Lowell Mason, sat down to write English words for it, and within half an hour had produced the national hymn. He did not know the tune was already the melody of "God Save the King."

"The morning light is breaking" came from the same remarkable winter. At the time, news was arriving from Adoniram Judson in Burma that after years of painful disappointment, multitudes were turning to Christ. Smith was fired with hopeful enthusiasm, and in that spirit he wrote his immortal missionary hymn:

> The morning light is breaking;
>   The darkness disappears;
> The sons of earth are waking
>   To penitential tears...

## The Linguistic Marvel

Dr. Smith filled many important pulpits in New England during his long and illustrious career and at one time was a professor of modern languages. He was an unusual linguist, being familiar with fifteen tongues.

In 1894, a year before his death, he was still vigorous in mind and body, writing and preaching, eighty-six years old --- and was found looking for a textbook that would enable him to begin the study of Russian. In that same year he wrote one of his finest hymns, for a church dedication:

> Founded on Thee, our only Lord,
>   On Thee, the everlasting Rock,
> Thy Church shall stand as stands Thy Word,
>   Nor fear the storm, nor dread the shock.

He heard his missionary hymn sung by Burmans, Karens, and Telugus in Asia. He had heard "The morning light is breaking" in five or six different languages. A son of his became a missionary to the Burmans.

## The Hymns

Altogether Dr. Smith contributed nearly 150 hymns to American hymnody, many on missionary themes. Notable among them:

- **"My country, 'tis of thee"** ("America") --- the national hymn, 1832
- **"The morning light is breaking"** --- missionary hymn, 1832, compared to Heber's "From Greenland's icy mountains" in spiritual fervor
- **"The Lord's Prayer"** hymn setting
- **"Onward speed thy conquering flight"**

## Legacy

Samuel Francis Smith is that rare figure: a man who wrote something lasting before he was twenty-five, and then spent sixty more years proving it was not an accident. He gave his nation a hymn and his Church a missionary song in the same season of his life, from the same inspired winter.

He died in 1895 at eighty-seven, still vigorous, still learning.

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

Butterworth-Brown place "The Morning Light Is Breaking" in direct comparison to Heber's "From Greenland's Icy Mountains" — calling it "Not far behind Dr. Heber's *chef-d'oeuvre* in lyric merit." They note it "has gone farther and been more frequently sung than any other missionary hymn," with versions in Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, and French, as well as Chinese and several East Indian tongues and dialects, plus Swedish. They record the remarkable vignette of Smith visiting his son, a missionary in Burma, "of hearing it sung by native Christians in their language, and of being welcomed with an ovation when they knew who he was" — an event confirming the hymn's remarkable global reach in Smith's own lifetime.

The tune "Webb" — composed by [[George_James_Webb]] — carried the hymn into the missionary movement.

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Burrage places Smith firmly within the Baptist institutional story. He was born in Boston on October 21, 1808, educated at the Boston Latin School and then Harvard (class of 1829, classmate of Oliver Wendell Holmes), and graduated from Andover Theological Seminary in 1832. Following a stint editing the Baptist Missionary Magazine, he was ordained February 12, 1834 at the Baptist church in Waterville, Maine, where he also served as professor of modern languages at Waterville College (now Colby University).

In 1842 he moved to the First Baptist Church in Newton, Massachusetts, which became his permanent home. From 1842 to 1848 he edited the Christian Review. His most important compilation work was "The Psalmist" (1843), co-edited with Dr. Baron Stow — a hymn book of wide use in Baptist churches containing twenty-seven of Smith's own hymns. Colby University conferred the D.D. in 1853; in 1854 he resigned his pastorate to devote himself to the editorial work of the American Baptist Missionary Union.

Burrage provides a particularly rich account of the hymn "Yes, My Native Land, I Love Thee" — generally overshadowed by "America." Smith wrote it while at Andover after reading Home's "Letters on Missions," in a rush of missionary enthusiasm. It was used at the designation service for Rev. John Taylor Jones before his departure to Burma, printed on cards given out by missionary Dr. J. Perkins before he left for Persia, and recited impressively by Edward Judson (son of [[Adoniram_Judson]]) at the 1886 meeting of the American Baptist Missionary Union — who added that the hymn was dear to his honored father. Smith himself came close to going to the foreign field; instead, his son D. A. W. Smith, D.D., served for many years with the American Baptist Missionary Union in Burma and became president of the Theological Seminary at Rangoon.

Burrage notes that Smith wrote approximately one hundred hymns in total, many composed for special occasions such as ordinations and dedications, and that the hymn "Today the Savior Calls" — written while at Andover and later abridged by [[Thomas_Hastings]] for his Spiritual Songs — was suggested by a line from Schiller's "Maria Stuart."

### From Lorenz (1938)

Lorenz discusses Samuel F. Smith in Chapter XVIII among the first great American hymn writers. Still a twenty-four-year-old theological student, Smith wrote both "The morning light is breaking" and "My country, 'tis of thee" for the 1832 *Spiritual Songs for Social Worship*. The national hymn was suggested by Lowell Mason, who needed a patriotic song for his children's singing schools and supplied German music. Smith's eye fell on "Heil dir im Sieger-Kranz," written to the English tune "God Save the King" --- a fact Smith did not know. Lorenz calls the resulting hymn "practically an improvisation, not intended for wide use," yet "greatly superior to its only competitor for national honors, 'The Star-Spangled Banner,' because of its practicability in singing, its dignity, and its noble expression of the American spirit." Of "The morning light is breaking," Lorenz says it was inspired by the missionary spirit prevalent in the seminaries and is "the peer of Heber's 'From Greenland's icy mountains' as a missionary hymn."
