# Phillips Brooks

Phillips Brooks was a great man. Not only was he a giant in stature, but he possessed a great mind and a great heart. Also, he was a great preacher --- one of America's greatest --- and he just missed being a great poet. The flashes of genius revealed in the few verses he wrote indicate that he might have become famous as a hymn-writer had he chosen such a career.

## Memorized in Childhood

Phillips was brought up in a pious New England home. Every Sunday the children of the Brooks household were required to memorize a hymn, and when the father conducted evening devotion, the children recited them. When Phillips was ready for college, he could repeat no fewer than two hundred hymns from memory.

This childhood discipline proved to be of inestimable value in his ministry. He frequently made effective use of hymn quotations in his preaching. But more than that --- the unconscious training had made of him a poet.

## Christmas Eve in Bethlehem

"O little town of Bethlehem" was written for a Sunday school Christmas festival in 1868, when Brooks was rector of Holy Trinity Episcopal Church in Philadelphia. He was thirty-two years old. Three years earlier he had visited the Holy Land, and on Christmas Eve he had stood on the star-lit hills where the shepherds had watched their flocks. Below the hills he had seen the little town of Bethlehem, slumbering in the darkness just as it had done on the night when Jesus was born. He had later attended midnight services in the Church of the Nativity in Bethlehem itself.

He could never entirely forget the impressions of that sublime night. When asked in 1868 to write a Christmas hymn for his Sunday school, he put down on paper the song that had long been ringing in his mind:

> O little town of Bethlehem,
>   How still we see thee lie;
> Above thy deep and dreamless sleep
>   The silent stars go by;
> Yet in thy darkness shineth
>   The everlasting Light;
> The hopes and fears of all the years
>   Are met in thee tonight.

## The Tune Dreamed by an Angel

The beautiful tune "St. Louis," to which the hymn is universally sung, also has a remarkable story. Brooks asked Lewis H. Redner, his organist and Sunday school superintendent, to write a suitable tune. Redner waited for inspiration that never seemed to come. Christmas Eve arrived and he went to sleep without having written it.

In the middle of the night he dreamed that he heard angels singing. He awoke with the melody still sounding in his ears, quickly seized paper to write it down, and the next morning filled in the harmony. Redner always insisted that the tune was "a gift from heaven" --- and those who know its exquisite strains are more than willing to believe it.

## The Great Preacher of Boston

Brooks also wrote a number of other Christmas and Easter carols, including one with the striking refrain "Everywhere, everywhere, Christmas tonight." But it was as a preacher that he made his deepest mark.

When he began preaching at Trinity Church in Boston at thirty-four years of age, he was preaching the old-fashioned religion of "Christ and Him crucified" --- but presenting Christ in a new and wonderful light. Crowds began to fill the church. Even sedate old Harvard was stirred. His ministry became the most potent single factor in checking the spread of Unitarianism in New England.

When a little girl of five was told by her mother that "Bishop Brooks has gone to heaven," the child exclaimed: *"Oh, mamma, how happy the angels will be!"*

Brooks was elevated to a bishopric in his Church. He died in 1893.

## Legacy

Phillips Brooks gave the Church one great hymn and America's greatest pulpit ministry. "O little town of Bethlehem" endures because it was not composed at a desk but remembered from a hillside --- the hymn of a man who had stood in the actual darkness above the actual town and felt the actual weight of that night. No other Christmas carol quite equals it for quiet, wondering adoration.
