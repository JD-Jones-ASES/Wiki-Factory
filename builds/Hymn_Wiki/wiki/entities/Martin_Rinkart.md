# Martin Rinkart

## The Man Who Gave Thanks Amid Ruin

Martin Rinkart is primarily remembered for one hymn, but what a hymn it is: "Nun danket alle Gott" --- "Now Thank We All Our God." It has been called the Lutheran *Te Deum*, Germany's national hymn of thanksgiving, and no less grand a successor to [[Martin_Luther]]'s "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God." That such a hymn of radiant gratitude was written by a man who buried thousands of his own parishioners with his own hands is one of the most astonishing facts in the history of Christian song.

## Biographical Sketch

Rinkart was born in Eilenburg, Saxony, on April 23, 1586. He attended a Latin school in his hometown and then studied at the University of Leipzig. In 1617 --- the very opening year of the Thirty Years' War --- the town council of Eilenburg invited him home to become pastor of the church in the city of his birth.

Because Eilenburg was a walled city, it became a refuge for thousands who had fled the destruction of the surrounding countryside. This was a mercy that quickly became an overwhelming burden. Famine, overcrowding, and pestilence descended together. The other two pastors of the city died, leaving Rinkart alone to serve an impossibly large and suffering population.

## Burying the Dead

During the worst years of the plague, Rinkart is said to have conducted burial services for as many as forty or fifty people a day. In a single year he buried over 4,000 citizens of Eilenburg, including his own wife.

In 1637 and again in 1639, Swedish armies threatened to levy enormous ransoms on the already-devastated city. Rinkart went out personally to the Swedish camp to plead for his townspeople. When the general refused to relent, Rinkart turned to those with him and said: "Come, my children, we can find no mercy with men, let us take refuge with God." He knelt and led them in fervent prayer, then in singing Paul Eber's hymn "When in the Hour of Utmost Need" --- a beloved comfort of that era. The Swedish commander was so moved by the scene that he reduced his demand dramatically.

Rinkart outlived the war by only one year. He died in 1649, a worn and broken man.

## "Now Thank We All Our God"

The exact date of composition is uncertain and has been much debated. It was long assumed the hymn was written in thanksgiving for the Peace of Westphalia in 1648, since its first two stanzas paraphrase a passage from Ecclesiasticus 50:29-32 --- the very Scripture text on which regimental chaplains were ordered to preach at the peace celebrations.

More likely, however, the hymn was written years earlier. In Rinkart's own devotional volume, "Jesu Hertz-Büchlein," it appears under the title "Tisch-Gebetlein" --- a short prayer before meals --- and many believe it was originally written for his children at the family table.

The hymn has three stanzas: the first two are a warm paraphrase of Ecclesiasticus, the third is the ancient doxology *Gloria Patri*. In its simplicity and majesty it combines personal thanksgiving with cosmic praise.

> *Now thank we all our God,*
> *With hearts and hands and voices,*
> *Who wondrous things hath done,*
> *In whom His earth rejoices;*
> *Who from our mother's arms*
> *Hath blessed us on our way*
> *With countless gifts of love,*
> *And still is ours today.*

## Afterlife of the Hymn

The hymn quickly became Germany's great national hymn of thanksgiving. After the battle of Leuthen (1757), Frederick the Great's victorious troops raised its strains spontaneously, and even the mortally wounded joined in the singing. Following the Franco-Prussian War, it rang out from a parish church organ in the middle of the night as exhausted Prussian soldiers, billeted there after the battle of Sedan, found they could not sleep for excitement --- and found peace in its music. It was sung at the opening of Cologne Cathedral in 1880, at the laying of the cornerstone of the Berlin Parliament building in 1884, and as a *Te Deum* in English churches at the close of the Boer War in 1902.

Johann Sebastian Bach harmonized the melody in his Christmas Oratorio, and Felix Mendelssohn incorporated it into his "Lobgesang" (Hymn of Praise) symphony.

## Significance

Rinkart's life is one of the most powerful testimonies in church history to the sustaining power of faith through catastrophe. That a man who buried thousands of plague victims, faced down enemy armies on behalf of his people, and lost his own wife to disease could write a hymn so full of warm, unclouded gratitude --- that says everything about where his trust was anchored.

Ryden writes: "No hymn except Luther's famous 'A mighty Fortress is our God' has been used more generally in the Lutheran Church than Rinkart's glorious paean of praise."

Together with [[Johann_Heermann]], [[Paul_Gerhardt]], and [[Martin_Luther]], Rinkart represents the pinnacle of the German Lutheran hymn tradition born in the crucible of the [[Thirty_Years_War_Hymns|Thirty Years' War]].
