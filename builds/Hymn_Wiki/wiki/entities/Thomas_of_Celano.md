# Thomas of Celano

Thomas of Celano wrote what Ryden calls "the masterpiece among judgment hymns" --- the *Dies irae, dies illa* ("That day of wrath, that dreadful day"), one of the most powerful and influential pieces of sacred Latin verse ever written.

## The Friar Who Wrote About Judgment

Thomas was a Franciscan friar of the thirteenth century, a contemporary and biographer of Francis of Assisi. He is primarily known today in two capacities: as the author of the earliest biographies of St. Francis, and as the writer of the *Dies irae*.

The *Dies irae* is a meditation on the Day of Judgment --- the Last Day when all the dead will rise and stand before God. It is written with extraordinary dramatic force: the earth trembling, the heavens giving way, the great trump sounding, and then, after all the terror, a plea for mercy that comes from the depths of human vulnerability. The contrast between the majesty of divine judgment and the frailty of the sinful soul is the poem's great subject, and Thomas handles it with a power that no Latin poet had matched before him.

The poem was adapted into English by Walter Scott as "That day of wrath, that dreadful day," and its melody has been used by Verdi, Mozart, and Berlioz in their Requiem settings --- making it one of the most musically influential Latin texts ever written.

## Place in the Medieval Tradition

Thomas belongs to the high medieval period of Latin hymnody that Ryden calls the [[Golden_Age_of_Latin_Hymnody]]. He follows the two Bernards ([[Bernard_of_Clairvaux]] and [[Bernard_of_Cluny]]) and comes just before the tradition reaches its end with [[Thomas_Aquinas]].

The *Dies irae* was incorporated into the Latin Requiem Mass and remained there until the liturgical reforms of the 1960s. For seven centuries it was the standard text for funeral Masses throughout the Catholic world.

## Significance

Thomas of Celano stands as one of the master craftsmen of the medieval Latin tradition. His *Dies irae* is a poem of enormous emotional range: it begins in fear and ends in the prayer of a soul that has nothing left but need. That trajectory --- from awe to vulnerability to petition --- is one of the great movements of Christian prayer, and Thomas captured it with a skill that has never been surpassed in the genre.
