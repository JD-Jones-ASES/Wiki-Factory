# William Cowper

Paul once wrote to the Corinthians: *"God chose the weak things of the world, that he might put to shame the things that are strong."* In a very special sense this truth was exemplified in the life of William Cowper. If God ever made use of a frail instrument to glorify Himself, He did it in this man.

## A Fragile Soul

Cowper was born at Great Berkhamstead, England, in 1731, the son of a clergyman. His mother died when he was only six years old. Throughout his life he was tormented by a high-strung, sensitive mind that was forever on the edge of breaking. He described his own mental makeup with poignant self-awareness: *"The meshes of that fine network, the brain, are composed of such mere spinner's threads in me that when a long thought finds its way into them it buzzes, and twangs, and bustles about at such a rate as seems to threaten the whole contexture."*

## The Crisis of 1773

The most harrowing episode in Cowper's life came in 1773, two years after he and his friend [[John_Newton]] had begun working together on what would become the *Olney Hymns*. Newton had suggested the project partly to occupy Cowper's increasingly troubled mind.

That year, Cowper became obsessed with the idea that he was divinely commanded to drown himself in the River Ouse. He ordered a post-chaise and instructed the driver to take him to a particular spot by the water. When they arrived, he found a man sitting at the exact place where he had planned to leap in. He returned home and tried to take his life by other means, but each attempt was frustrated. Some unseen hand seemed to prevent it.

After his recovery from this dreadful experience, he was so struck by the reality of God's overruling providence that he was moved to write the hymn by which he is perhaps best known: "God moves in a mysterious way."

> God moves in a mysterious way,
>   His wonders to perform:
> He plants His footsteps in the sea,
>   And rides upon the storm.
>
> Blind unbelief is sure to err,
>   And scan His works in vain.
> God is His own interpreter,
>   And He will make it plain.

Many critics consider it the finest hymn ever written on the theme of divine providence. The irony is almost unbearable: the hymn about God's mysterious ways was written by a man who, in the depths of despair, had just been saved from himself by a stranger sitting on a riverbank.

## The Hymns

Cowper contributed sixty-six hymns to the *[[Olney_Hymns]]* collection of 1779. They are among the most personal and searching hymns in English, born from a life of genuine spiritual struggle. Notable among them:

- **"God moves in a mysterious way"**
- **"O for a closer walk with God"**
- **"Sometimes a light surprises"**
- **"Jesus, where'er Thy people meet"**
- **"There is a fountain filled with blood"**
- **"In holy contemplation"**

The last hymn in the list stirred controversy because of its vivid figurative language --- "a fountain filled with blood" --- which some found offensive. The American hymnist Ray Palmer defended it eloquently, pointing out that such criticism *"takes the words as if they were intended to be a literal prosaic statement. It forgets that what they express is not only poetry, but the poetry of intense and impassioned feeling."*

## Light and Shadow

God did spare Cowper's life, and he lived for twenty-seven years after the crisis of 1773. Some of those years were productive, even peaceful. He wrote celebrated secular poetry as well --- *The Task* was called "one of the wisest books ever written," and his comic poem "John Gilpin" was a delight. When Cowper was fifty-six years old, a cousin sent him a miniature portrait of his long-dead mother. He wrote in reply: *"I had rather possess my mother's picture than the richest jewel in the British crown; for I loved her with an affection that her death, fifty years since, has not in the least abated."*

But the darkness always returned. In 1797, three years before his death, he lost his lifelong companion and caretaker, Mrs. Morley Unwin, and sank again into deep gloom. His last poem, "The Castaway," written in March 1799, is one of the most desolate things ever written by a Christian. He felt that God had abandoned him. He was wrong.

Bishop Moule tells the story of his last moments: *"About half an hour before his death, his face, which had been wearing a sad and hopeless expression, suddenly lighted up with a look of wonder and inexpressible delight. It was as if he saw his Saviour, and as if he realized the blessed fact, 'I am not shut out of Heaven after all!'"*

The expression of holy surprise and joyful adoration remained on his face even as he lay in his coffin.

## Elizabeth Barrett Browning's Tribute

The poet Elizabeth Barrett Browning wrote a poem called "Cowper's Grave" that ends with these lines:

> *"O poets, from a maniac's tongue was poured the deathless singing!*
> *O Christians, at your cross of hope a hopeless hand was clinging!*
> *O men, this man in brotherhood your weary paths beguiling,*
> *Groaned inly while he taught you peace, and died while you were smiling."*

## Legacy

Cowper's life is perhaps the most paradoxical in all of hymnody: a man who doubted his own salvation, who suffered mental anguish beyond most people's comprehension, yet who gave the Church some of its most triumphant expressions of trust in God. He is proof that God can sing through broken instruments, and that what we feel is not always the measure of what is true.

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

Butterworth-Brown contribute compact but useful musical notes on two Cowper hymns.

For "O for a Closer Walk with God," they note two tunes "more or less closely identified" with it: William Gardiner's "Dedham" and "Mear," often attributed to Aaron Williams (a Welsh-descent psalmody composer, born 1734, died 1776) though possibly by "a minister of Boston of still earlier date." "Mear" appears in a small New England collection of 1726, and is "presumably an American tune." Both tunes, they observe, "have not worn out their harmony — or their fitness to Cowper's prayer."

For "What Various Hindrances We Meet," they note that "no one ever suffered more deeply the plaintive regret in the opening lines, or better wrought into poetic expression an argument for prayer." They quote the celebrated couplet "Satan trembles when he sees / The weakest saint upon his knees," and note that the whole hymn "is (or once was) so thoroughly learned by heart as to be fixed in the church among its household words." The tune is Lowell Mason's "Rockingham."

## Hymnological Context (Duffield)

Duffield (1886) covers 11 of Cowper's hymns, consistently drawing on the intimate biographical record to illuminate the hymns' origins.

On **"God Moves in a Mysterious Way"**, Duffield calls it "the greatest hymn ever written on the subject of divine Providence," noting that "much of its power" comes from "the circumstances which gave it birth" — Cowper's own mental breakdown and the paradox of a man who had lost faith in his own salvation writing the Church's most confident hymn about Providence.

On **"Oh, for a Closer Walk with God"**, Duffield provides Newton's philosophy of the Christian life as context: "When a Christian goes into the world, because he sees it is his call, yet, while he feels it a duty, to bear the cross." The Olney Collection's placement of this hymn alongside Newton's own on the same scripture text reflects their collaborative method.

On **"Sometimes a Light Surprises"**, Duffield records that this was "the last hymn that Rev. Robert Murray McCheyne seemed to comprehend upon his dying bed" — his sister reading it to him on March 21, 1843, four days before his death. This is one of the most poignant hymn anecdotes in the record.

On **"There Is a Fountain Filled with Blood"**, Duffield quotes an editor who "entirely rewrote the first verse" — recording an early example of the hymn's frequent textual revision — and notes the objections raised by some to the hymn's imagery.

On **"The Spirit Breathes upon the Word"**, Duffield dates the inspiration to July 1764, when Cowper opened a Bible lying on a bench in a garden and it fell to a passage that spoke directly to his condition.

Duffield's treatment of Cowper is consistently sympathetic, presenting him as a man whose hymns rose above his private anguish to speak universal Christian experience — a judgment still shared by all students of the field.

### From Lorenz (1938)

Lorenz discusses Cowper in Chapter XVI as Newton's famous co-worker on the *Olney Hymns*. Born in 1731, the son of one of George II's chaplains, Cowper was frail and shy, suffering a "very painful experience among the boys of the Westminster School" over ten years that Lorenz believes contributed to his later mental affliction. At thirty-six he moved to Olney, where Newton became his close friend, protector, and leader in hymn-writing. He contributed his hymns for the cottage prayer meetings that were a feature of Newton's work.

Lorenz observes that while Cowper's secular literary work shows no trace of melancholia --- being cheerful and even humorous --- his hymns frequently reveal it, notably "God moves in a mysterious way" and "O for a closer walk with God." Lorenz attributes part of this to Newton's habit of introspection and the obscurity of the occasions for which Cowper wrote, giving him "a sense of freedom in expressing his deeper, subconscious experience." Lorenz notes Cowper was "an exceedingly spiritual-minded man" of whom it was said, "Of all the men I ever heard pray, none equaled Mr. Cowper." He had a vivid conversion experience during which he reported being "too happy to sleep much." Lorenz calls Cowper's literary work preliminary studies for his later secular poetry, done after age fifty. His hymns --- "God moves in a mysterious way," "O for a closer walk with God," "Sometimes a light surprises," "There is a fountain filled with blood" --- Lorenz declares "will all survive as long as devout hearts meditate and sing."
