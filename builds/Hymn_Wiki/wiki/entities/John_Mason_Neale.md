# John Mason Neale

There are hymns we sing every Easter, every Christmas, every Sunday of Advent, that we would not have in English if not for the scholarship of one man. John Mason Neale spent his life reaching back through the centuries, through Greek and Latin manuscripts, and bringing the voices of the ancient Church into the living worship of his own day.

## The Scholar

Neale was born in London in 1818. When he was only five years old, his father died, and he received most of his early education from a gifted mother. At Cambridge University he became a brilliant student, leading his classes and winning numerous prizes. After graduation he was ordained as a minister of the Church of England.

His interest in the ancient hymns of the Christian Church led him to spend much time in the Eastern Mediterranean, particularly in Greece. He seems to have been enabled to catch the spirit of the Greek hymns to such a degree that his translations read almost like original compositions.

A story illustrates his extraordinary facility with verse. John Keble, author of *The Christian Year*, was visiting Neale one day. Neale stepped from the room briefly, returned, and exclaimed: "I thought, Keble, that all your poems in *The Christian Year* were original; but one of them, at least, seems to be a translation." He then handed Keble a very fine Latin rendering of one of Keble's own English poems --- made during his brief absence from the room.

## From the Greek

In order to do justice to the great Easter hymn of John of Damascus (8th century), Neale traveled to Athens and heard the "glorious old hymn of victory" sung by a great throng of worshipers at midnight on Easter. The result is his sublime translation:

> The day of resurrection!
>   Earth, tell it out abroad!
> The Passover of gladness,
>   The Passover of God!
> From death to Life eternal,
>   From earth unto the sky,
> Our Christ hath brought us over,
>   With hymns of victory.

Another very famous translation from the Greek is "Art thou weary, art thou languid?" --- based on a hymn by St. Stephen the Sabaite, a monk who lived near Bethlehem from the age of ten until his death at sixty-nine.

## From the Latin

Perhaps Neale's most celebrated translation is his rendering of Bernard of Cluny's immortal Latin hymn:

> Jerusalem, the golden,
>   With milk and honey blest!
> Beneath thy contemplation
>   Sink heart and voice oppressed:
> I know not, O I know not,
>   What blissful joys are there,
> What radiancy of glory,
>   What light beyond compare!

From this same source came "Brief life is here our portion" and "For thee, O dear, dear country" --- three glorious hymns from a single medieval source.

## Original Hymns and Good King Wenceslas

Neale also wrote a large number of original hymns, many for the holy days and festivals of the church year. He wrote "Good King Wenceslas" as a carol for the Feast of Stephen. His original hymns for Advent and the other seasons of the church year are among the most distinctive in English.

## High Church and Faithful

Because of his "high church" tendencies, shaped in part by the Oxford Movement, Neale was sometimes suspected of leaning toward Rome. But his hymns are clear. The evangelical note rings pure, and for this reason they continue to be loved and sung across all traditions.

Neale died August 6, 1866, at forty-eight years of age, trusting in the atoning blood of Christ. He died with the words of his own translation on his lips:

> If I still hold closely to Him,
>   What hath He at last?
> "Sorrow vanquished, labor ended,
>   Jordan passed."

## Legacy

Neale is unique in hymnody: his greatest contributions are not his own words but his ability to give ancient words a new voice. He is the bridge between the worship of the early Church and the pew-books of modern congregations. Every time a congregation sings "Jerusalem the golden" or "O come, O come, Emmanuel" or "The day of resurrection," they are singing through Neale's gift.

## Hymnological Context (Duffield)

Duffield (1886) covers 25 hymns attributed to Neale as translator — an entry count that understates his influence, since many hymns carrying only their original authors' names passed through Neale's translating hands. Duffield draws heavily on Neale's own scholarly apparatus: his *Medieval Hymns* (1851), *Hymns of the Eastern Church* (1862), and the *Hymnal Noted* (1856), treating these volumes as primary sources.

On **"Jerusalem the Golden"**, Duffield provides bibliographic detail: Neale's translation is taken from Bernard of Cluny's vast Latin poem "De Contemptu Mundi," which Duffield notes Neale himself divided into three parts — yielding the three separate English hymns "Jerusalem the Golden," "Brief Life Is Here Our Portion," and "For Thee, O Dear, Dear Country." Duffield notes that Neale was "struggling also with poverty" during the Cambridge years when this work was accomplished, and that "on eleven occasions he gained the Seatonian prize poem" — a detail showing the man behind the translations was himself a prize-winning poet of the first order.

On **"Art Thou Weary, Art Thou Languid?"**, Duffield records that the translation provoked scholarly controversy: Neale titled it an *"Idiomela, in the Week of the First Oblique Tone"* — explaining *"idiomela"* as "stanzas which are their own models" — a level of technical precision typical of his scholarly method. The literary use of the hymn is also noted: it appears in Sally Pratt McLean's story *Cape Cod Folks*.

On **"O Come, O Come, Emmanuel"** (listed as "Draw nigh, draw nigh, Immanuel"), Duffield traces it to "an anonymous Latin composition of the twelfth century" — first used in the *Hymnal Noted* (1856), then in *Hymns Ancient and Modern* (1861). This publication history shows how Neale's translations moved from scholarly pamphlets into the mainstream worship book of the Victorian Church of England.

On **"The Day of Resurrection"**, Duffield quotes Neale's own account of his visit to Athens to hear the Easter canon of John of Damascus sung "by a great throng of worshipers at midnight" — the experiential encounter that animated a translation of unusual power.

Duffield's treatment of Neale, taken across its 25 entries, presents him as neither primarily a hymnist nor a theologian but a philologist-priest: a man who understood that the Church's worship life was being impoverished by its ignorance of its own past, and who set out to remedy that with scholarly tools wielded in service of living faith.

### From Lorenz (1938)

Lorenz discusses Neale in Chapter XVII as a disciple of the Oxford Movement who, unlike Newman and Caswall, did not leave the English Church. Neale was "intensely interested in the liturgics of his church," which led to his studies of the early Greek breviaries. Lorenz calls him "the primate among those who found their inspiration in these ancient books of service and breathed into these ancient lyrics the breath of modern life." He brought to his translations "a literary skill, a spiritual insight, and a fervor" unmatched by others. Lorenz lists among his most notable successes: "Art thou weary, art thou languid?", "Christian, dost thou see them?", "The day is past and over," "Fierce was the wild billow," "'Tis the day of resurrection," "Brief life is here our portion," and "Jerusalem the golden." Lorenz emphasizes that these are not literal translations but "English hymns made up of ideas suggested by phrases in the originals" --- the work of "a poet imbued with devout feelings, responding to the vague suggestions of the often obscure originals."
