# George Frederick Root

George Frederick Root was, in the truest popular sense, the best-known American composer of the nineteenth century --- and the story of his most beloved Sunday-school tune crossing the Atlantic on an emigrant ship captures why.

## Farm Boy and Musician

Root was born in Sheffield, Massachusetts, in 1820, the eldest of eight children, and spent his youth on a farm. His genius for music drew him to Boston, where he became a pupil of [[Lowell_Mason]] and quickly advanced so far as to teach music himself and lead the choir at Park Street Church. He then went to New York as director of music in Dr. Deems's Church of the Strangers.

In 1852, after a year of study in Europe, he returned to New York and founded the Normal Musical Institute. In 1860 he removed to Chicago, where he spent the remainder of his life writing and publishing music. He died on August 6, 1895, in Maine.

## "When He Cometh" --- The Jewel Hymn

The hymn "When He Cometh, When He Cometh" was written by Rev. William Orcutt Cushing of Hingham, Massachusetts, who composed it in 1856 for his Sunday school, inspired by Malachi 3:17. Cushing must have had a subconsciousness of the tune he wanted, for the words and melody seem made for each other. Root composed the music while living in Reading, Massachusetts.

The most remarkable tribute to this hymn came when a minister returning from Europe visited the steerage of an English steamer crowded with emigrants from nearly every part of Europe. The steerage-master suggested he try "His Jewels," saying it was the one tune "everybody" knew. The minister struck out with the melody ---

> When He cometh, when He cometh
>   To make up His jewels ---

--- and scores of the poor passengers joined in. Voices chimed from everywhere, male and female, catching the air and sometimes the words, until the whole crowd of emigrants had it by heart. When the trains filled with new arrivals rolled away from Quebec, the song was swelling from nearly every car.

Butterworth and Brown suggest Root had "baptized the comic fugue" --- the melody bears resemblance to the old glee "Johnny Schmoker" --- and promoted it from the vaudeville stage to the Sunday school. Root himself never reaped a richer triumph than he shared with his poet-partner that day.

## Secular and War Songs

Root was equally at home in the secular world. His "Hazel Dell," "There's Music in the Air," and "Rosalie the Prairie Flower" were universal tunes (the latter two with words by [[Fanny_Crosby]]). His cantata *The Haymakers* was sung in shops and factories everywhere.

When the Civil War came, Root's music took the country by storm: "Shouting the Battle-cry of Freedom" and "Tramp, Tramp, Tramp, the Boys Are Marching" became the anthems of the Federal armies. The same muscular gift that wrote those marching songs also drove his gospel hymn "O Where Are the Reapers?" with its eager trip and tread.

## Musical Context (Metcalf)

Metcalf's *American Writers and Compilers of Sacred Music* (1925) gives Root a full chapter, drawing on personal letters and the memoir Root wrote shortly before his death.

Root was born at Sheffield, Massachusetts, August 30, 1820, the eldest of eight children. When he was six the family moved to North Reading, Massachusetts. A storekeeper, noticing his musical ability, sent him to study in Boston with A.N. Johnson and [[George_James_Webb]]. He later studied under [[Lowell_Mason]] and Benjamin F. Baker. At nineteen he had his own pupils and was leading choirs. He met Mason and Webb at the age of twenty.

In August 1845 he married Mary Olive Woodman, sister of the Boston musician Jonathan C. Woodman, who later composed the tune "State Street." In New York he taught harmony, voice, and piano at the New York Institution for the Blind --- where one of his pupils was [[Fanny_Crosby]], then twenty-three years old. "In 1845, before Miss Crosby became so widely known," Root wrote, "she and I were fellow teachers." Their collaboration over the following decades produced dozens of hymns that appeared in Root's publications. (Metcalf, p. 290)

In 1853 Root launched the Normal Musical Institutes --- summer training schools for music teachers --- in partnership with Mason and Webb. These institutes ran annually and shaped a generation of American church musicians. He moved to Chicago in 1859, entering the publishing firm Root and Cady with his brother. The Chicago fire of 1871 destroyed the firm but not his reputation. He was awarded the Doctor of Music degree in 1872.

Metcalf's hymnal survey found "Yarina" in 7 of 12 hymnals; "Rialto" in 5; and "The Shining Shore" in 4. The origin of "The Shining Shore" (for Pratt's "My days are gliding swiftly by") is traced to a conversation with Root's mother: she told him she could not decide whether to cross over when her time came or to remain with those she loved. "Go, Mother," he replied, "and I will come to you on the Shining Shore." He went to his desk and wrote the tune "Shining Shore" on the spot. (Metcalf, p. 291)

Root died August 6, 1895, at his summer home on Bailey's Island, Maine. Metcalf counted him, alongside Mason and Bradbury, as one of the three composers who "led the prevailing school of sacred music at the beginning of the last century." (Metcalf, p. 293)

## Legacy

Root was a pupil of Mason who outgrew his teacher in popular reach. He bridged the worlds of Sunday school, concert hall, and campfire, and his tunes were democratic in the best sense --- they belonged to whoever would sing them. Thousands of emigrants carried his "Precious Jewels" to homes across North America without ever knowing his name.
