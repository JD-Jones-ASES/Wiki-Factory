# Hilary of Poitiers

Hilary of Poitiers holds a quiet but significant place in the history of Christian song: he is generally recognized as the first person to write hymns in Latin verse. He was, in other words, the man who opened the door that [[Ambrose_of_Milan]] would later walk through so triumphantly.

## Who He Was

Hilary was Bishop of Poitiers in what is now western France. He died in 368 AD. The seventh-century scholar Isidore of Seville, writing several centuries after the fact, credited Hilary as "the first who flourished in composing hymns in verse" in the Latin-speaking Western Church.

Hilary himself tells us that he brought some of his hymns from the East --- he had been familiar with the rich Greek and Syriac hymnody of the Eastern church and recognized that the West needed something similar. His most famous Latin hymn is *Lucis largitor splendide*, a morning hymn praising God as the generous giver of light.

## Context: Why Latin Hymns Mattered

Before Hilary, the Western churches had used Greek hymns and Scriptural chants, often still sung in their original Greek. Latin was the language of the educated classes and of the liturgy, but it had not yet become the language of congregational song. Hilary's contribution was to begin translating the impulse of Eastern hymnody into the Western tongue.

His work was preliminary --- his hymns were never as widely popular as those of Ambrose, who came a generation later and brought far greater musical gifts to the task. But Hilary was the pioneer, and Ambrose was the one who made the path he opened into a highway.

## Significance

Ryden situates Hilary as the starter of the Latin hymn tradition, with Ambrose as its great fulfiller. In that relationship --- forerunner and accomplisher --- Hilary's role is genuinely important, even if his actual hymns are little remembered today. He saw the need for congregational song in the Western Church and took the first step toward meeting it.
