# Richard Baxter

Richard Baxter is one of the great unsung architects of English hymnody. Most histories of the subject leap from the Reformation Psalters to Isaac Watts, skipping a century of difficult, contested transition. Benson's account restores Baxter to his rightful place in that story: the man who did more than any other in the century before Watts to argue for, model, and popularize the singing of hymns of human composition in English Protestant worship.

## Life and Ejection

Baxter was born in 1615 in Rowton, Shropshire. A self-educated man of enormous industry and learning, he served as vicar of Kidderminster, where his ministry transformed the town over twenty years into one of the most celebrated examples of pastoral care and popular piety in seventeenth-century England. He was a moderate Puritan — Calvinist in soteriology but no enemy of episcopacy if it could be reformed — and spent much of his life attempting the impossible task of uniting the fractured Protestant world.

When the Act of Uniformity was passed in 1662, Baxter was among the approximately two thousand ministers who refused to conform and were ejected from their livings. He spent the rest of his life as an ejected Presbyterian — preaching and writing clandestinely, enduring persecution with great steadiness, imprisoned for over a year in 1685 by the savage Judge Jeffreys. He died in 1691.

## The Hymn-Singing Advocate

What Benson's account makes clear is that Baxter was the dominant intellectual force behind the movement to introduce hymns among the ejected Nonconformists. He was, as Benson puts it, "the most influential of all the 'Ejected' ministers" in pushing the case for congregational hymn singing at a time when both the established Church and much of Dissent resisted it.

Baxter wrote hymns himself. His *Poetical Fragments* (1681) contained seventy-two hymns, including his well-known "Lord, it belongs not to my care." He did not regard these as polished literary productions — he was writing for devotional use, for the strengthening of ordinary believers — but they circulated widely and gave him standing as a practitioner, not merely an advocate.

More significant was his role as "only begetter" of the hymn collections published by William Barton (1598-1678), a Leicester vicar. Benson establishes that Baxter was the moving spirit behind Barton's successive hymn-books, beginning with *A Century of Select Hymns* (1659), and that their aim was explicitly to provide English congregations with an alternative to the crude Sternhold-Hopkins Psalter. These books represent the first sustained attempt in England to introduce hymns of human composition for congregational use.

## The Context of the Psalmody Controversy

Baxter was writing and arguing in a world sharply divided between two positions. Calvin's Reformed tradition — which held sway in England and Scotland — maintained that only Scripture itself, in metrical form, could be sung in worship; all "human compositions" were excluded. Luther's tradition, by contrast, had embraced hymns from the beginning. Most of English Nonconformity had sided with Calvin.

Baxter argued for a middle position: Scripture could be versified in ways that genuinely expressed its meaning (rather than merely translating it word-for-word), and Christians should sing what their hearts actually believed. His case was theological and pastoral rather than merely practical. He saw the Psalter tradition as impoverishing English congregational worship — stripping it of the New Testament gospel and reducing it to a kind of Jewish worship that did not acknowledge Christ.

## Relationship to Watts

Benson is explicit that Baxter and his contemporaries prepared the soil that Watts ploughed. The older scholarship treated Watts as emerging suddenly and transforming everything; Benson shows that Watts had predecessors among the ejected Presbyterians and Independents, and that the movement they had been slowly building since the 1660s was what Watts, writing in the first decade of the eighteenth century, dramatically accelerated and completed.

Baxter did not live to see the success of the enterprise he had spent his life advancing. Watts was fourteen when Baxter died. But the connection is direct: Baxter's advocacy legitimized the hymn-singing impulse among the most educated and theologically serious Nonconformists; Watts gave that impulse its definitive expression.

## Legacy

Baxter's direct hymnic legacy is small. Only "Lord, it belongs not to my care" remains in common use today. But the institutional legacy is immense: he is the link between the Reformation heritage of Psalm-singing and the hymn-singing tradition that Watts inaugurated. Without the decades of advocacy, writing, and example that Baxter and his circle provided, the Church of England and English Nonconformity would have been far less ready to receive Watts when he came.
