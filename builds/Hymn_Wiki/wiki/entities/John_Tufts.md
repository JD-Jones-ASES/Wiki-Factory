# John Tufts

The Rev. John Tufts holds a quietly remarkable place in the story of American sacred music: he produced the first book of psalm tunes ever compiled in the colonies. Before his little pamphlet appeared, most congregations could count their known tunes on one hand, and all of them were learned by rote.

## The First American Tune Book

Tufts was born in Medford, Massachusetts, on May 5, 1689, the son of Captain Peter Tufts and Mercy Cotton. Through his mother he could trace his ancestry to the Rev. John Cotton, patriarch of New England. He graduated from Harvard College in 1708 and followed his ministerial ancestors into the church. In 1714 he was ordained as assistant pastor of the Second Church in Newbury, Massachusetts.

That same year --- or perhaps as late as 1721, the date is debated --- he published a small pamphlet of psalm tunes with instructions for singing by note. Its title was plain: *An Introduction to the Singing of Psalm-Tunes in a Plain and Easy Method*. The book was a daring innovation. Instead of conventional musical notes, Tufts placed the letters F, S, L, and M on the staff lines, standing for the solmization syllables Fa, Sol, La, Mi. A period after a letter indicated a half note; a colon indicated a whole note. Thirty-seven tunes were set in three parts --- cantus, medius, and bassus.

The system did not catch on permanently, but the book did. At least eleven editions were issued over the following twenty-five years. According to Metcalf, "Mr. Tufts' book was the entering wedge for the new way, and it was only a short time before his ideas, if not his methods, were adopted." (Metcalf, p. 17)

## Ministry and Controversy

For more than twenty years Tufts served the Second Church in Newbury. In 1737, however, accusations of impropriety were brought against him by some women of his parish. He declined to cooperate with the investigating council of ten ministers, and in March 1738 asked to be released from his pastoral duties. The church granted his request. He spent his remaining years in Amesbury, where he died in August 1750.

His personal life was more settled than his clerical end suggests. He had married Sarah Bradstreet, a daughter of Dr. Humphrey Bradstreet, on December 9, 1714 --- just months after his ordination. They had four children; his son Joshua graduated from Harvard in 1736 and became a minister himself.

## Legacy

The ten editions of Tufts' *Introduction* that appeared in twenty-five years are testimony to real demand. His letter-notation system was soon replaced by Thomas Walter's round-note approach, but Tufts' conviction that people could and should learn to sing by note was the conviction that transformed American worship music. He opened a door that never closed.
