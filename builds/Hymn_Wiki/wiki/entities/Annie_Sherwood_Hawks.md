# Annie Sherwood Hawks

Annie Sherwood Hawks was born in Hoosick, New York, in 1835. She became one of the most successful hymn-writers of the late nineteenth century, though she is primarily remembered for a single hymn of perennial power.

## I Need Thee Every Hour

Written in 1872, "I Need Thee Every Hour" is a hymn of simple, persistent dependence on God — the prayer of one who has learned that human sufficiency is an illusion and that the moment-by-moment need for divine grace is the truest account of the Christian life.

Mrs. Hawks sent the hymn — five stanzas — to Dr. [[Robert_Lowry]], who composed the tune and added a chorus to make it more effective in congregational use. It first appeared in a small collection of original songs prepared by Lowry and William Howard Doane for the National Baptist Sunday School Association, which met at Cincinnati, Ohio, in November 1872, and was sung there for the first time.

> I need Thee every hour,
>   Most gracious Lord,
> No tender voice like Thine
>   Can peace afford.
>
> *Chorus:*
> I need Thee, Oh, I need Thee,
> Every hour I need Thee;
> Oh, bless me now, my Saviour,
>   I come to Thee!

Butterworth-Brown note that the chorus was Lowry's contribution, and cite the hymn as "one instance, at least, of a hymn made doubly impressive by its chorus."

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Burrage establishes Mrs. Hawks's Baptist connections more precisely. She was born in Hoosick, New York, on May 28, 1835, and at the time of Burrage's writing had been a resident of Brooklyn for many years. It was there, around 1868, that her pastor — Rev. [[Robert_Lowry]], D.D. — discovered her gift as a hymn-writer and encouraged her to exercise it. The discovery of her talent and the first composition of her hymns thus grew directly out of her relationship with Lowry and his Hanson Place Baptist Church.

The hymn "I Need Thee Every Hour" was first sung at the National Baptist Sunday School Convention in Cincinnati, Ohio, on November 20, 1872 — a specific institutional Baptist occasion Burrage pinpoints more precisely than other accounts.

Burrage also preserves a story about the hymn's power that other accounts omit: the chaplain of the Massachusetts state prison at Concord, Rev. W. J. Batt, told how an ex-prisoner who had never had a home of his own prepared a simple but tasteful one, and invited the chaplain to help dedicate it. The man "with evident brokenness of spirit, for he was naturally a proud man and not unacquainted with larger surroundings, could not refrain from some criticism upon his poor things" — but his heart was so full that his embarrassment was only temporary. For the first number of the home dedication service he started the hymn "I Need Thee Every Hour." The chaplain wrote: "For me this hymn is likely always henceforth to be associated with the dedication of Mr. B's home."

Other hymns by Mrs. Hawks noted by Burrage: "I am the Lord's and he is mine," "Lord, let me live for thee, for thee," "Who'll be the next to follow Jesus," and "What can wash away my stain."
