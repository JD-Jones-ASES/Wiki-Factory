# Thomas Ken

If any single piece of Christian verse has been sung more times than any other, the best candidate is Thomas Ken's four-line doxology. Born in Berkhampstead, Hertfordshire, in July 1637, this slight, fearless bishop made his mark on the Church through plain speaking, personal purity, and one unforgettable refrain.

## The King's Chaplain

King Charles II, notoriously irreligious, appointed Ken as his chaplain --- and apparently respected him precisely because Ken refused to flatter him. "I must go in and hear Ken tell me my faults," the king used to say at chapel time. Macaulay recorded that "of all the prelates, he liked Ken the best." It was a remarkable tribute from a dissolute monarch to a man who made no pretense of approving his lifestyle.

## Seven Bishops and After

Under the Catholic James II, Ken joined the famous protest of the "Seven Bishops" against the king's Declaration of Indulgence. He was arrested, tried, and acquitted to popular rejoicing. But when William III came to the throne, Ken's absolute loyalty to his oath of allegiance cost him his place. He refused to swear allegiance to the new regime and was deprived of his bishopric. He retired in poverty to the home of Lord Viscount Weymouth in Longleat, where he spent a "serene and beloved old age." He died March 17, 1711, and was carried to his grave, by his own request, by "six of the poorest men in the parish."

## The Doxology

Ken's doxology --- "Praise God, from whom all blessings flow" --- began as the closing refrain of each of his three long hymns for Morning, Evening, and Midnight, printed in a *Prayer Manual* for the students of Winchester College. The "Evening Hymn" was said to draw scenic inspiration from "Heaven's Gate Hill" in Horningsham Park, which Ken could see on his walks to and from church.

The doxology's one limitation in universal use, Butterworth-Brown note, is its Trinitarian final line, which "cannot be universal, owing to its credal line being objectionable to non-Trinitarian Protestants." A simpler doxology by Nahum Tate --- "Be Thou, O God, exalted high" --- avoids this issue and is "suited to all occasions, to all Christian denominations, and to all places."

## Musical and Thematic Context

The tune "Tallis' Evening Hymn" has been associated with Ken's evening hymn text for more than 350 years. The tune predates Ken, composed by Thomas Tallis in the 16th century, and its dignity has made it the natural partner for the bishop's prayer. Butterworth-Brown note that Ken's "great doxology" is so often sung with "Old Hundred" that the pairing feels equally ancient and inevitable.

## Ken in the Anglican Hymnodic History (Benson)

Benson's *The English Hymn* (1915) places Ken in the opening chapter of the institutional history of English hymnody. Benson notes that Ken's Morning and Evening Hymns, while written for private devotion and not for congregational use, were "in a sense the first original hymns of the Church of England" — predating by decades the broader movement to introduce congregational hymn-singing into Anglican worship.

Ken's significance for Benson is institutional as well as individual. He wrote at the moment when the Church of England was most firmly committed to Psalms-only worship, and his hymns — circulated privately through the Winchester College community rather than imposed on a congregation — represent the earliest flowering of distinctively Anglican sacred verse. The Doxology, in Benson's account, became the most widely used single piece of congregational song in all of English Protestantism, bridging denominations that agreed on almost nothing else.

Benson also notes Ken's appearance in the story of Reginald Heber's proposed hymnal for the Church of England in the 1820s. Heber, seeking precedents for his project of providing the Anglican church with a proper hymnal, looked back to Ken as evidence that original hymns were not foreign to the Anglican tradition. Ken's Morning and Evening Hymns — by then over a century old — served as the earliest examples Heber could cite of authoritative Anglican sacred verse.


### From Lorenz (1938)

Lorenz places Bishop Thomas Ken (1637--1711) in Chapter XIV as a crucial figure in the development of the English congregational hymn. Closely allied to the Herbert school of religious lyrics, Ken had the advantage of belonging to a later generation in which the conception of the congregational hymn had begun to crystallize. Lorenz praises Ken's Morning and Evening Hymns as "both simple in structure --- in Ambrose's iambic long meter --- free from affectations and bizarre rhetoric, easily comprehensible, and devout and spiritual," accepted "as among the best hymns in the language." Of Ken's famous doxology, Lorenz writes: it "has been sung more frequently and with greater elevation of mind and heart than any other four lines in all earth's literature." He declares that with Ken, "English hymnody had at last found its voice, its spirit, and its model," establishing it as "a distinct department of English lyrical poetry."
