# George James Webb

George James Webb was born in England in 1803. He came to Boston, where he became one of the most active figures in American church music during the mid-nineteenth century. He died in 1887.

## The Tune "Webb"

The story of Webb's most famous composition is charming in its simplicity: he wrote the tune on board ship while crossing the Atlantic to America. The melody that came to him on the open sea was eventually named "Webb" in his honor, and also "The Morning Light Is Breaking" from the first line of the missionary hymn for which it became most famous.

Butterworth-Brown note that the tune was "borrowed" both for [[James_Montgomery]]'s "Hail to the Lord's Anointed" — a later tune assignment, along with Sebastian Wesley's "Aurelia" and the German "Ellacombe" — and for [[Samuel_Francis_Smith]]'s "The Morning Light Is Breaking," to which it is most permanently attached.

## Musical Context (Metcalf)

Metcalf's *American Writers and Compilers of Sacred Music* (1925) provides a full biographical account of Webb, derived partly from personal correspondence with Webb's daughter.

Webb was born June 24, 1803, at Rushmore Lodge, Wiltshire, near Salisbury, England. His father was a large landowner who, though musically untrained, was a good singer and wanted his children to have music instruction. His mother was a cultured musician who began his training before he was seven. He studied at Salisbury under Alexander Lucas (father of the later principal of the Royal Academy), learning piano and violin. At sixteen he returned to his father's house uncertain of his vocation. When asked, he chose music. He studied in Falmouth, quickly mastered the organ, and then heard of opportunities in America. He had booked passage to New York when a ship captain persuaded him to sail to Boston instead. Within weeks of arrival he was engaged as organist at the Old South Church. He served Boston churches for the next forty years, meeting Lowell Mason almost immediately and forming a partnership --- professional and eventually familial (Webb's daughter Mary married Mason's son William) --- that shaped American sacred music for a generation.

Webb collaborated with Mason on *The Psaltery* (1845), *The National Psalmist* (1848), and *Cantica Laudis* (1850). He compiled *Scripture Worship* (1834), *The Massachusetts Collection of Psalmody* (1840), and *The American Glee Book* (1841). He was president of the Handel and Haydn Society in 1840 when *The Massachusetts Collection* appeared. He also co-edited two musical journals. In 1871 he moved to Orange, New Jersey, the same town where Mason had settled, and remained there giving lessons until his death, October 7, 1887.

George F. Root, who worked with Webb in the Normal Institutes, called him "the best vocal teacher in Boston, and the most refined and delightful teacher of the English glee and madrigal that I have ever known, an elegant organist, an accomplished musician and a model Christian gentleman." (Metcalf, p. 135)

Of his many compositions, only the tune "Webb" ("Goodwin" in its original form) survived into common use. It was originally written to secular words ("'Tis dawn, the lark is singing"), then adapted for "The morning light is breaking" and given the name "Goodwin." After 1872 the name "Webb" was adopted. Of the twenty-six hymnals Metcalf examined, thirteen used it for Duffield's "Stand Up for Jesus"; nineteen used it for Smith's "The morning light is breaking." (Metcalf, p. 244-245)

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

Webb was a colleague of [[Lowell_Mason]] in Boston and shared Mason's commitment to improving the standard of church music in America. Together they helped transform American congregational singing from the rough traditions of early New England psalmody into something more polished and capable.

"Webb" in its simplicity and forward movement suited the evangelical optimism of the missionary era perfectly — a tune with the energy of a sea voyage, carrying words about the spread of the Gospel to the ends of the earth.
