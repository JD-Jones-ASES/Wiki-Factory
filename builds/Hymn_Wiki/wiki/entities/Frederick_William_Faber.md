# Frederick William Faber

Frederick William Faber was born in Yorkshire, England, in 1814. Educated at Oxford, he was drawn into the circle of the Oxford Movement and eventually, following his friend John Henry Newman, was received into the Roman Catholic Church. He became a priest and founded the London Oratory. He died in 1863.

Butterworth-Brown describe his literary gift as "a peculiar genius for putting golden thoughts into common words" — a quality that allowed his hymns, despite their Catholic devotional context, to cross denominational lines and become beloved in Protestant churches as well.

## There's a Wideness in God's Mercy

This hymn of generous, expansive grace became one of Faber's most widely sung:

> There's a wideness in God's mercy,
>   Like the wideness of the sea.

Its central image — the vast, unhurried mercy of God, broader than human fear and narrowness — gave it a universal appeal that transcended its author's particular religious tradition.

## Hark! Hark, My Soul!

This great pilgrimage hymn of the pilgrim church moving toward its heavenly home is described by Butterworth-Brown as one of Faber's finest. It generated two notable tunes: [[John_B_Dykes]]'s "Vox Angelica" in B flat — described as "a noble composition for choir or congregation" — and Henry Smart's "Pilgrim." Butterworth-Brown print both in adjacent pages as optional selections, suggesting Smart's "Pilgrim" may have "the more sympathetic melody," though "Vox Angelica" is the more frequently reprinted.

## Faith of Our Fathers

Faber's martial hymn of Catholic devotion — rooted in the memory of English Catholic martyrs — became, through its Protestant hymnbooks, a hymn of general Christian steadfastness. The tune most associated with it is "St. Catherine."

## Musical and Thematic Context

Faber is an unusual figure in the hymn tradition: a Roman Catholic priest whose hymns were enthusiastically adopted by Protestants. His warm emotional tone, his rich imagery, and his gift for the memorable phrase all contributed to this trans-denominational appeal. Butterworth-Brown note his "peculiar genius" — a phrase that captures the quality of his best work: not theological precision but devotional warmth, the kind that crosses every boundary the intellect constructs.

## Hymnological Context (Duffield)

Duffield (1886) covers 10 of Faber's hymns, and uses the entry for **"Sweet Saviour, Bless Us Ere We Go"** as the occasion for a full biographical sketch. He records that Faber was "of Huguenot origin, the seventh child of Thomas Henry Faber, Esq." — a detail that gives Faber a Protestant ancestry ironically at odds with his later conversion. Faber was "ardent, impulsive and poetical from his earliest years," and sorrow came early: his mother died in 1829, his father about four years later. After ordination as an Anglican clergyman he served a parish noted for "its intemperance and immorality," where his pastoral qualities found full scope before his conversion to Rome and removal to Birmingham (April 1849) and eventually the founding of the London Oratory.

On **"My God, How Wonderful Thou Art"**, Duffield quotes extensively from the hymn's stanzas to illustrate what he considers Faber's peak power as a devotional poet — lines that express the paradox of divine majesty accessible to human awe: *"Thus doth thy hospitable greatness lie / Outside us like a boundless sea; / We cannot lose ourselves where all is home."* Duffield calls this evidence that "no uninspired poet has ever written more grandly."

On **"O Paradise! O Paradise"**, Duffield calls it "one of the very loveliest things that Faber wrote" — linking it inseparably with Barnby's equally beautiful tune, a match as close as any in Victorian hymnody.

On **"There's a Wideness in God's Mercy"**, Duffield provides the source: the hymn is taken from Faber's poem entitled "Come to Jesus," which runs to thirteen stanzas — the familiar hymn being a selection from a longer meditation on divine mercy.

Duffield is careful to note Faber's textual methods: "Oh, See How Jesus Trusts Himself" is taken from "True Love," a poem of twenty-three stanzas — with Duffield noting that the later portion of the poem "runs into sentimentality and mawkishness," a frank critical judgment that illustrates both Faber's gift and its limit. The 10 entries collectively show a poet capable of extraordinary concentrated beauty who was not equally in command of the longer form.
