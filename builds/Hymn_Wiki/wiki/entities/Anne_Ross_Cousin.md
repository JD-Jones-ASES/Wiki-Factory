# Anne Ross Cousin

Mrs. Anne Ross (Cundell) Cousin was the daughter of David Ross Cundell, M.D., and the widow of the Rev. William Cousin of the Free Church of Scotland. She was born in Melrose (circa 1824) and died in 1906. She wrote many poems, most of which Butterworth-Brown describe as "beautiful meditations rather than lyrics suitable for public song."

## The Sands of Time Are Sinking

Her "Rutherford Hymn" stands apart from her other work. It is a meditation on the dying words and spirit of [[Samuel_Rutherford]], the seventeenth-century Scottish Covenanter who burned with longing for heaven — and whose exclamation "Glory shineth in Immanuel's Land" supplied the hymn's immortal refrain.

The hymn was first published in the *Christian Treasury* in 1857. Its opening stanza captures the quality that has kept it in use:

> The sands of time are sinking,
>   The dawn of heaven breaks,
> The summer morn I've sighed for —
>   The fair, sweet morn — awakes.
> Dark, dark hath been the midnight,
>   But dayspring is at hand;
> And glory, glory dwelleth
>   In Immanuel's land.

The personal imagery — "My nest hung in no forest / Of all this death-doomed shore" — reflects Rutherford's own fierce homesickness for heaven, and Cousin's deep imaginative identification with his experience.

## Musical and Thematic Context

The tender and appropriate choral in B flat named "Rutherford" was composed by Chrétien Urhan (D'Urhan), a French violinist and composer born at Montjoie about 1788, who died in Paris in 1845. It was doubtless named by those who fitted it to the words. Butterworth-Brown note that in the days of the hymn's more frequent use, people became accustomed to calling "The Sands of Time Are Sinking" simply "Rutherford's Hymn" — because Rutherford's dying words had supplied its unforgettable refrain.

The hymn appears in Peloubet's *Select Songs* and in the *Coronation Hymnal*.
