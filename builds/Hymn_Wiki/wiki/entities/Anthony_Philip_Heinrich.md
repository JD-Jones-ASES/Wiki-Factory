# Anthony Philip Heinrich

Anthony Philip Heinrich — widely known as "Father Heinrich" — was born in affluence in Schoenbuchel, Bohemia, March 11, 1781. He became the principal in an extensive banking house in Hamburg, and during business travels visited Malta, where he purchased a Cremona violin and immediately set about learning to play it. His travels brought him to Lisbon, then to America in 1818. He settled in Philadelphia for a time, directing music at the Southwick Theater, when he learned of the failure of his business house and was suddenly reduced to poverty.

## From Banker to Frontier Composer

From Philadelphia, Heinrich moved to Louisville, Kentucky, giving violin lessons to support himself. He lived for some time among the Indians at Bardstown — an experience that shaped his mature compositional identity. He became, as Metcalf describes him, "a species of musical Catlin, painting his dusky friends upon the musical staff, instead of upon canvas." The Library of Congress later recognized him as "the first to recognize the North American Indian as a fit subject for music in symphonic and choral works of large dimensions calling for an orchestra of almost Richard Straussian proportions, and indeed, the first to show, as a symphonic composer, pronounced nationalistic aspirations."

## Boston and New York

By 1832 Heinrich was in Boston, serving briefly as organist at the Old South Church. That year Nathaniel D. Gould published his *National Church Harmony* and included four of Heinrich's hymn tunes, one called "Antonia" (the Latin form of his own name, and also the name of his daughter).

By 1838 he had moved to New York, where a Boston musical publication reported on "his mighty 'Condor'" with admiration. He visited London — playing in the Drury Lane Orchestra for thirty-six shillings a week — then returned to Germany in search of his daughter Antonia, who had been left in the care of a relative when her mother died in Bohemia in 1814. He returned to America without finding her; she had followed him, and they eventually discovered each other.

In 1842 he took part in a Grand Musical Festival at the Broadway Tabernacle in New York, which became his home for most of his remaining years. He had finished seventy-five complete works including several operas when they were all destroyed by fire. Many of his compositions were written for orchestras so large they were never performed, and much remained in manuscript. A fortunate purchase by the Library of Congress placed his surviving work where it is accessible to researchers, along with letters, memoranda, and newspaper clippings.

He died in New York, May 3, 1861, having borne a final four months of serious illness "with Christian resignation." (Metcalf, p. 186-190)
