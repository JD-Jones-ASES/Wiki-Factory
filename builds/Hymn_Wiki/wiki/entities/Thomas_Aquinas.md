# Thomas Aquinas

Thomas Aquinas is best known as the greatest theologian of the medieval Catholic Church --- the man whose *Summa Theologica* defined Catholic intellectual tradition for centuries. But he was also a hymn writer, and it is in that capacity that he appears in Ryden's account of the history of Christian song.

## The Theologian as Poet

Thomas was an Italian Dominican friar, born in 1225 near Aquino in southern Italy. He studied under Albertus Magnus in Cologne and Paris, and became the defining voice of what is called Scholastic theology --- the great medieval project of harmonizing Christian faith with Aristotelian philosophy.

He was also asked by Pope Urban IV in 1264 to compose the liturgy for the newly established feast of Corpus Christi, the celebration of the Eucharist. For this occasion Thomas wrote *Lauda Sion salvatorem* --- "Praise, O Zion, thy salvation" --- a hymn of praise centered on the Eucharist that Ryden describes simply as "a glorious hymn of praise."

## Closing the Golden Age

Ryden places Thomas at the very end of the [[Golden_Age_of_Latin_Hymnody]] --- the last in a line that runs from [[Bernard_of_Clairvaux]] and [[Bernard_of_Cluny]] through [[Thomas_of_Celano]] and [[Adam_of_St_Victor]] to Thomas himself. "With these writers," Ryden writes, "the age of Latin hymnody is brought to a close."

There is a certain fitness in this. Thomas was the great synthesizer of the medieval intellectual tradition --- the man who tried to hold everything together in one vast system. That he should also write the last great hymn of the Latin Middle Ages is entirely appropriate. He was a man who brought things to completion.

## The Corpus Christi Hymns

Thomas's most enduring liturgical poetry is the set of hymns he wrote for Corpus Christi, which includes not only *Lauda Sion* but also the famous *Tantum ergo* (the last two stanzas of the longer *Pange lingua* --- not Fortunatus's hymn of the same name but Thomas's own). The *Tantum ergo* has been sung at Benediction of the Blessed Sacrament in Catholic churches ever since.

## Significance

Thomas Aquinas closes an era. The golden age of Latin hymnody that he brings to completion stretched from the eleventh century through the thirteenth, producing some of the greatest sacred poetry ever written in any language. After Thomas, the center of gravity in Christian hymnody would shift --- first to the vernacular songs of the Reformation, and then to the rich traditions of German, English, and eventually American hymnody that occupy the later chapters of Ryden's story.
