# Simeon Butler Marsh

Of all the tunes composed by Simeon Butler Marsh, only one has endured --- but what an endurance. "Martyn," written in 1834, has been set to Charles Wesley's "Jesus, Lover of My Soul" in virtually every English-language hymnal for nearly two centuries, and the pairing is now so established that one name instantly calls up the other.

## Life

Marsh was born on June 1, 1798, in Sherburne, New York, where his family had moved from Weathersfield, Connecticut, the previous year. His father was Eli Marsh, his mother Azubah Butler. He was a farm boy who joined a children's choir at the age of seven and felt the pull of music his whole life. At sixteen he found a music teacher; in 1817 he began teaching singing schools, which at that period were a fixture of country life across America.

The following year he met Dr. Thomas Hastings at his school in Geneva, New York, and "from him received much help and encouragement" (Metcalf, p. 225) --- a connection that set the pattern for his musical life. For thirty years he worked with congregations within the Albany Presbytery, teaching choirs and leading singing schools.

In 1837 he took up newspaper work, establishing *The Intelligencer* in Amsterdam, New York (later *The Recorder*), and later *The Sherburne News*. He also gave thirteen years of free music instruction to the children of Schenectady. His golden wedding was celebrated in 1870. After his wife died in 1873, he moved to Albany to live with his son, and died there on July 14, 1875.

## "Martyn"

The tune was written in 1834, though where it first appeared in print is uncertain. Metcalf found it in the *Plymouth Collection*, compiled by Henry Ward Beecher in 1855, where it was set to words of John Newton. But within a generation it had settled permanently with Wesley's text, "Jesus, Lover of My Soul," and there it has remained. Metcalf wrote: "During the last fifty years the joint product of Marsh and Wesley has appeared together in every hymnal." (Metcalf, p. 226)

The simplicity of the tune and the nobility of Wesley's words make the pairing unusually powerful. Marsh wrote other music, including two cantatas, but he would not have minded being remembered for "Martyn" alone.
