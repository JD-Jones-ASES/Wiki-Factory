# James Montgomery

James Montgomery, son of Rev. John Montgomery, was born at Irvine, Ayrshire, Scotland, on November 4, 1771, and educated at the Moravian Seminary at Fulneck, Yorkshire, England. He became the editor of the *Sheffield Iris*, and his pen was busy in non-professional as well as professional work until old age. He died in Sheffield on April 30, 1854.

His father and mother had gone to the West Indies as missionaries and died there. This missionary legacy burned in Montgomery's blood all his life and kindled his greatest hymns.

## Return to the Moravian Fold

Butterworth-Brown describe the deeply personal origin of one of Montgomery's finest hymns. After years in the "swim" of social excitements and ambitions, he came back at the age of forty-three to the little church of his boyhood, and was readmitted to the Moravian "meeting" at Fulneck. Out of that homecoming he wrote "People of the Living God":

> People of the living God,
>   I have sought the world around,
> Paths of sin and sorrow trod,
>   Peace and comfort nowhere found.

The melody exactly suited to the trochaic step of the home-song is "Whitman," composed for it by [[Lowell_Mason]]. Butterworth-Brown write that "few Christians, in America, we venture to say, could hear an instrument play 'Whitman' without mentally repeating Montgomery's words."

## Hail to the Lord's Anointed

Montgomery is described by Butterworth-Brown as "distinguished as the only layman besides Cowper among hymn-writers of the front rank in the English language." His missionary hymn "Hail to the Lord's Anointed" — really the 72nd Psalm in metre — was written as a Christmas ode and first sung on December 25, 1821, at a Moravian Convocation. In 1822 he recited it at a great missionary meeting in Liverpool, where Dr. Adam Clarke was so charmed that he inserted it in his famous *Commentary*. In no long time it found its way into general use.

The spirit of his missionary parents was Montgomery's Christian legacy, and "in exalted poetical moments it stirred him as the divine afflatus kindled the old prophets."

## Prayer Is the Soul's Sincere Desire

Butterworth-Brown describe this as one of "the imperishable" hymns on prayer, noting that "selections from almost any part of which are perfect definitions, and have been standard hymns on prayer for three generations." Its memorable couplet — "'Tis not the whole of life to live / Nor all of death to die" — is described as "haunting."

## Musical and Thematic Context

Butterworth-Brown describe Montgomery's literary career as "singularly successful," and write that "his hymns were too many to enumerate here, and the more enduring ones too familiar to need enumeration." They note that he "could say that he had written no line which, dying, he would wish to blot." For the tune to "Hail to the Lord's Anointed," later hymnals have chosen Sebastian Wesley's "Aurelia" and the German "Ellacombe." The tune "Webb" — originally written by George James Webb at sea on his way to America — has also been borrowed for this missionary hymn and for S.F. Smith's "The Morning Light is Breaking."

## Hymnological Context (Duffield)

Duffield (1886) treats 28 of Montgomery's hymns — giving him more entries than any other writer except Watts, Charles Wesley, and Newton. Duffield's precise cross-referencing of Montgomery's *Original Hymns* (1853) is particularly useful.

On **"Angels from the Realms of Glory"** (Original Hymns, No. 239, titled "Good Tidings of Great Joy"), Duffield confirms it was "written for a Christmas number of the *Sheffield Iris*" — clarifying the journalistic occasion of its composition.

On **"Hail to the Lord's Anointed"**, Duffield confirms the account of Dr. Adam Clarke being so pleased with the manuscript that "he begged it and printed it in his own commentary beside the 72nd Psalm." The hymn is placed in Original Hymns as one of the most significant missionary pieces.

On **"According to Thy Gracious Word"** (Original Hymns, No. 129, titled "This Do in Remembrance of Me — Luke 22:19"), Duffield observes that this communion hymn reveals Montgomery's Moravian formation — his love of the sacrament as intimate encounter rather than mere observance.

On **"Forever with the Lord"**, Duffield records a remarkable personal testimony: Montgomery himself said in the winter of 1849 that he had received more indications of approval for the verses beginning "Here in the body pent" than for "anything he had ever written, except the lines of 'Go to dark Gethsemane.'" The hymn was the favorite of Hugh McCalmont Cairns, Earl Cairns, Lord High Chancellor of England.

On **"Go to Dark Gethsemane"**, Duffield notes its first publication in the *Christian Psalmist*, 1825, and that it "has been much altered" from the original.

Duffield consistently praises the quality of Montgomery's devotional verse, noting that across 28 hymns his craftsmanship never falters — a tribute from one hymn scholar to what he evidently regarded as the most consistently fine body of work after Watts.

### From Lorenz (1938)

Lorenz discusses Montgomery (1771--1854) in Chapter XVII as a distinguished contemporary who was probably influenced by the literary impulses of the times. A Moravian layman and son of a Moravian minister, Montgomery was a professional writer and newspaper editor who did not publicly profess faith in Christ until age forty-two. Having served a full literary apprenticeship and formulated his theories of the hymn before beginning to write sacred verse, his hymns have "an average excellence and effectiveness that can be paralleled only by those of Bishop Heber." Lorenz quotes Montgomery's sharp critical eye from his *Christian Psalmist*: "The faults in ordinary hymns are vulgar phrases, low words, hard words... bad rhymes, or no rhymes where rhymes are expected, but above all numbers without cadence." Lorenz notes that he almost rewrote the Moravian hymnbook over twelve years' labor. Among his most widely used hymns Lorenz lists "Angels from the realms of glory," "Forever with the Lord," "Hail to the Lord's Anointed," "Prayer is the soul's sincere desire," and "In the hour of trial."
