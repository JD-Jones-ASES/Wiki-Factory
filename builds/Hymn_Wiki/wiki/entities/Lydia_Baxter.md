# Lydia Baxter

Lydia Baxter was born in Petersburgh, Rensselaer County, New York, September 2, 1809. In early life, through the labors of a Baptist home missionary, Rev. Eben Tucker, she became a Christian. Her conversion and that of a sister were followed by the organization of a Baptist church in her native town. In the fellowship of this church and its Sunday school she was trained in Christian work and developed an earnest Christian life.

## Life and Ministry from an Invalid's Bed

Her marriage to Col. John C. Baxter led to her removal to New York City, which became her permanent home. Here she and her husband worked together for those around them — he was led to Christ through her influence — and their home became a center of Christian hospitality and evangelism. For more than a generation, pastors, Sunday-school workers, missionaries, and colporteurs gathered there to talk of things pertaining to the kingdom.

During most of this long period, Mrs. Baxter was an invalid. Yet from her bed she worked for her Master, and Burrage applies to her the Lord's commendation from Mark's Gospel: "She hath done what she could." She died June 22, 1874.

## Her Hymns

A volume of her hymns, entitled *Gems by the Wayside*, was published in 1855 and had a large sale. Her later hymns, used in connection with the labors of Moody and Sankey, became more widely known:

- "On the banks beyond the river"
- "O! shall I wear a starless crown"
- "We are coming, blessed Savior"
- "By the gate they'll meet us"
- "The bright hills of glory"
- **"Take the name of Jesus with you"**

And the hymn that became her most famous, number 15 in Sankey's *Gospel Hymns*:

*There is a gate that stands ajar, / And through its portals gleaming, / A radiance from the cross afar, / The Savior's love revealing. / Oh, depth of mercy! can it be / That gate was left ajar for me?*

## The Story of the Dying Girl

Burrage records a moving account from the *Sunday School Times* concerning Maggie Lindsay of Aberdeen, Scotland, who was converted during the great Edinburgh revival of 1873, and whose last hours are inseparable from this hymn. Returning from Edinburgh by train on January 28, 1874, Maggie was caught in a fearful railroad collision. Left lying on the bank for several hours, she was finally taken to a nearby cottage. She was supposed to have been reading the hymn — the leaf was turned down at the words "The gate ajar for me," and the pages were stained with her heart's blood.

Lying with both limbs broken, a fractured skull, and other internal injuries, she yet sang with bleeding lips: "Oh, depth of mercy! can it be / That gate stands open wide for me? / For me! for me! for me!" — to the uncontrollable emotion of those beside her. Her chief concern was for the effect her scarred face would have on her mother. She was alone among strangers twelve hours; "alone, yet not alone," she said, "for Jesus is here." At last, unable to speak, whenever a hymn was sung there was a gurgling sound in her throat, as if she was trying to join in the song of praise.

## Baptist Tradition Context (Burrage)

Lydia Baxter exemplifies a recurring type in the Baptist hymn tradition: the woman of suffering whose very suffering becomes a school of faith, and whose faith produces hymns that reach farther than any comfortable writer could reach. The hymn "There Is a Gate That Stands Ajar" found its largest audience not in churches but in revival meetings — and the story of Maggie Lindsay's death is the kind of story that gave the revival tradition its power.
