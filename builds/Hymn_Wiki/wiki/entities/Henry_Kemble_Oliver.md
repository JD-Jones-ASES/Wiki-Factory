# Henry Kemble Oliver

Henry Kemble Oliver was a Massachusetts public official who also made a lasting contribution to American hymnody through the composition of the tune "Federal Street."

Oliver was born in Beverly, Massachusetts, in 1800 and had a distinguished public career: he served as Treasurer of Massachusetts and as Adjutant General of the state. He died in 1885.

## The Tune "Federal Street"

The origin of "Federal Street" illustrates the occasional, amateur nature of hymn-tune composition in nineteenth-century America. Oliver was reading the devotional verse of [[Anna_Steele]] — specifically her line "So Fades the Lovely Blooming Flower" — when the melody came to him. He named the tune after the street where he lived in Salem (or Boston).

"Federal Street" became primarily associated with [[Gerhard_Tersteegen]]'s "God Calling Yet, Shall I Not Hear?" — its quiet, dignified movement carrying the restrained appeal of that German pietist's invitation. Butterworth-Brown note it as one of the characteristic tunes of the American middle-period church.

## Musical Context (Metcalf)

Metcalf's *American Writers and Compilers of Sacred Music* (1925) gives Oliver a full chapter, tracing his remarkable double life as statesman and musician.

"Federal Street" was composed in 1832 --- one of Oliver's earliest compositions. Its origin is described by S.J. Barrows: one afternoon Oliver was sitting in his library in Salem reading Theodore Hook's novel *Passion and Principle*, an affecting story. Setting the book down and walking around the room, he thought of Anna Steele's hymn "So fades the lovely blooming flower" and particularly its last verse about patience and hope. An unbidden melody floated into his mind. He sat down at the piano, played the tune, added the harmonies, wrote it out, and threw it in a drawer, where it remained for two years.

When [[Lowell_Mason]] was teaching in Salem, he asked whether anyone had attempted musical composition. Oliver produced the tune, and Mason was so pleased that he asked permission to publish it in *The Boston Academy Collection of Church Music* (1836). The tune was set to the last stanza of Steele's hymn. In nine of thirty hymnals Metcalf examined, it was used for Joseph Grigg's hymn "Jesus, and Shall It Ever Be." Oliver also composed "Merton" during a church service: unable to find a suitable closing tune, he wrote out four parts during the sermon, handed them to his choir, and they sang it to the minister's satisfaction. When the minister later discovered Oliver had composed it during the service, he rebuked him --- but relented when reminded that he himself made notes on his sermon margins for later development.

Oliver was born November 24, 1800, in Beverly, Massachusetts, son of Samuel Oliver and Elizabeth Kemble. He began singing in the choir of Park Street Church, Boston, at age ten. He attended Boston Latin School, Phillips Andover, and then Dartmouth College, graduating in 1818 at sixteen. Harvard later honored him with an A.B. and A.M. degree in 1862, and Dartmouth granted him a Mus.D. in 1883.

His public career spanned six decades: Adjutant General of Massachusetts for four years, superintendent of the Atlantic Cotton Mills in Lawrence for ten years, mayor of Lawrence for one year, state treasurer during the Civil War, and the first chief of the Bureau of Labor for ten years. He then served four years as mayor of Salem before retiring to his Federal Street home, where he died August 12, 1885. Through it all, music held him captive: church organist for thirty-six years, school teacher for twenty-four, founder of the Old Mozart Association (1826), and connected with the Salem Glee Club for twenty years from 1832. (Metcalf, p. 231-234)

## Musical and Thematic Context

Oliver represents the tradition of the public man who contributed to sacred music as a private avocation — a tradition particularly strong in New England, where civic and religious life were so closely intertwined that a state treasurer might reasonably compose a hymn tune in the intervals of his official duties. "Federal Street" outlasted his public career.
