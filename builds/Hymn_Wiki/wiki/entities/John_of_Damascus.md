# John of Damascus

John of Damascus gave the Christian Church two of its most beloved Easter hymns: "The day of resurrection" and "Come, ye faithful, raise the strain." For that gift alone, he deserves an honored place in the history of Christian worship.

## From the Court to the Cloister

John was born in Damascus into a prominent Christian family that had served the Umayyad caliphate. He held a position of significant responsibility at the court of the caliph --- a remarkable situation for a Christian in the Muslim-ruled city. But at some point, drawn by a deeper calling, he forsook this brilliant career and entered a monastery.

The monastery he chose was the great Lavra of St. Sabas, founded in 520 AD, perched in one of the wild gorges near the brook Kedron, not far from Bethlehem, with the Dead Sea visible in the distance below. He brought with him his ten-year-old nephew, Stephen --- who grew up within those walls and became, in his own right, a gifted hymn writer.

## The Easter Hymns

John's two great Easter hymns have been sung in Eastern and Western churches ever since he wrote them. Both were later translated into English by [[John_Mason_Neale]] in the nineteenth century, through whom they entered Protestant worship.

**"The day of resurrection"** is one of the great Easter morning songs of the Christian tradition. It rings with the confidence and joy of the empty tomb.

**"Come, ye faithful, raise the strain"** has the quality of a great Easter processional --- strong, forward-moving, and full of Old Testament imagery about liberation and new life. It was one of Neale's most celebrated translations.

John also wrote a remarkable hymn on the angels for St. Michael's Day, which begins with the vivid image:

> Stars of the morning, so gloriously bright,
> Filled with celestial resplendence and light,
> These that, where night never followeth day,
> Raise the "Thrice Holy, Lord!" ever and aye.

## The Nephew

John's nephew [[Stephen_the_Sabaite]], who grew up in the monastery with him, is the author of another hymn made famous by Neale's translation: "Art thou weary, art thou languid?" Stephen died in 794 AD. The two men together represent a remarkable family legacy of sacred poetry within the walls of the same monastery.

## Significance

John of Damascus stands at the end of the great era of Greek hymnody. After him, Ryden notes, the last important figure is [[Joseph_the_Hymnographer]] in the ninth century. But John's contribution is secure: two Easter hymns of extraordinary vitality that have been singing in churches for twelve hundred years and show no signs of stopping.

His significance is also tied to [[John_Mason_Neale]], the great Victorian translator who rescued John's hymns --- and those of many other early Greek writers --- from Latin scholarly editions and gave them to English-speaking congregations. Without Neale, few modern worshipers would know the name John of Damascus. With Neale, his Easter hymns are sung every spring around the world.
