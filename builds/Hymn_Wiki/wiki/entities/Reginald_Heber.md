# Reginald Heber

Alfred Tennyson, who had seen the best English poetry of his century, called "Holy, holy, holy, Lord God Almighty" the world's greatest hymn. The man who wrote it was Reginald Heber, missionary bishop of Calcutta, and the words were only part of a gift he gave the Church.

## Early Gifts

Born April 21, 1783, at Malpas, Cheshire, England, Heber was educated at Oxford, where he formed an early friendship with Sir Walter Scott. His gift for poetry showed itself quickly. When he won a university prize for a poem on Palestine --- he was only seventeen --- his parents found him on his knees in grateful prayer after the announcement. Walter Scott had actually suggested one of the poem's most striking lines.

For sixteen years Heber served quietly in the obscure parish of Hodnet as a minister of the Church of England. It was during this long, patient period of country ministry that all of his hymns were written. All the while, he nursed a secret longing for India. He would work out imaginary journeys on the map, hoping that someday he might become bishop of Calcutta.

## "From Greenland's Icy Mountains"

The story of how his famous missionary hymn was written is one of the best in all of hymnody. It was 1819, and Heber was visiting his father-in-law, Dr. Shipley, vicar and dean of Wrexham, on the Saturday before Whitsunday. The dean was planning a missionary sermon for the following morning and asked young Heber if he could write a missionary hymn to be sung at the service. Heber withdrew to another part of the room.

After a while the dean asked, "What have you written?" Heber read the first three stanzas. The dean was satisfied, but the poet shook his head: "No, no, the sense is not complete." He added the fourth verse --- "Waft, waft, ye winds, His story" --- and the greatest missionary hymn of the ages was born.

Even the tune has a remarkable story. A Christian woman in Savannah, Georgia, obtained a copy of Heber's words but could find no music to fit the unusual meter. She sent the poem to a young bank clerk said to be gifted as a composer. Within half an hour it was returned to her with the beautiful tune "Missionary Hymn." The young bank clerk was Lowell Mason, who would become one of America's greatest hymn composers. Words and music, both written almost in a moment.

## Bishop of Calcutta

In 1822, his earnest prayer was answered: he was called to the episcopate as Bishop of Calcutta. After only three years of arduous work in India, during which time he ordained the first native pastor of the Episcopal Church --- a man named Christian David --- the life of the gifted bishop was cut short. He was forty-three years old.

## The Hymn of Pure Adoration

It was Tennyson's estimate that "Holy, holy, holy, Lord God Almighty" was the world's greatest hymn. What distinguishes it is that it contains nothing of confession, petition, or thanksgiving --- only worship. Pure adoration, from beginning to end. Its language is Scriptural throughout. The tune "Nicaea," composed by the English musician Rev. John B. Dykes, matches the hymn itself in majesty.

Altogether Heber wrote fifty-seven hymns, all published in a single collection after his death. It is said that every one of them is still in use --- a tribute without parallel in hymnody.

His other major hymns include:

- **"Holy, holy, holy, Lord God Almighty"** --- Tennyson's greatest hymn
- **"From Greenland's icy mountains"** --- the great missionary hymn
- **"Brightest and best of the sons of the morning"** --- rich in imagery
- **"The Son of God goes forth to war"**
- **"Bread of the world in mercy broken"**
- **"By cool Siloam's shady rill"**

## Legacy

"One of the best of English gentlemen," Thackeray called him. He was not famous in his lifetime; it was only after his death that he leaped into fame through his hymns. The man who had patiently waited sixteen years in an obscure parish for a call to India left behind, in his fifty-seven hymns, one of the most perfect collections in English hymnody --- a collection in which, uniquely, not a single hymn has gone out of use.

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

Butterworth-Brown retell the story of "From Greenland's Icy Mountains" with vivid detail. On that Saturday afternoon in 1819 at the Wrexham Vicarage, Heber returned from the far end of the room with three stanzas, which were "hailed with delighted approval" — but the poet insisted upon adding another, and came back with "Waft, waft, ye winds, His story." By common consent, they write, Heber's "Missionary Hymn" is "the silver trumpet among all the rallying bugles of the church."

The tune's origin they describe as "a record of providential birth quite as interesting as that of the hymn." In 1823 a lady in Savannah, Georgia, having received a copy of Heber's lyric from England, wanted to sing it but knew no music to fit the metre. She sent her son to a young bank clerk nearby, Lowell Mason by name, who "sometimes wrote music for recreation." The boy returned in half an hour with the composition. In the words of Dr. Charles Robinson: "Like the hymn it voices, it was done at a stroke, and it will last through the ages."

## Hymnological Context (Duffield)

Duffield (1886) treats 12 of Heber's hymns, providing important compositional and biographical details not found in other sources.

On **"From Greenland's Icy Mountains"** (1819), Duffield confirms the celebrated account of its composition at the old vicarage, with Heber asked to provide verses for the close of the morning service. The hymn was written on the spot, and the lines "Waft, waft, ye winds, his story" were added just afterward. Duffield also records a remarkable detail about the second stanza: Heber's own comment that "in the Straits of Malacca a smell like that of a hawthorn hedge is commonly experienced; and from Ceylon, at thirty or forty miles, under certain circumstances, a yet more agreeable scent is inhaled" — verifying the accuracy of the "spicy breezes" image. He also records that in 1852, two missionaries were sent to represent a South Carolina church directly inspired by the hymn. The hymn experienced only one change from Heber's original.

On **"Holy, Holy, Holy, Lord God Almighty"**, the hymn appears in Heber's posthumous collection of 1827, published by Mrs. Heber after her husband's death. Duffield notes that whoever wrote a lesser-known Moravian hymn to the same theme "has the real spirit of adoration in it, beyond almost any of our best lyrics" — a generous comparison that implies Duffield shared Tennyson's high estimate of the genre.

On **"The Son of God Goes Forth to War"** (1827), Duffield notes it was written for the Feast of St. Stephen. He confirms that Heber's first publication of hymns was in 1812 and that his original design was to furnish pieces for the entire Church year.

On **"Thou Art Gone to the Grave"**, Duffield gives a precise circumstance: it was composed in December 1818 upon the death of Heber's only child, at the age of six months. Heber wrote that at the moment of her death, "I felt almost overwhelmed."

On **"Lord of Mercy and of Might"**, Duffield records the remarkable manner of Heber's death — that his servant, finding him unusually delayed, opened the door and found him dead. The hymn is noted as possibly a translation from a Latin hymn of the eighth century.
