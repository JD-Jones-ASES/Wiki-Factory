# Thomas Hastings

High among the names of those who in the early days of America labored to raise the standard of hymnody stands Thomas Hastings, Doctor of Music. Poet and musician by nature, Hastings devoted his entire life to the elevation of sacred song --- and the Church has been singing his melodies ever since.

## Pioneer and Pioneer's Son

The story of his life is typical of the struggles of many American pioneers. Born at Washington, Connecticut, on October 15, 1784, young Hastings moved with his parents to Clinton, New York, when he was only twelve years old. The journey was made in ox-sleds through unbroken wilderness in the dead of winter.

The frontier schools of those days offered little opportunity for education, but the eager boy trudged six miles each day to receive what instruction was given. A passionate fondness for music was first satisfied when he secured a musical primer of four pages, costing six pence. The proudest moment of his childhood came when he was named leader of the village choir.

It was not until he was thirty-two years old that Hastings was able to secure employment as a music teacher. But from that point until his death in 1872, he devoted all his energies to the work he loved.

## The Tireless Worker

Hastings was ever tireless in contending that good music should have a recognized place in religious worship. From 1823 to 1832 he edited the *Western Recorder* in Utica, New York, spreading his views on sacred music. In the latter year, twelve churches in New York City jointly engaged his services as choir director, and for the rest of his life the great American metropolis was his home.

Though seriously handicapped by eye trouble, Hastings produced a prodigious amount of work. It is claimed that he wrote more than one thousand hymn tunes and published fifty volumes of church music.

## The Tunes

Some of the finest tunes in American hymnals were composed by Hastings. Two above all deserve mention:

**"Toplady"** --- the haunting, powerful tune to which "Rock of Ages" is universally sung, and which has endeared [[Augustus_Toplady]]'s great hymn to millions of hearts. The tune and the text have become inseparable.

**"Ortonville"** --- the sweet and haunting melody that has made so many hymns more beloved. Ryden asks: *"Who has not found inspiration in singing that sweet and haunting melody known as 'Ortonville'?"*

Besides these, there are at least a score of other beautiful hymn tunes that have been loved by the singing Church for nearly a century.

## The Hymn Texts

Through composing tunes, Hastings was led to write words for hymns as well. More than 600 texts are attributed to him, though many were written anonymously. "Hail to the brightness of Zion's glad morning" is generally regarded as his finest. It strikingly reflects the missionary spirit of his age.

Another stirring missionary hymn, written in 1831, opens:

> Now be the gospel banner
>   In every land unfurled;
> And be the shout, Hosannah!
>   Reechoed through the world...

His hymn "Gently, Lord, O gently lead us" is singularly beautiful in its tenderness:

> Gently, Lord, O gently lead us,
>   Pilgrims in this vale of tears...

## The Long Faithfulness

Hastings did not cease writing and composing until three days before his death. It is said that more of his hymns are found in the standard church hymnals of America than those of any other American writer. Their survival through nearly a century is testimony to their enduring quality.

He died in 1872, at the age of eighty-seven, having made music for the Church from a musical primer of four pages to a legacy of fifty volumes and a thousand tunes.

## Legacy

Thomas Hastings may be the least famous indispensable figure in American hymnody. His name is not widely known, but his tunes are sung every Sunday. Every congregation that has ever sung "Rock of Ages" to his melody of "Toplady" has received his gift, often without knowing it.

## Musical Context (Metcalf)

Thomas Hastings is the subject of a full chapter in Metcalf's *American Writers and Compilers of Sacred Music* (1925). Metcalf places him alongside Lowell Mason as the two men to whom "a larger proportion of the psalm tunes of American origin now in common use among Protestant peoples" are owed than to any other pair. (Metcalf, p. 195)

Hastings was born October 15, 1784, at Washington, Connecticut, son of Seth, a country physician and farmer. At twelve the family moved to Clinton, New York; at eighteen he was leading the village choir. He had only a country-school education. In 1828 he moved to Utica to edit *The Western Recorder*, a weekly religious paper, through which he expressed his musical opinions. In 1832 twelve New York City churches combined to secure his services as choir director. His son Thomas S., later president of Union Theological Seminary, remembered him as "a devout and earnest Christian, a hard student, and a resolute worker, not laying aside his pen until three days before his death." (Metcalf, p. 195-196) The University of the City of New York conferred the degree of Doctor of Music upon him in 1858.

Metcalf catalogs his output: six hundred hymn texts, roughly one thousand hymn tunes, fifty volumes of music. Of his most-used tunes, Metcalf found in a survey of a dozen hymnals that "Toplady" appeared in every one; "Ortonville" in ten; "Retreat" in nine; and "Zion" in eight. He sometimes published under pseudonyms, including "Kl if," explaining that "a foreigner's name went a great way, and that very ordinary tunes would be sung if 'Palestrina' or 'Pucitto' were over them, while a better tune by Hastings would go unnoticed." (Metcalf, p. 198)

Metcalf also notes a charming personal detail: Hastings and two of his brothers were complete albinos, with hair "entirely destitute of color so that he looked old while he was still young." He was nearsighted, and when directing classes would bow his head close to the book, following the music as his eyes moved across the page --- yet he was able to direct with the book held either way up. (Metcalf, p. 198-199)

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

Butterworth-Brown devote considerable space to Hastings's central role in making hymns known "by sound" rather than name. On the tune "Toplady" for "Rock of Ages," they observe: "Almost anywhere to call for the tune of 'Toplady' (namesake of the pious poet) is as unintelligible to the multitude as 'Key' would be to designate the 'Star-spangled Banner.' The common people — thanks to Dr. Hastings — have learned 'Rock of Ages' by *sound*." Hastings wrote the tune in 1830.

They also chronicle his role as the editor who transformed Thomas Moore's poem "Come Ye Disconsolate" into a usable evangelical hymn. Moore's original had lines like "Go ask the infidel what boon he brings us" — unsuitable for congregational use. Hastings changed the second line from "Come, at God's altar fervently kneel" to "Come to the mercy seat," rewrote the second stanza's key line, and replaced almost the entire final stanza. Their verdict is characteristically sharp: "Dr. Hastings was not much of a poet, but he could make a *singable* hymn." They add, charitably, that "it was to Moore, however, that the real hymn-thought and key-note first came, and the title-line and the sweet refrain are his own — for which the Christian world has thanked him, lo these many years." The tune was Samuel Webbe's, which came with the poem already attached and apparently satisfied Hastings.

Hastings's tune "Zion" (composed in Washington, CT, 1830) is noted as the standard musical companion for William Williams's Welsh missionary hymn "O'er the Gloomy Hills of Darkness," and his tune "Halle" for Keble's "Sun of My Soul" was superseded by William Henry Monk's "Hursley." His "Ortonville" (also 1830) has served as the companion melody for dozens of hymns across more than a century.
