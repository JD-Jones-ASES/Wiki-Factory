# Stephen Collins Foster

Stephen Collins Foster is the father of American popular song --- the first composer in the new nation to create music that the whole country knew and loved. His connection to sacred music is less known, but real.

## Life

Foster was born on July 4, 1826, at Lawrenceburg (now part of Pittsburgh), Pennsylvania. His mother, of English ancestry from Maryland, gave him his taste for poetry and music; his father was of Irish descent, a founder of the town of Lawrenceburg. Without a teacher, Foster learned to play guitar and flute, and at thirteen composed a waltz for four flutes performed at a school commencement. He had little formal education, relying on private tutors.

He married Jane Denny McDowell in 1850. Most of his later years were spent alternating between Pittsburgh and New York. He died in New York on January 13, 1864, from the effects of a fall, at the age of thirty-seven. His body was carried to Pittsburgh at the expense of the Pennsylvania Railroad. He left one daughter, Marion, later Mrs. Welch of Pittsburgh.

## Sacred Music

Late in his life, working for the New York publisher Horace Waters, Foster contributed twenty-nine pieces to *The Atheneum Collection of Hymns and Tunes for Church and Sunday Schools* (1863). Some were arrangements of existing melodies; others were new compositions. Fifteen of these pieces also appeared in Waters's *Heavenly Echoes* (1867). As Metcalf noted, "This was among the last of his work, for he died the following year." (Metcalf, p. 295)

In later years, Foster's secular tunes were adapted for hymn use. His melody for "Hard Times, Come Again No More" appeared in *The Revivalist* (1872) with sacred words; "Massa's in the Cold, Cold Ground" was adapted for "Hear the Gentle Voice of Jesus"; and "The Suwanee River" was used for "Our Shepherd True." (Metcalf, p. 296-301) Editors were sometimes criticized for such adaptations, but the logic was practical: familiar tunes help new words take root.

## Legacy

Foster's secular songs --- "Old Folks at Home," "My Old Kentucky Home," "Beautiful Dreamer," "Old Black Joe," "Oh! Susanna" --- are the beginning of American popular music. He attended Black camp meetings to understand their music, and his best songs have that quality of reaching directly to the heart. His brief foray into sacred music was a genuine contribution, even if his place in the hymnody tradition is minor compared to his permanent place in American musical memory.
