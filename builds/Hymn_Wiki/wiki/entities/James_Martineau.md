# James Martineau

James Martineau was one of the Victorian era's most distinguished religious minds, and his influence on hymnody came not from writing hymns — though he wrote a few — but from editing them. His two great hymnal compilations reshaped Unitarian worship in Britain and established a standard of literary excellence and theological liberalism that influenced many other denominations' hymn books. Benson regards him as the decisive figure in moving British Unitarian worship from the tradition of Watts toward a distinctly modern and theologically liberal conception of congregational song.

## Life and Career

Martineau was born in Norwich in 1805, the brother of the celebrated social theorist Harriet Martineau. He served as a Unitarian minister in Liverpool and Dublin before becoming professor of philosophy at Manchester New College (later moved to London), where he taught for decades. He retired in 1885 and lived to the extraordinary age of ninety-five, dying in 1900.

His theological career moved steadily in a liberal direction. By the time of his mature work he had traveled a long way from the Calvinistic Unitarianism of his youth — and the hymnody he compiled reflected that journey. He was a prominent figure in what Benson calls "the passing of the old Unitarianism into a free and 'universal' religion."

## The Hymnbooks

Martineau's first major contribution to hymnody was *Hymns for the Christian Church and Home* (1840). This collection represented a significant departure from the Watts-dominated tradition of earlier Unitarian worship. It was selected with a scholar's discrimination and a poet's ear — Benson notes that Martineau freely altered texts to suit his theological and literary standards, a practice both praised for its results and criticized for its liberties.

His second and more influential collection was *Hymns of Praise and Prayer* (1873). By this point Martineau's theology had moved beyond what most traditional Unitarians would have recognized as historically Christian. The collection drew on the widest possible range of sources, including much that was not specifically Christian at all, and aimed to provide a Hymnody adequate to what he called "universal religion." Benson notes that this book provided "the new faith with a Hymnody that confessedly transcended the limits of historical Christianity."

Both books were distinguished by their literary quality. Martineau refused to admit hymns he considered poetially or intellectually dishonest, and the resulting collections, whatever their theological controversies, were models of editorial taste. They were widely consulted by editors outside the Unitarian tradition who were trying to raise the literary standard of their own hymnals.

## Theological Significance

Martineau's hymnody represents the logical conclusion of a development that began with Watts: if the hymn could embody the New Testament rather than merely the Old, why could it not embody the broadest possible conception of religious experience? If the congregation could sing human compositions rather than Scripture alone, why could it not sing truths that went beyond any particular confession?

This argument, followed to its conclusion, arrived at a hymnody that Benson treats as marking the outer boundary of what could still be called Christian congregational song. The American counterpart, developed slightly earlier, was the work of Samuel Longfellow, Samuel Johnson, and Oliver Wendell Holmes — the *Hymns of the Spirit* (1864) which Martineau's English collections paralleled and in some ways inspired.

## Influence Beyond Unitarianism

The practical influence of Martineau's books extended well beyond Unitarian congregations. His work was consulted by editors of Baptist, Congregational, and other Nonconformist hymnals who were trying to raise their collections' literary standard. The very strictness of his editorial standards — and the results that strictness produced — set a benchmark against which other editorial efforts measured themselves.
