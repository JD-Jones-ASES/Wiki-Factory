# Philip Paul Bliss

Philip Paul Bliss was, in the generation before his death, the most prolific and gifted writer of both words and music for the Gospel Hymns movement. He died in a railway disaster at thirty-eight years of age, but not before he had given the Church a dozen tunes that are still sung.

## Origins

Bliss was born in 1838. His death came in December 1876 when the train carrying him and his wife plunged through a trestle bridge near Ashtabula, Ohio. His monument in Rome, Pennsylvania, bears the inscription: "P.P. Bliss --- author of 'Hold the Fort.'"

## The Songs

**"I Am So Glad"** --- Both words and music are by Bliss. He wrote it after a sudden inspiration: hearing the chorus "O how I love Jesus," he thought to himself, "I have sung long enough of my poor love to Christ --- now I will sing of His love for me." The result was a Sunday-school favorite as widely loved in Scotland as in America.

**"Pull for the Shore"** --- When a shipwrecked crew off a rocky coast ran back to save belongings and the captain shouted "She's sinking! There's nothing to do but pull for the shore!" Bliss caught up the words and wrought them into a hymn and tune. The hymn-tune is a buoyant allegro, full of hope and courage, popular at religious services on sea and land alike.

**"Let the Lower Lights Be Burning"** --- Inspired by an illustration from one of D.L. Moody's sermons about a stormy night on Lake Erie, where a pilot missed the channel because the lower lights had gone out, and many lives were lost. Bliss wrote both words and music in 1871. "Hold the Fort can be sung in the street," the authors observe; "'Let the Lower Lights Be Burning' is at home between echoing walls."

**"Hold the Fort!"** --- Based on the flag-signal waved by General Sherman's order from Kenesaw Mountain during the March through Georgia in October 1863. A visit to the site inspired Bliss to write both the words and music.

**"Hallelujah! 'Tis Done"** --- One of the spontaneous thanksgivings in revival meetings, written and composed entirely by Bliss.

**"Almost Persuaded"** --- The Rev. Mr. Brundage preached a sermon whose closing words were: "He who is almost persuaded is almost saved, but to be almost saved is to be entirely lost." Bliss was in the audience, was impressed, and composed what proved one of his most popular and convicting songs. He wisely omitted a chorus: this revival piece brings sense and sound to bear upon the conscience in one unbroken pleading.

**"It Is Well with My Soul"** (tune) --- The words were written by Horatio Gates Spafford after losing his children in a shipwreck. Bliss composed the "gentle, gliding melody that suits the mood of the words."

**"When Jesus Comes"** --- Written in Peoria, Illinois, in 1872, suggested by a conversation on the Second Coming of Christ. As Bliss came down from his room, the verses and notes came to him simultaneously on the stairs; he seized pencil and paper and fixed hymn and tune in a few minutes.

**"Eternity"** --- Words by Mrs. Ellen M.H. Gates; Bliss set it to music. After the beautiful harmony through seven lines, the choral reverently softens under the rallentando of the closing bars, and dwelling on the awe-inspiring syllables, solemnly dies away.

**"What Shall the Harvest Be?"** (tune) --- Words by Mrs. Emily Oakey. The solo and its organ-chords; and the chorus with its sustained soprano note dominating the running concert adds the last emphasis to the solemn repetition.

## The Legacy

James McGranahan, himself a gifted composer, was considered the natural successor to Bliss. The Gospel Hymns movement --- Sankey's *Sacred Songs and Solos* combined with Bliss's *Gospel Songs* --- was the foundation of the great series of popular hymn-and-tune books that numbered six volumes and swept the English-speaking world.
