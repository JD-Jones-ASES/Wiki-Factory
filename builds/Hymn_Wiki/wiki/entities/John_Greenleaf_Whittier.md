# John Greenleaf Whittier

Of all American poets, there is none who is so genuinely loved as John Greenleaf Whittier. A man of the people, a true American, and full of the milk of human kindness, Whittier's poetry reflects so much of his own character that it will never lose its singular charm and beauty.

He is also one of the most unusual figures in hymnody, for he was a Quaker who did not sing in worship and was not sure he had ever succeeded in composing a hymn --- and yet his name appears on at least seventy-five hymns now in use throughout the English-speaking world.

## The Barefoot Boy

Whittier was born of humble Quaker parents at Haverhill, Massachusetts, on December 17, 1807. Instead of receiving the advantages of education, he knew drudgery and hard work throughout his childhood. But the poetic spark was in him even then. As a small boy, he once sat before the kitchen fire and wrote on his slate:

> And must I always swing the flail
> And help to fill the milking pail?
> I wish to go away to school;
> I do not wish to be a fool.

The memory of those childhood experiences inspired him years later to write "The Barefoot Boy" and the other poems that captured the American rural childhood with such tenderness.

A wandering Scotchman who visited the quiet Quaker home one day sang "Bonny Doon" and "Auld Lang Syne," and the boy's imagination was kindled. He borrowed a copy of Burns' poems from the village schoolmaster and began to seriously think of becoming a poet.

## The Abolitionist

When Whittier was only twenty-five, he had already begun to attract attention by his poetry and to find some success in politics. But then came the call of the Abolition movement, and Whittier --- always true to his Quaker sense of "the inner voice" --- sacrificed all his political ambitions to become a champion of the enslaved.

He became, as Ryden writes, preeminently the poet of anti-slavery, as Phillips was its orator, Mrs. Harriet Beecher Stowe its novelist, and Sumner its statesman. At Philadelphia, where he published *The Pennsylvania Freeman*, the office was attacked by a mob and burned. He was not dismayed. When Daniel Webster in 1850 defended the Fugitive Slave law, Whittier wrote "Ichabod" in reply.

## The Hymn-Writer Who Wasn't Sure

Because the Quakers do not sing in their services, Whittier knew little of music. He once wrote: *"A good hymn is the best use to which poetry can be devoted, but I do not claim that I have succeeded in composing one."*

And yet the poems of Whittier --- notably "Our Master" and "The Eternal Goodness" --- have been the source of some of the finest hymns in the English language. The hymns are extracts from longer poems, selected and adapted by others. "Dear Lord and Father of mankind" comes from his poem "The Brewing of Soma" (1872), which is about the contrast between frenzied religious excitement and the calm, still voice of God:

> Dear Lord and Father of mankind,
>   Forgive our feverish ways;
> Reclothe us in our rightful mind,
> In purer lives Thy service find,
>   In deeper reverence, praise.

> Drop Thy still dews of quietness,
>   Till all our strivings cease;
> Take from our souls the strain and stress,
> And let our ordered lives confess
>   The beauty of Thy peace.

It is perhaps the most perfectly Quaker hymn ever written --- a hymn that asks not for religious excitement but for the silence of eternity interpreted by love.

## The Hymns

Among the at least seventy-five hymns drawn from Whittier's poetry:

- **"Dear Lord and Father of mankind"** --- his most famous
- **"I bow my forehead to the dust"**
- **"We need not climb the heavenly steeps"**
- **"Immortal love, forever full"**

His most famous secular poem is "Snowbound," the great winter idyll of New England rural life.

## The End

Whittier died in 1892. His last words were: *"Love --- love to all the world."* A friend bent over the dying man and whispered the words of his poem "At Last."

## Legacy

Whittier is the paradox of American hymnody: a man who did not think he had written any good hymns, whose hymns are among the most beautiful in the language. He is the poet of the quiet inward life of faith --- of Quaker stillness, of *"the beauty of Thy peace"*. In an age of religious excitement and revival noise, his voice is the still small voice.
