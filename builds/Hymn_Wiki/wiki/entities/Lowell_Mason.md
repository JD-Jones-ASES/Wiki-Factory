# Lowell Mason

Lowell Mason is the man who gave American church music its voice. Born in Medfield, Massachusetts in 1792, he grew up quietly, showed early talent in music, and spent sixteen years of his young manhood in Savannah, Georgia, working as a bank clerk --- while studying music on the side. Those Georgia years, modest as they seemed, produced one of the most celebrated tunes in Christian hymnody.

## The Missionary Hymn

In Savannah, Mason received a copy of Reginald Heber's newly written missionary hymn, "From Greenland's icy mountains." A local lady, charmed by the words, asked the young bank clerk if he could write a tune for them. The boy she sent to Mason with the request came back in half an hour with what would become the "Missionary Hymn." In the words of Dr. Charles Robinson, "Like the hymn it voices, it was done at a stroke, and it will last through the ages."

## Boston and National Influence

When Mason moved to Boston in 1827, he was already known as the composer of that tune. He became president of the Handel and Haydn Society, traveled to Europe for special study, and returned loaded with themes from the great masters. He founded the Boston Academy of Music, established singing schools, and organized musical conventions that touched communities across New England. What he did for sacred song in America, Butterworth-Brown compared to what Noah Webster did for the English language.

## The Tunes

Mason composed an astonishing number of hymn tunes, many of which have become inseparable from the words they carry:

- **"Missionary Hymn"** --- "From Greenland's icy mountains" (composed in Savannah, c. 1823)
- **"Bethany"** --- "Nearer, my God, to Thee" (1856) --- became hymn and tune's permanent marriage in America
- **"Laban"** --- "My soul, be on thy guard" (1830) --- dash and animation to reinforce the text
- **"Meribah"** --- "When Thou, my righteous Judge, shalt come" --- one of his masterpieces
- **"Naomi"** --- "Father, whate'er of earthly bliss"
- **"Henley"** --- "Come unto Me" --- melody and words that "melt together like harp and flute"
- **"Hamburg"** --- the best of his Gregorian adaptations, long associated with "When I survey the wondrous cross"
- **"Rockingham"** --- "What various hindrances we meet"
- **"Whitman"** --- "People of the living God" by James Montgomery
- **"Lanesboro"** --- "Early, my God, without delay," arranged from William Dixon
- **"Uxbridge"** and **"Malvern"** --- both used for Zinzendorf's "Jesus, Thy blood and righteousness"
- **"Ward"** --- "God is the refuge of His saints"
- **"Evan"** --- adapted from W.H. Havergal's song (1850)
- **"Eltham"** --- "Hasten, Lord, the glorious time" --- "Its harmony is like a chime of bells"
- **"Zion"** --- Thomas Hastings composed this, but Mason was its chief vehicle to "O'er the gloomy hills of darkness"
- **"Antioch"** --- "Joy to the world" (adapted from Handel's "Messiah")

## The Anecdote at the Organ

A vivid story from Butterworth-Brown shows Mason's instinctive pastoral sensitivity. He was once presiding at choir service when the minister gave out "When Thou, my righteous Judge, shalt come" and by mistake directed the singers to "omit the second stanza." Mason sat at the organ, played the last strain of the first stanza, glanced ahead in the hymnbook, and turned with a start just in time to command, "Sing the *next* verse!" The choir obeyed, and "O Lord, prevent it by Thy grace!" --- a plea not to be kept out of heaven --- was saved from being skipped as a horrible accidental prayer.

## Legacy

Mason was awarded the Doctor of Music degree and died in Orange, New Jersey, in 1872. He stands as the single most influential figure in the history of American church music.

## Methodist Hymnal Context (Nutter)

Nutter and Tillett, writing in 1911, state that Mason "did more to improve and elevate American Church music than any other man." They document thirty-four tunes by Mason in the Methodist Hymnal of 1905, including Azmon, Ariel, Hebron, Olivet, Naomi, Hamburg, Missionary Hymn, Bethany, Boylston, and Watchman — making him by far the most represented composer in that collection. Nutter notes that Mason composed the tune to "From Greenland's icy mountains" while in Savannah, and traces his career from that early achievement through the Boston Academy of Music and his introduction of music into the public schools — which Nutter calls a contribution alone sufficient to "immortalize his name." Mason is also noted as associated with [[George_James_Webb]] in musical work in Boston.

## Musical Context (Metcalf)

Metcalf's *American Writers and Compilers of Sacred Music* (1925) gives Mason one of its longest chapters, establishing him as the dominant figure of nineteenth-century American sacred music.

Mason was born in Medfield, Massachusetts, January 8, 1792, son of Johnson Mason and Catherine Hartshorn. His early musical ability showed quickly, and he became his town's choir leader. He moved to Savannah, Georgia, in his early twenties, worked fourteen years as a bank clerk while leading church choirs and compiling music. For seven years he was organist of the Independent Presbyterian Church in Savannah. His great compilation manuscript, offered first in Philadelphia and Boston without finding a publisher, came to the Handel and Haydn Society through Dr. George K. Jackson, who approved it. The book was published as the Society's own, with Mason's name omitted at his request: "I was then a bank officer in Savannah and did not wish to be known as a musical man." (Metcalf, p. 212) The *Handel and Haydn Collection of Sacred Music* (1822) ran through seventeen editions and sold over 50,000 copies, bringing each of Mason and the Society over $30,000.

When he returned to Boston in 1827 he became president of the Handel and Haydn Society, serving five years. He adopted Pestalozzi's educational methods and applied them to music teaching. He founded the Boston Academy of Music in 1832, working with [[George_James_Webb]]. Their children's music classes began free, held in the Bowdoin Street Church. In 1838 music was adopted into Boston's public schools --- Mason's crowning civic achievement.

Metcalf also documents Mason's introduction of the Musical Convention in 1834: week-long gatherings for singers and aspiring teachers that spread from Boston across the country. "When Lowell Mason organized the musical convention in Boston and carried it from thence into the country, he set in motion an influence that for forty years or more did more to make this nation a musical one than any one thing else has done," wrote L.O. Emerson in 1916. (Metcalf, p. 215)

Mason's library, one of the largest and most valuable of its kind in America, was donated to Yale College after his death and is kept there as a special collection. He received the first Doctor of Music degree granted in America (University of New York, 1855). He died at Orange, New Jersey, August 11, 1872.

Among his most used collections: *The Choir* (1833, over 50,000 copies), *The Modern Psalmist* (1839, 50,000+), *Carmina Sacra* (1841) and *New Carmina Sacra* (1852, over 500,000 copies combined), *The American Tune Book* (reaching over a million total). He worked closely with [[Thomas_Hastings]] and [[William_Bradbury]], and these three "led the prevailing school of sacred music at the beginning of the last century." (Metcalf, p. 197)

## Musical and Thematic Context (Butterworth-Brown)

Butterworth-Brown describe Mason repeatedly as the man who "created" fitting tunes for hymns that had wandered without proper musical clothing for years. His genius was the pairing --- matching the emotional temperature of a text to the movement and mood of a melody. When he succeeded, as with "Bethany" and "Missionary Hymn," hymn and tune became so fused that separating them is nearly unthinkable.
