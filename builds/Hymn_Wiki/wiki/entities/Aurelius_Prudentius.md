# Aurelius Prudentius

Aurelius Prudentius was a Spanish Roman who gave up a distinguished career in government service to become the greatest Latin Christian poet of his age. Richard Bentley called him "the Horace of the Christians" --- and in terms of sheer poetic craft, the comparison is deserved.

## A Man of Two Careers

Prudentius was born in 348 AD in northern Spain, probably in Saragossa. In his early life he was a successful Roman official, holding important positions of state. But in his later years he made a decisive turn: he retired to a monastery and dedicated his remaining time and talent to sacred Latin poetry.

It was a remarkably productive decision. Working in the quiet of monastic life, he produced a body of sacred verse that set a standard few Latin Christian poets have matched.

## Poet of the Martyrs

Prudentius had a particular devotion to the Christian martyrs --- the men and women who had died for their faith in the Roman persecutions. Ryden describes him as "preeminently the poet of the martyrs, never ceasing to extol their Christian faith and fortitude." His verse celebrated their courage as the highest expression of Christian life, the ultimate act of loyalty to Christ.

Martin Luther, who had strong opinions about most things, expressed the wish that Prudentius's works should be studied in the schools. Rudelbach, the hymn historian, said his poetry "is like gold set with precious stones." Archbishop Trench of England called his funeral hymn "the crowning glory of the poetry of Prudentius."

## The Funeral Hymn

Prudentius's most celebrated work is a remarkable funeral hymn --- 44 verses long, beginning with the Latin line *Deus ignee fons animarum* ("God, fiery fountain of souls"). It is sometimes called the "song of the catacombs," which evokes the era of early Christian martyrdom out of which its spirit was born.

Archbishop Johan Olof Wallin, the great Swedish hymn writer, made four separate attempts to translate this hymn before he was satisfied with his result --- which says something about how seriously he took it. An English version of part of the hymn begins:

> Despair not, O heart, in thy sorrow,
> But hope from God's promises borrow;
> Beware, in thy sorrow, of sinning,
> For death is of life the beginning.

That final line --- "death is of life the beginning" --- is a quintessentially Christian reversal of ordinary assumptions, and it captures why Prudentius was so beloved: he could find the grain of resurrection hope even in the heart of grief.

## Significance

Prudentius represents the golden age of early Latin Christian poetry. He was a contemporary of [[Ambrose_of_Milan]], though their paths apparently did not cross. Together they represent two streams of early Latin sacred verse: Ambrose the congregational singer, Prudentius the literary poet. Both contributed to the richness of the Western Christian tradition.

His long funeral hymn, in particular, is a masterpiece that has outlasted virtually everything else from his era in popular use. It stands as evidence that sacred poetry at its best does not merely decorate Christian experience --- it illuminates it.
