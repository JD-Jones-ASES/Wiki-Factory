# Johann Scheffler (Angelus Silesius)

## The Romanist Whose Hymns the Protestants Kept

Johann Scheffler presents one of the great paradoxes of Christian hymnody: a man who left the Lutheran Church to become a Roman Catholic and devoted considerable energy to writing polemics against Protestantism --- yet whose hymns of love for Christ the Heavenly Bridegroom were eagerly adopted by the Lutheran Church and became more popular among Protestants than among Catholics. As Albert Knapp observed: "Whencesoever they may come, they are an unfading ornament of the Church of Jesus Christ."

## Biographical Sketch

Scheffler was born in Breslau, Silesia, in 1624. His father, a Polish Lutheran nobleman, had fled Poland because of his Lutheran convictions --- giving Scheffler a somewhat ironic family heritage for a man who would later aggressively attack Lutheranism. He studied medicine at Strassburg, Leyden, and Padua, and in 1649 became private physician to Duke Sylvius Nimrod of Württemberg-Oels.

During his travels he had encountered the writings of various Christian mystics, particularly the medieval Catholic tradition, and he became deeply attracted to them. When he began promoting separatist practices (absenting himself from public worship, refusing the Lord's Supper with the unconverted) and sought to publish his mystical poems, the Lutheran authorities refused to permit it on grounds of their strong mystical and potentially heretical tendencies.

Scheffler resigned his position and moved to Breslau, where he joined a Jesuit circle, studied the medieval Catholic mystics intensively, and in 1653 was received into the Roman Church. He took the name Angelus Silesius (the Silesian Angel) after a Spanish mystic, John ab Angelis. In 1661 he was ordained a Catholic priest. He became a prolific and combative writer, directing more than fifty polemical tractates against his former church.

It has been well said of his polemics: "He certainly became more Roman than the Romans."

## His Hymns

Despite the polemics, Scheffler was a poet of the first rank. His hymns, collected in his "Heilige Seelenlust" (Holy Soul-Delight, 1657), are almost uniformly of a high order. They are saturated with mystical love for Christ the Bridegroom, drawing heavily on the imagery of the Song of Solomon. The imagery can occasionally be overdrawn, approaching what some readers find sensuous; but the devotional heat is unmistakable.

His greatest hymn is **"Thee Will I Love, My Strength, My Tower"**, a hymn of total consecration and love for Christ:

> *Thee will I love, my Strength, my Tower,*
> *Thee will I love, my Joy, my Crown;*
> *Thee will I love with all my power,*
> *In all Thy works, and Thee alone;*
> *Thee will I love, till Thy pure fire*
> *Fill all my soul with chaste desire.*

Other hymns by Scheffler in common Protestant use include:
- **"Earth Has Nothing Sweet or Fair"**
- **"Come, Follow Me, the Saviour Spake"**
- **"Jesus, Saviour, Come to Me"**
- **"Loving Shepherd, Kind and True"**

## The Zinzendorf Connection

[[Count_Nikolaus_von_Zinzendorf|Count Zinzendorf]] was particularly fond of Scheffler's hymns. He included no fewer than 79 of them in his Moravian hymn collection at Herrnhut --- a striking example of the Moravian willingness to draw from any source that expressed genuine love for Christ.

## Significance

Scheffler's story raises enduring questions about the relationship between a person's theology, their institutional loyalties, and the gifts they leave behind. He spent his adult life attacking the Lutheran Church; the Lutheran Church kept his hymns anyway, because those hymns expressed a love for Jesus Christ that transcended his polemics. The man who insisted "he certainly became more Roman than the Romans" wrote songs that the Romans largely ignored --- while the Protestants sang them for centuries.

His pantheistic tendencies (he wrote lines like "God in my nature is involved, / As I in the divine") put him perilously close to the border of orthodox Christianity. But the hymns that crossed over into Protestant use are those in which these tendencies are suppressed by his genuine, burning love for the person of Christ.
