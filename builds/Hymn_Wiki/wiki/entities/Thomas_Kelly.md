# Thomas Kelly

The Rev. Thomas Kelly was born in Dublin in 1769, the son of the Hon. Chief Baron Thomas Kelly, a judge of the Irish Court of Common Pleas. His father designed him for the legal profession, but after graduating at Trinity College he took holy orders in the Episcopal Church. Over a long ministry — becoming Nonconformist in his later years — he labored in Ireland for more than sixty years. He died on May 14, 1855, at the age of eighty-five.

Butterworth-Brown note that when Lord Plunkett once told him he would reach a great age, Kelly replied: "I expect never to die." He was a sweet-souled man, "honored as much for his piety as for his poetry, music, and oriental learning."

## On the Mountain Top Appearing

Kelly's missionary hymn has a strophic grandeur that suits its subject — the herald standing on the mountain with good news, the captive Zion about to be freed:

> On the mountain-top appearing,
>   Lo! the sacred herald stands,
> Joyful news to Zion bearing,
>   Zion long in hostile lands;
>     Mourning captive,
> God himself will loose thy bands.

## Musical and Thematic Context

Butterworth-Brown note that Kelly was himself a composer, though no original tune for this hymn seems to survive. In modern use, Dr. [[Thomas_Hastings]]'s "Zion" is most frequently attached to the hymn and "was probably written for it."

Kelly wrote over seven hundred hymns — a remarkable output — of which "On the Mountain Top Appearing" is the most widely remembered. His hymns were published in several collections between 1802 and 1853. Like James Montgomery, he was one of those laypeople — later Nonconformist clergy — who enriched the hymnal through genuine theological passion rather than institutional position.

## Hymnological Context (Duffield)

Duffield (1886) covers 25 of Kelly's hymns, drawing throughout on the successive editions of Kelly's own hymnal as primary documents. Most hymns are traced to specific numbered entries in the third edition (1809) or fifth edition (1820), with their original scripture headings preserved.

On **"Hark! Ten Thousand Harps and Voices"**, Duffield recounts the circumstance of Kelly's conversion: one of the works of the pious Romaine "fell in his way, and he was so deeply affected by the volume that he renounced the world and its legal allurements" — a detail echoing Kelly's origins as the son of a judge.

On **"The Head That Once Was Crowned with Thorns"** (1820), Duffield preserves a remarkable testimonial: "After about sixty years' experience of the blessings of the Gospel, Mr. Kelly was asked if he still found the Gospel as valuable as ever. His reply was: 'More so — far more so.'" The hymn, Kelly's finest, is based on Heb. 2:10 and has six stanzas.

On **"Keep Us, Lord, Oh, Keep Us Ever"**, Duffield inserts a vivid sailor's anecdote: a voyager who had gone around the world several times described being startled from sleep in his berth in the middle of the night and finding great comfort in this hymn. The story illustrates the seafaring reach of Kelly's work — his hymns traveled the globe in the pockets of ordinary believers.

On **"What Is Life? 'Tis But a Vapor"** (1809), Duffield records the encounter with Lord Plunket: "Meeting him one day when they had both grown old, said to him: 'You will live to a great old age.'" Kelly's reply — "I expect never to die" — has the ring of a man whose entire body of work was oriented not toward this world but the next.

Duffield's treatment of Kelly is systematic rather than biographical. The 25 entries reveal a writer whose output was rigorously organized: each hymn assigned a number, a scripture text, and a title in the original edition, with careful revision between editions. Kelly emerges as a craftsman of doctrinal precision who happened also to write with uncommon warmth.
