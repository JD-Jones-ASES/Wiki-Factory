# Ira David Sankey

Few musicians in Christian history have had a more decisive moment than Ira Sankey at an Edinburgh revival meeting in 1873 --- the moment he composed a tune in real time, before more than a thousand pairs of eyes and ears, for a poem he had never seen before.

## Early Life

Sankey was born in Edinburgh, Lawrence County, Pennsylvania, on August 28, 1840. He united with the Methodist Church at fifteen and became choir leader, Sunday-school superintendent, and president of the local Y.M.C.A. Hearing Philip Phillips sing convinced him of the power of a gifted solo vocalist over assembled multitudes, but he did not fully realize his own capability until Dwight L. Moody heard his remarkable voice and convinced him of his divine mission to be a gospel singer.

## The Birth of "The Ninety and Nine"

The most celebrated creative act of Sankey's career came during the great Scottish revival meetings with Moody. The hymn "The Ninety and Nine" had been written by Mrs. Elizabeth Cecilia Clephane at Melrose, Scotland, in early 1868 --- a small poem seen by Sankey in the *Christian Age* and cut out as potentially useful. At an impressive moment in one of the Edinburgh meetings, Moody said to him in a quiet aside: "Sing something."

Precisely what was wanted for the hour was in Sankey's vest pocket. But how could it be sung without a tune? With a silent prayer for help, the musician laid the slip containing Mrs. Clephane's poem on the little reed-organ and began playing and singing simultaneously. He had to read the unfamiliar words and make up the music as he went. The tune came --- and grew with each verse. A new melody was born in the presence of more than a thousand people. Afterward he said: "It was the most intense moment of my life."

In a touching interview afterward, a sister of Mrs. Clephane told Sankey that the authoress had not lived to see her hymn in print and to know of its blessed mission.

## The Gospel Hymns

Sankey compiled at least five singing books and wrote the *Story of the Gospel Hymns*. He was co-editor with [[Philip_Paul_Bliss]] and James McGranahan of the *Gospel Hymns* series --- the six-volume collection that became the soundtrack of the evangelical movement on both sides of the Atlantic. His *Sacred Songs and Solos*, combined with Bliss's *Gospel Songs*, was the foundation of the entire series.

He composed the tune for "Only Remembered" (Dr. Bonar's words, in *Gospel Hymns No. 6*), the tune for "There Are Lonely Hearts to Cherish," and contributed to the harmonization of "Throw Out the Life-Line" by Rev. E.S. Ufford, which he also purchased and published.

## Blindness and Perseverance

In his later years Sankey was overtaken by blindness. But like Isaac Newton and Thomas Carlyle before him, when the manuscript of his *Story of the Gospel Hymns* was destroyed by accident, he began his task again. With the help of an amanuensis the book was restored and given to the public in 1905.

## Stories of the Songs' Power

Sankey's own stories of the songs illustrate the reach of the Gospel Hymns. He sang "Where Is My Wandering Boy Tonight?" in California and in a snow-bound train to men who had been estranged from their fathers for twenty years. He sang it in a city church where a prodigal son --- present that very evening --- rose from his back seat as his widowed mother finished the second stanza.

"There is more electricity in that song than in any other I ever heard," Dr. Cuyler said to Sankey when he heard him sing "Throw Out the Life-Line." Its electricity, Sankey noted, had carried it nearly round the world.
