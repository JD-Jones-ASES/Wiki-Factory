## Text

> *From [[The_Christian_Hymn_Book|The Christian Hymn Book]] (1870), Hymn 75*

```
Our God, our help in ages past,
    Our hope for years to come,
  Our shelter from the stormy blast,
    And our eternal home!

  2 Under the shadow of thy throne
    Thy saints have dwelt secure:
  Sufficient is thine arm alone,
    And our defense is sure.

  3 Before the hills in order stood,
    Or earth received her frame,
  From everlasting thou art God,
    To endless years the same.

  4 A thousand ages in thy sight
    Are like an evening gone;
  Short as the watch that ends the night
    Before the rising sun.

  5 Time, like an ever-rolling stream,
    Bears all its sons away;
  They fly forgotten as a dream
    Dies at the opening day.

  6 Our God, our help in ages past,
    Our hope for years to come,
  Be thou our guard while troubles last,
    And our eternal home.
```

## Historical Context

*Source material: [[English_Hymns_Their_Authors_and_History]] (Duffield, 1886); [[The_Hymns_and_Hymn_Writers_of_the_Church]] (Nutter & Tillett, 1911); web research.*

[[Isaac Watts]] wrote this in 1714, at a moment of acute national anxiety. Queen Anne of England was dying without a clear Protestant heir, and the prospect of a Catholic successor threatened everything English Protestantism had built since the Reformation. Watts turned to Psalm 90 --- the great Moses psalm of divine eternity and human frailty --- and paraphrased it for the crisis of his own moment.

**The original title:** Watts titled the poem "Man Frail and God Eternal" --- a summary that captures the hymn's logic perfectly. Against the brevity and vulnerability of human life ("Time, like an ever-rolling stream, bears all its sons away"), Watts sets the eternal constancy of God ("From everlasting thou art God, to endless years the same"). The political crisis of 1714 is forgotten; the theological fact endures.

**The Wesley alteration:** [[John Wesley]] changed the first line from "Our God, our help in ages past" to "O God, our help in ages past" in his *Collection of Psalms and Hymns* (1738), replacing the communal "Our" with the direct address "O." The change is subtle but significant: Wesley's version is more urgent, more personal. Most modern hymnals follow Wesley's version; the Campbell 1870 collection retains Watts's original "Our."

**A second national anthem:** In Britain, this hymn is treated with something approaching civic reverence. It is sung at Remembrance Day services at the Cenotaph in London, at state funerals, and at times of national mourning and celebration. When the editorial commission for a major hymnal came to this number, Professor Winchester of Wesleyan University reportedly exclaimed: "The greatest hymn in the book!" Julian, in his great *Dictionary of Hymnology*, wrote: "Of Watts's original it would be difficult to write too highly."

**Nine stanzas:** Watts originally wrote nine stanzas; most collections use only five or six. The Campbell 1870 edition prints six. The hymn loses little by compression because each stanza stands on its own as a complete thought.

*Scripture basis: Psalm 90:1–5. The Campbell collection attributes the first line as "Our God" (Watts's original); the more familiar modern form uses Wesley's "O God."*
