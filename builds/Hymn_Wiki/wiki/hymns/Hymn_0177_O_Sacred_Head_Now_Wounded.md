## Text

> *From [[The_Christian_Hymn_Book|The Christian Hymn Book]] (1870), Hymn 177*

```
O sacred head, now wounded,
    With grief and shame weighed down—
  O sacred brow, surrounded
    With thorns, thine only crown:
  Once on a throne of glory,
    Adorned with light divine;
  Now all despised and gory,
    I joy to call thee mine.

  2 On me, as thou art dying,
    O, turn thy pitying eye;
  To thee for mercy crying,
    Before thy cross I lie.
  Thine, thine the bitter passion;
    Thy pain is all for me;
  Mine, mine the deep transgression;
    My sins are all on thee.

  3 What language can I borrow
    To praise thee, heavenly Friend,
  For all this dying sorrow,
    Of all my woes the end?
  O, can I leave thee ever?
    Then do not thou leave me;
  Lord, let me never, never
    Outlive my love to thee.

  4 Be near when I am dying;
    Then close beside me stand;
  Let me, while faint and sighing,
    Lean calmly on thy hand:
  These eyes, new faith receiving,
    From thee shall never move,
  For he who dies believing,
    Dies safely—in thy love.
```

## Historical Context

*Source: [[The_Story_of_Our_Hymns]] by Ernest Edwin Ryden (1930), Part I.*

This hymn has one of the most remarkable journeys in all of Christian hymnody --- moving across three languages, five centuries, and three major Christian traditions, each time gaining rather than losing power.

**The original:** The Latin poem *Salve caput cruentatum* was written by [[Bernard_of_Clairvaux]], the great Cistercian monk and preacher of the Second Crusade (1091-1153). It is a sustained meditation on the crucified Christ, addressed to the wounded head of the Saviour. The hymn is part of a longer cycle of poems meditating on Christ's suffering body. Luther paid Bernard extraordinary tribute: "If there has ever been a pious monk who feared God it was St. Bernard, whom alone I hold in much higher esteem than all other monks and priests throughout the globe."

**The German version:** Five centuries after Bernard, the great Lutheran hymn writer [[Paul Gerhardt]] translated Bernard's Latin into German, deepening and enriching it. Ryden quotes Lauxmann's assessment: "Bernard's original is powerful and searching, but Gerhardt's hymn is still more powerful and profound, as re-drawn from the deeper spring of evangelical Lutheran, Scriptural knowledge and fervency of faith."

**The English version:** Gerhardt's German was then translated into English by James W. Alexander of Princeton, a Presbyterian. Philip Schaff described the full journey perfectly: "This classic hymn has shown in three tongues --- Latin, German and English --- and in three confessions --- Roman, Lutheran and Reformed --- with equal effect the dying love of our Saviour and our boundless indebtedness to Him."

**The tune:** Johann Sebastian Bach gave the melody its present form as the "Passion Chorale" --- transforming what was originally a rather frivolous German folksong (adapted by Hans Leo Hassler in 1601 to the hymn "Herzlich thut mich verlangen") into one of the world's masterpieces of sacred music.

**A dying witness:** In 1798, the great Lutheran missionary to India, Christian Schwartz, lay dying. His Indian pupils gathered around his bed and sang the last verses of this hymn in their own Malabar tongue. Schwartz himself joined in the singing until his voice was silenced in death.

The hymn appears in Campbell's collection attributed to Gerhardt, which is accurate as far as the German text goes; the English version most commonly sung traces back through Alexander's translation of Gerhardt's rendering of Bernard's Latin original.
