## Text

> *From [[The_Christian_Hymn_Book|The Christian Hymn Book]] (1870), Hymn 512*

```
When I survey the wondrous cross,
    On which the Prince of glory died,
  My richest gain I count but loss,
    And pour contempt on all my pride!

  2 Forbid it, Lord, that I should boast,
    Save in the death of Christ, my Lord:
  All the vain things that charm me most,
    I sacrifice them to his blood.

  3 See from his head, his hands, his feet,
    Sorrow and love flow mingled down;
  Did e’er such love and sorrow meet—
    Or thorns compose so rich a crown?

  4 Were the whole realm of nature mine,
    That were a present far too small;
  Love so amazing, so divine,
    Demands my soul, my life, my all!
```

## Historical Context

*Source material: [[English_Hymns_Their_Authors_and_History]] (Duffield, 1886); [[The_Hymns_and_Hymn_Writers_of_the_Church]] (Nutter & Tillett, 1911); web research.*

[[Isaac Watts]] (1674–1748) is the father of English hymnody, and "When I Survey the Wondrous Cross" is considered by many --- including his contemporaries --- to be his single greatest poem. It appeared in his *Hymns and Spiritual Songs* (1707) and was written to be sung at Communion, as a meditation on Galatians 6:14.

**A historic innovation:** When Watts wrote this hymn, English congregational singing was almost exclusively restricted to metrical paraphrases of the Psalms. Writing a hymn based on a New Testament text was already bold. But Watts went further: this is considered the first English hymn written in the first person singular ("When *I* survey..."), introducing individual spiritual experience into congregational worship. Some worshippers found it dangerously self-centered; others found it transforming. [[Charles Wesley]] reportedly said he would give up all of his own hymns to have written this one.

**The text's power:** The hymn achieves something rare: it moves from contemplation to self-surrender in four stanzas without a false step. The final stanza's logic --- that even "the whole realm of nature" would be too small a gift in response to such love --- is one of the most compressed and devastating statements of devotion in the English language.

**In literature:** Duffield notes that in George Eliot's novel *Adam Bede*, the character Dinah Morris (modeled on Eliot's real aunt, a Methodist preacher) sings this hymn in a memorable scene. It was a deliberate choice by Eliot to use Watts's most serious and concentrated hymn for her most serious and concentrated character.

**The tune:** The familiar tune "Hamburg" was composed by [[Lowell Mason]], arranged from a Gregorian chant. The "Rockingham" tune (associated with this hymn in Britain) was composed by Edward Miller, who had played flute in Handel's orchestra --- one more thread connecting this most personal of hymns to the grandest of musical traditions.
