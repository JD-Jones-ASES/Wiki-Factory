## Text

> *From [[The_Christian_Hymn_Book|The Christian Hymn Book]] (1870), Hymn 660*

```
O thou Fount of every blessing!
    Tune my heart to sing thy grace;
  Streams of mercy, never ceasing,
    Call for songs of loudest praise.

  2 Teach me ever to adore thee,
    May I still thy goodness prove,
  While the hope of endless glory
    Fills my heart with joy and love.

  3 Here I’ll raise my Ebenezer,
    Hither by thy help I’ve come,
  And I hope, by thy good pleasure,
    Safely to arrive at home.

  4 Jesus sought me when a stranger,
    Wandering from thy fold, O God!
  He to rescue me from danger,
    Interposed his precious blood.

  5 O! to grace how great a debtor
    Daily I’m constrained to be!
  Let thy goodness, like a fetter,
    Bind me closer still to thee!

  6 Never let me wander from thee,
    Never leave thee whom I love;
  By thy Word and Spirit guide me,
    Till I reach thy courts above.
```

## Historical Context

*Source material: [[English_Hymns_Their_Authors_and_History]] (Duffield, 1886); [[The_Hymns_and_Hymn_Writers_of_the_Church]] (Nutter & Tillett, 1911); web research.*

[[Robert Robinson]] (1735–1790) was a Norfolk-born man whose early life bears more than a passing resemblance to John Newton's --- and whose later life gives the phrase "prone to wander" its sharpest edge.

**From hairdresser's apprentice to preacher:** Robinson was sent to London at the age of 14 to be apprenticed to a hairdresser. In 1752, he heard George Whitefield preach on "the Wrath to Come" and was deeply shaken. After years of spiritual struggle, he converted at age 20 and eventually became a Baptist minister in Cambridge. He was 22 years old when, in 1758, he wrote this hymn for a Pentecost Sunday sermon. The hymn was first published in *A Collection of Hymns Used by the Church of Christ in Angel Alley Bishopsgate* (1759).

**"Prone to wander, Lord, I feel it":** This is the line that haunts the hymn --- and Robinson's biography gives it a particular resonance. In later years, Robinson drifted from orthodoxy. He became close to the scientist and Unitarian minister Joseph Priestley, and his theology grew uncertain. A famous story (possibly apocryphal) describes Robinson in a coach, meeting a young woman absorbed in reading a hymnal. She asked him what he thought of the hymn "Come Thou Fount." He reportedly replied: "Madam, I am the poor, unhappy man who composed that hymn many years ago; and I would give a thousand worlds, if I had them, to enjoy the feelings I had then." The story may be legend, but it describes something true about the hymn: it is the prayer of someone who knows their own capacity for wandering.

**The Ebenezer:** The word "Ebenezer" in the third stanza comes from 1 Samuel 7:12, where Samuel raises a stone and names it Ebenezer --- "stone of help" --- after God gave Israel victory. "Here I'll raise my Ebenezer" means: this is a place where I mark what God has done. It is one of the most ancient and personal acts of faith in the Bible, translated into an 18th-century hymn.

**Authorship controversy:** Duffield notes that there were attempts to attribute this hymn to Selina, Countess of Huntingdon, backed by a manuscript produced by the bibliographer Daniel Sedgwick. The claim is now universally rejected; Robinson's authorship is not seriously disputed.

*The tune "Nettleton" (from John Wyeth's *Repository of Sacred Music*, 1813) is the American tune most commonly associated with this hymn. The Campbell 1870 collection prints six stanzas.*
