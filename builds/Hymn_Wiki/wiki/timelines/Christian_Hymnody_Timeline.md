# Christian Hymnody Timeline

A chronological guide to the history of Christian song, from the apostolic church to the eve of the modern era. Each century tells a distinct chapter; each chapter builds on the ones before.

---

## First and Second Centuries (c. 50–200 AD)

| Date | Event | Key Figures |
|------|-------|-------------|
| c. 50–60 | Paul's letters instruct churches to sing psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs | --- |
| c. 112 | Pliny the Younger reports Christians singing hymns to Christ before dawn | --- |
| c. 200 | Oldest surviving Christian hymn text composed | [[Clement_of_Alexandria]] |

**Context:** Christian worship inherits the Jewish psalmodic tradition. Hymn singing is already central and controversial: the Romans use it as evidence of cult activity. Clement's hymn at the close of his *Instructor* is the first hymn we can actually read.

See [[Early_Christian_Hymnody]]

---

## Third and Fourth Centuries (200–399)

| Date | Event | Key Figures |
|------|-------|-------------|
| c. 307–373 | Syriac hymnody flourishes; orthodox song deployed against Gnostic and Arian popular music | [[Ephrem_the_Syrian]] |
| c. 360 | First Latin hymn writer active in Gaul | [[Hilary_of_Poitiers]] |
| c. 340–397 | Father of Latin hymnody establishes the Ambrosian meter; teaches Milan to hold its faith by singing it | [[Ambrose_of_Milan]] |
| 325–389 | Greatest Greek patristic hymnist flourishes | [[Gregory_Nazianzen]] |

**Context:** The great Trinitarian controversies (Arianism, Gnosticism) spur doctrinal hymnody. Ephrem's insight --- that heresy spreads through popular song and must be countered with better song --- shapes the entire future of Christian hymnody. Ambrose establishes the template that will govern Latin hymnody for a millennium.

See [[Greek_and_Syriac_Hymnody]], [[Latin_Hymnody]]

---

## Fifth and Sixth Centuries (400–599)

| Date | Event | Key Figures |
|------|-------|-------------|
| 348–413 | "Horace of the Christians" writes the funeral hymn of the catacombs and the Christmas morning hymn | [[Aurelius_Prudentius]] |
| c. 530–609 | Last voice of the Ambrosian tradition; writes hymns of the Cross and Easter | [[Venantius_Fortunatus]] |

**Context:** Rome falls (476) but Latin hymnody continues to develop in the monasteries and cathedral churches. Prudentius is the most accomplished poet of this era. Fortunatus, writing at the Frankish court, closes the first great chapter of Latin hymnody.

---

## Seventh through Tenth Centuries (600–999)

| Date | Event | Key Figures |
|------|-------|-------------|
| 7th–8th c. | Greek hymnody continues in the Byzantine East | [[Anatolius]] |
| c. 675–749 | Greatest Easter hymnist of the Eastern church; Easter Day hymns still sung | [[John_of_Damascus]] |
| c. 725–794 | Nephew of John of Damascus; "Art thou weary, art thou languid?" | [[Stephen_the_Sabaite]] |
| c. 750–821 | "All glory, laud, and honor" written in prison during Holy Week | [[Theodulph_of_Orleans]] |
| 9th c. | Last great Greek hymnist; closes the Byzantine golden age | [[Joseph_the_Hymnographer]] |

**Context:** The split between the Greek East and Latin West deepens, but both traditions continue producing great hymnody. The Byzantine church develops an elaborate liturgical hymnody (troparia, kontakia, kanones) that has no exact parallel in the West. In the West, the monastic office preserves and transmits the Ambrosian tradition.

See [[Greek_and_Syriac_Hymnody]]

## Eleventh through Thirteenth Centuries (1000–1299): The Golden Age of Latin Hymnody

| Date | Event | Key Figures |
|------|-------|-------------|
| 1091–1153 | Greatest medieval Latin hymn writer; Passion meditations that will later shape Lutheran hymnody | [[Bernard_of_Clairvaux]] |
| c. 1100–1156 | Cluniac satirist-poet writes the great vision of the heavenly city | [[Bernard_of_Cluny]] |
| c. 1185–1265 | Franciscan friar composes *Dies irae*, the great sequence on judgment | [[Thomas_of_Celano]] |
| 1225–1274 | Philosopher-theologian writes *Lauda Sion*, closing the golden age | [[Thomas_Aquinas]] |

**Context:** The medieval flowering of Latin hymnody is one of the richest chapters in the tradition. The Cistercian and Franciscan movements bring new emotional depth and popular energy. The sequence form allows more complex narrative and theological development than the Ambrosian hymn. The two Bernards and the two Thomases represent the apex.

See [[Golden_Age_of_Latin_Hymnody]], [[Latin_Hymnody]]

## Sixteenth Century: The Reformation

| Date | Event | Key Figures |
|------|-------|-------------|
| 1483–1546 | Father of Evangelical Hymnody; restores vernacular congregational song | [[Martin_Luther]] |
| 1484–1551 | Luther's first co-laborer; great hymn of salvation by grace written in a Moravian prison | [[Paul_Speratus]] |
| d. 1541 | Former monk; metrical Gloria and Agnus Dei; Luther prized both | [[Nicolaus_Decius]] |
| c. 1505–1570 | French musician; probable composer of Old Hundred in Geneva | [[Guillaume_le_Franc]] |
| 1532–1592 | Co-author of the Formula of Concord; "Let Me Be Thine Forever" | [[Nicolaus_Selnecker]] |
| 1562 | Genevan Psalter published; Reformed psalmody reaches its classic form | --- |
| 1652–1715 | Tate of the New Version (English) | [[Nahum_Tate]] |
| 1659–1726 | Brady of the New Version (English) | [[Nicholas_Brady]] |

**Context:** Luther's recovery of congregational song is the decisive event in the modern history of hymnody. He writes, adapts, and champions hymns with the conviction that music is "the noblest of God's gifts, next to theology." The Reformed tradition takes a different path: strict psalm-singing in Calvinist churches, resisting any "merely human" additions to worship. This division between Lutheran and Reformed approaches to hymnody will persist for two centuries.

See [[Reformation_Hymnody]]

## Seventeenth Century: War, Plague, and the Chorale

| Date | Event | Key Figures |
|------|-------|-------------|
| 1556–1608 | King and Queen of Chorales written during a plague | [[Philipp_Nicolai]] |
| 1585–1647 | War-born Passion hymns from Silesia; ranked with Luther and Gerhardt | [[Johann_Heermann]] |
| 1586–1649 | Germany's Lutheran Te Deum; author buried 4,000 plague victims | [[Martin_Rinkart]] |
| 1607–1676 | Prince of Lutheran Hymnists; 123 hymns from a life of suffering and exile | [[Paul_Gerhardt]] |
| 1618–1648 | Thirty Years' War; the crucible of Lutheran hymnody | --- |
| 1618–1677 | Pioneer of the Jesus hymn tradition | [[Johann_Franck]] |
| 1624–1677 | Catholic convert whose mystical hymns became Lutheran treasures | [[Johann_Scheffler]] |
| 1634–1703 | Denmark's first great hymnist, "poet of Easter-tide," 85 hymns | [[Thomas_Kingo]] |
| 1648–1717 | French mystic imprisoned in the Bastille; hymns translated by Cowper | [[Madame_Guyon]] |
| 1650–1680 | First great Reformed hymn-writer; "Praise to the Lord the Almighty"; died at 30 | [[Joachim_Neander]] |

**Context:** The seventeenth century is the greatest single century of Lutheran hymnody. The Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) destroys a third of Germany's population but produces an extraordinary body of hymnody. The pattern is consistent: catastrophe strips away everything except essential faith, and essential faith sings. Meanwhile, Denmark and Sweden begin developing their own Lutheran traditions.

See [[Thirty_Years_War_Hymns]], [[Paul_Gerhardt]]

**Context:** The early eighteenth century sees the liberation of English hymnody through Watts, the German Pietist renewal through Schmolck and Tersteegen, the extraordinary Moravian experiment under Zinzendorf, and the beginning of Welsh revival hymnody. The stage is set for the great Methodist explosion.

**Context:** The late nineteenth century sees the gospel song tradition crystallize around the Moody-Sankey campaigns. Fanny Crosby, Robert Lowry, William Doane, and Ira Sankey create the distinctive sound of American evangelical revival. Meanwhile, the Victorian Anglican tradition reaches its peak: Dykes, Monk, and Hopkins compose the great tune-book that will define English-language worship through the twentieth century.

## Early Twentieth Century (1900–1930)

| Date | Event | Key Figures |
|------|-------|-------------|
| 1904–1905 | Welsh Revival; Welsh Hymnody reaches its peak of influence | --- |
| 1920s | Gospel song tradition continues; hymn revision movements begin | Various |
| 1930 | Ryden's *Story of Our Hymns* published; comprehensive historical synthesis | [[The_Story_of_Our_Hymns]] |

**Context:** By 1930, the era of the great individual hymn writer is effectively over. The twentieth century will bring new movements --- the Social Gospel, mid-century liturgical renewal, contemporary Christian music --- but the tradition surveyed in this wiki is substantially complete. The 1,324 hymns of Campbell's collection represent a repertoire that had been assembled over nearly nineteen centuries and across a dozen languages. Most of it is still in use.
