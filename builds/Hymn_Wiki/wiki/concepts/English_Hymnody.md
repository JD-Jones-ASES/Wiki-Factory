# English Hymnody

For more than a century after the Reformation, English Christians sang no hymns of human composition in their churches. Calvin's prohibition against anything but metrical Psalms held almost absolute sway, and the crude versifications of Sternhold and Hopkins were the staple of Reformed worship on both sides of the Channel.

Then, in the generation between 1637 and 1720, three men changed everything: Bishop Thomas Ken, the essayist Joseph Addison, and above all [[Isaac_Watts]]. English hymnody erupted into one of the most extraordinary flowerings of sacred song in the history of Christianity.

## The Dawn: Ken and Addison

Bishop Thomas Ken (1637-1711) is England's first hymn-writer of note. He wrote the famous Doxology --- "Praise God from whom all blessings flow" --- and two hymns for the students of Winchester College: a morning hymn ("Awake, my soul, and with the sun") and an evening hymn ("Glory to Thee, my God, this night"). Both rank among the four masterpieces of English praise.

Ken's life matched his hymns in courage. When King Charles II asked him to give up his house to house the notorious Nell Gwynne, Ken answered: "Not for the King's kingdom." Charles so admired his boldness that he named him Bishop of Bath and Wells and used to say: "I must go in and hear Ken tell me of my faults." The historian Macaulay said Ken came as near to the ideal of Christian perfection "as human weakness permits."

Joseph Addison, the celebrated essayist, appended five hymns of rare beauty to his *Spectator* essays. Among them was "The spacious firmament on high" --- the "Creation hymn" that Haydn included in his celebrated oratorio.

## The Revolution: Isaac Watts

With [[Isaac_Watts]] (1674-1748), the Reform Church's last defenses against hymns of human composition fell. Watts was challenged as a young man to "give us something better" than the Sternhold and Hopkins versifications --- and he did. His *Hymns and Spiritual Songs* (1707) was the first real hymn-book in the English language. His *Psalms of David* (1719) rendered the Hebrew Psalter so thoroughly in New Testament language that the Old Testament origin is often forgotten.

"When I survey the wondrous cross" is ranked by Matthew Arnold as the finest hymn in the English language. "Joy to the world" is sung at virtually every Christmas service in the world. "O God, our help in ages past" is indispensable at New Year's. Watts opened a treasury that has never been exhausted.

## The Revival: Methodism and the Wesleys

The Methodist revival produced the second great explosion in English hymnody. [[Charles_Wesley]] (1707-1788) wrote over 6,500 hymns, among them "Hark! the herald angels sing," "Love divine, all love excelling," and "Jesus, Lover of my soul." His brother John's movement gave those hymns a vast, eager congregation.

The Olney Hymns (1779), the joint production of [[John_Newton]] and [[William_Cowper]], represent a different but equally important stream: Anglican Evangelical hymnody. "Amazing Grace" and "God moves in a mysterious way" came from this collection. The contrast between Newton's dramatic autobiography and Cowper's afflicted poetry makes the Olney Hymns one of the most humanly moving collections in the tradition.

[[Augustus_Toplady]] (1740-1778), the Calvinist opponent of Wesley, contributed "Rock of Ages" --- the most popular hymn ever written by an Englishman. The hymn that outlived the quarrel that produced it.

[[Philip_Doddridge]] (1702-1751) contributed from the Independent tradition: "O happy day" and "Hark, the glad sound, the Saviour comes."

## The Victorian Era

The nineteenth century brought a different kind of hymnody to England: more lyrical, more personal, and shaped by three great movements --- the Evangelical awakening, the Oxford Movement, and the explosion of women hymnists.

**The lyricists:** [[Reginald_Heber]] (1783-1826) brought pure poetry to English hymns --- "Holy, holy, holy" (Tennyson's "world's greatest hymn") and "From Greenland's icy mountains." [[Henry_Francis_Lyte]] (1793-1847) gave the Church "Abide with me," written on his last Sunday of ministry. [[George_Matheson]] (1842-1906) wrote "O Love that wilt not let me go" in minutes on a dark evening, blind and in pain.

**The translators:** [[John_Mason_Neale]] (1818-1866) reached back through the centuries to give English Christians "Jerusalem the golden," "The day of resurrection," and many more treasures from the ancient Greek and Latin Church.

**The Scots:** [[Horatius_Bonar]] (1808-1889) gave Scottish Presbyterianism its warmest singing voice: "I heard the voice of Jesus say" and "I lay my sins on Jesus."

**The women:** The Victorian era saw an extraordinary rise of women hymn-writers. [[Charlotte_Elliott]] (1789-1871) wrote "Just as I am" --- the hymn D.L. Moody called the most soul-winning ever written. [[Sarah_Flower_Adams]] (1805-1848) wrote "Nearer, my God, to Thee." [[Frances_Ridley_Havergal]] (1836-1879), the "consecration poet," wrote "Take my life, and let it be."

**The one-night wonder:** [[Sabine_Baring_Gould]] (1834-1924) wrote "Onward, Christian soldiers" overnight for a children's march and lived to ninety, still surprised by its popularity.

## Character and Legacy

English hymnody as a whole is notable for:

- **Doctrinal richness**: Watts and Wesley between them cover the entire range of Christian theology in verse
- **Personal depth**: Cowper, Elliott, Matheson, and Havergal wrote hymns of real spiritual autobiography
- **Poetic quality**: Heber, Lyte, and Bonar brought genuine lyrical gifts to sacred song
- **Cross-tradition range**: Anglican, Methodist, Baptist, Presbyterian, Congregational, Quaker, and even Unitarian voices all contributed

By the middle of the nineteenth century, English hymnody had become the dominant tradition in the English-speaking Christian world, and its influence extended wherever missionaries carried the gospel. Every tradition that sings in English draws on this treasury.

## Institutional History (Benson)

Benson's *The English Hymn: Its Development and Use in Worship* (1915) is the foundational institutional history of this tradition. Benson's central argument is that English hymnody was not a smooth development but a battle — fought over a century against the Calvinist preference for Scripture-only congregational song — that was won slowly, denomination by denomination, through the advocacy of individuals who had to argue the case from scratch against established tradition.

**The Reformation Settlement and Its Legacy.** Calvin's triumph in the Reformed tradition meant that England and Scotland became nations of Psalm-singers rather than hymn-singers. The Sternhold-Hopkins Psalter (1562) was the devotional inheritance of English Protestantism. Its adequacy was acknowledged by almost no one, but its dominance was complete. The theological argument — that only Scripture itself, in metrical form, could be sung in worship — was powerful enough to resist all practical objections.

**The Pre-Watts Movement.** Benson's most original contribution is his detailed account of the movement to introduce hymns in the century before Watts. The ejected Presbyterians of 1662 — especially Richard Baxter — began the argument and published early hymn collections. John Playford's unsuccessful 1671 attempt to introduce hymns into the Church of England showed how closed that institution was. The Baptist controversy over hymn-singing in the 1670s-1690s, resolved by Keach, established the Baptist tradition. Independents and Presbyterians gradually moved toward hymns through the later seventeenth century. By 1700, the ground was prepared — but the tree had not yet grown.

**The Watts Revolution (1707-1719).** Watts' *Hymns and Spiritual Songs* (1707) and *Psalms of David Imitated* (1719) settled the practical question for English Nonconformity. Within two decades, most English Congregational and many Presbyterian and Baptist churches were using Watts. The establishment of the Church of England lagged far behind.

**The Methodist Expansion.** The Methodist Revival added a vast popular dimension to English hymnody. Charles Wesley's 6,500+ hymns gave the movement its identity; John Wesley's editorial and administrative genius gave them their institutional form. The Evangelical Revival produced the Olney Hymns and, through Whitefield's circle and Lady Huntingdon's Connexion, a Calvinistic counterpart to Wesley's Arminian hymnody.

**The Romantic Movement and Its Hymnody.** The early nineteenth century brought a new type: the literary hymn. Reginald Heber, trained on the Romantic poets, brought poetic standards to Anglican hymnody that it had never had. Independently, the Unitarian tradition was developing the most literary body of sacred verse in the English language.

**The Oxford Transformation.** The Oxford Movement's recovery of ancient and medieval hymnody through translation, and its institutional expression in Hymns Ancient and Modern (1861), transformed both Anglican worship and, eventually, English Nonconformist worship. The devotional rather than homiletical conception of hymnody gradually became the standard across denominations.

**The Gospel Hymn's Challenge.** The Moody-Sankey Gospel Hymn movement from the 1870s onward temporarily displaced the established hymnody in many English and American churches, before a reaction restored the standard. The challenge it posed — how to hold together mass popular appeal and genuine spiritual quality — remained unresolved.
