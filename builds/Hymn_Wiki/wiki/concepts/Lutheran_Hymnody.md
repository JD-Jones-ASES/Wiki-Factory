# Lutheran Hymnody

> [[_overview|Home]] > [[_index|Index]] > Concepts

The Lutheran tradition is the oldest and largest stream of Protestant congregational hymnody. It was born in the Reformation, survived persecution and warfare, deepened through Pietism, spread through Scandinavia, and eventually enriched the whole English-speaking church through the translations of [[Catherine_Winkworth]] and others. To trace Lutheran hymnody is to trace five centuries of the Christian church at worship.

## The Reformation: A Singing Revolution

When [[Martin_Luther]] nailed his theses to the Wittenberg door in 1517, no one could have predicted that one of the Reformation's lasting legacies would be a new tradition of congregational song. But Luther grasped what his enemies understood too: "the whole people are singing themselves into his doctrines." Music was theology made irresistible.

Luther himself wrote approximately thirty-six hymns and composed several of the beloved chorale melodies. His collaborators --- [[Paul_Speratus]], [[Nicolaus_Decius]], [[Nicolaus_Selnecker]], and others --- built alongside him the first Protestant hymnal tradition. By Luther's death in 1546, more than sixty hymn collections had been published. See [[Reformation_Hymnody]] for the full story of this period.

## The German Chorale

The **German chorale** --- the sturdy, metrically regular hymn tune designed for congregational voices --- is one of the Reformation's great gifts to the church. These tunes were sometimes adapted from Gregorian chant, sometimes borrowed from secular song, sometimes freshly composed. Bach's harmonizations have given them a permanent home in Western choral music, but long before Bach they were beloved by ordinary Lutheran congregations singing them in four-part harmony on Sunday mornings.

The chorale tradition is both a musical and a theological achievement: music simple enough for untrained voices to sing together, yet substantial enough to carry weighty doctrinal content.

## The Crucible of the Seventeenth Century

The generation of the [[Thirty_Years_War_Hymns|Thirty Years' War]] produced some of the greatest Lutheran hymns ever written. [[Philipp_Nicolai]] composed his two masterworks during a devastating plague. [[Johann_Heermann]] survived repeated devastation of his parish. [[Martin_Rinkart]] buried his townspeople by the hundreds. [[Paul_Gerhardt]], the towering figure of the century, watched Lutheran Germany reduced to rubble and wrote hymns of such warmth and beauty that they have never been surpassed.

These men were not writing in comfortable studies. They were writing theology in blood and fire, and their hymns carry the weight of that experience. See [[Post-Reformation_Hymnody]] for a full treatment of this period.

## Pietism and the Interior Turn

The late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries brought [[Pietism]] --- a movement within Lutheranism that emphasized personal conversion, warm devotion, and practical Christian life over doctrinal correctness. The Pietist hymn-writers extended the tradition in a more intimate, subjective direction.

[[Benjamin_Schmolck]] (1672-1737) wrote with a gentle warmth characteristic of the Pietist spirit. [[Gerhard_Tersteegen]] (1697-1769) produced mystical devotional verse of rare depth. [[Johann_Franck]] (1618-1677), though technically pre-Pietist, anticipated much of the Pietist sensibility. The Pietist thread ran eventually into the [[Moravian_Hymnody|Moravian movement]] of Count [[Count_Nikolaus_von_Zinzendorf|Zinzendorf]], which had a direct and documented influence on the Wesley brothers and, through them, on English and American hymnody.

## The Scandinavian Extension

Lutheranism's expansion into Scandinavia produced a distinct regional tradition of great richness. See [[Scandinavian_Hymnody]] for the full account.

**Denmark:** [[Thomas_Kingo]] (1634-1703) gave the Danish church its first great hymn-writer, whose passion hymns for Holy Week remain beloved. [[Hans_Adolph_Brorson]] (1694-1764) brought the Pietist warmth into Danish song with a gentleness and musical beauty that places him among the finest Lutheran hymn-writers of any country. [[N_F_S_Grundtvig]] (1783-1872) was the giant of the nineteenth century --- poet, theologian, bishop, reformer --- whose hymns reshaped Danish church life.

**Sweden:** [[Johan_Olof_Wallin]] (1779-1839), Archbishop of Uppsala, revised and largely composed the Swedish hymnal of 1819. His work is considered the classical age of Swedish hymnody.

**Norway** and the other Scandinavian churches developed their own traditions in dialogue with both the German source and their own folk musical heritage.

## Into English

The Lutheran treasury was largely inaccessible to English readers until the nineteenth century, when [[Catherine_Winkworth]] (1827-1878) accomplished one of the most important acts of translation in the history of hymnody. Her *Lyra Germanica* (1855) and *Chorale Book for England* (1863) gave English congregations Paul Gerhardt, Philipp Nicolai, and dozens of other German masters in verse that could actually be sung. The debt of English hymnody to Catherine Winkworth is enormous.

## The Character of Lutheran Hymnody

Across five centuries and several national traditions, Lutheran hymnody is marked by:

- **Doctrinal substance**: These are hymns that teach, not merely express feeling
- **Corporate voice**: Even the most personal of these hymns often speaks in the first-person plural --- *we*, not just *I*
- **Musical seriousness**: The chorale tradition demands singable tunes of structural integrity
- **Eschatological hope**: Death, resurrection, and heaven are constant themes, forged in the crucible of the Thirty Years' War and the plague years
- **Cross-centered devotion**: Christ's passion and atonement are the recurring center
