# Anglican Hymnody

> [[_overview|Home]] > [[_index|Index]] > Concepts

The Church of England came late to original hymnody. For the better part of two centuries after the Reformation, the established church sang metrical Psalms and little else --- suspicious of "human compositions" in worship, content with the crude versifications of Sternhold and Hopkins that even their admirers could not call beautiful. When the dam finally broke, the flood was magnificent.

## The Long Drought: Metrical Psalmody

The Church of England's early worship music was dominated by the metrical Psalter. Sternhold and Hopkins (1562) was the standard for generations, supplemented eventually by the Tate and Brady "New Version" of 1696 (the work of [[Nahum_Tate]] and [[Nicholas_Brady]]). These versifications served a theological purpose --- keeping the congregation to Scripture --- but they were not poetry, and the most honest of churchmen admitted it.

[[Joseph_Addison]], the celebrated essayist, appended five hymns of rare beauty to his *Spectator* essays in the early eighteenth century. They were widely admired, but written for private devotion rather than congregational use. The wall against human composition remained standing in most parishes.

## The First Hymn-Writer: Thomas Ken

The honor of being England's first hymn-writer of note belongs to Bishop [[Thomas_Ken]] (1637-1711). He wrote three hymns for the students of Winchester College --- a morning hymn, an evening hymn, and a midnight hymn --- not for congregational use but for private devotion. The Doxology appended to his morning and evening hymns ("Praise God from whom all blessings flow") has been sung at more services than any other piece of Christian verse.

Ken's life was as principled as his verse. He refused to vacate his house to accommodate King Charles II's mistress, was appointed Bishop of Bath and Wells for his boldness, and later refused to take the oath to William and Mary and was deprived of his see. Macaulay said he came as near to the ideal of Christian perfection "as human weakness permits."

## The Nonconformist Catalyst: Isaac Watts

Here the story grows complicated. [[Isaac_Watts]] (1674-1748) was not an Anglican but a Nonconformist --- a Dissenter from the Church of England. Yet his influence on Anglican hymnody is incalculable. His *Hymns and Spiritual Songs* (1707) demonstrated that original English hymns were possible, desirable, and capable of genuine quality. Anglican clergy could not ignore the evidence.

Watts changed what English Christians expected from congregational song. After him, the question was no longer whether original hymns belonged in worship but simply who would write them well enough.

## The Olney Hymns and Anglican Evangelicalism

The [[Olney_Hymns]] (1779), produced by the curate [[John_Newton]] and his parishioner [[William_Cowper]], represent the Anglican Evangelical stream at its finest. Newton and Cowper were both within the Church of England, and their collection was written for an Anglican parish. The hymns they produced --- intimate, theologically rich, born of real experience --- broke the final resistance of educated Anglicans to original congregational song.

## The Victorian Flowering

The nineteenth century was the great age of Anglican hymnody, and three forces shaped it.

**The Evangelical stream** continued Newton's legacy. [[Reginald_Heber]] (1783-1826) brought genuine poetry to Anglican worship. [[Henry_Francis_Lyte]] (1793-1847) added personal depth. [[Sabine_Baring_Gould]] (1834-1924) contributed with characteristic energy and occasional haste.

**The Oxford Movement** transformed Anglican liturgy and, with it, Anglican music. [[John_Keble]] (1792-1866), whose *Christian Year* (1827) had already become a devotional classic, gave the movement its devotional spirit. [[John_Henry_Newman]] (1801-1890), before his eventual move to Rome, wrote hymns of remarkable beauty. Most important for hymnody was [[John_Mason_Neale]] (1818-1866), whose translations from Greek and Latin opened the entire treasury of [[Medieval_Hymnody]] and [[Early_Christian_Hymnody]] to Anglican congregations. *Hymns Ancient and Modern* (1861), the great synthesis hymnal of the Oxford Movement era, defined Anglican worship for generations.

**The women hymn-writers** contributed from within and around the Anglican tradition. [[Charlotte_Elliott]] (1789-1871), [[Frances_Ridley_Havergal]] (1836-1879), and others wrote hymns of personal faith that transcended denominational boundaries. [[Frederick_William_Faber]] (1814-1863) brought a Catholic convert's devotional intensity back into the tradition before his departure for Rome.

## Roman Catholic Converts

The Oxford Movement's trajectory carried several of its most gifted members into Roman Catholicism, most notably Newman and Faber. Their hymns remained in Anglican (and broader Protestant) use regardless --- another example of how the best sacred poetry refuses to stay within denominational fences.

## Character of the Anglican Tradition

Anglican hymnody at its best is marked by:

- **Poetic seriousness**: From Ken to Heber to Lyte, Anglican hymn-writers valued genuine literary quality
- **Breadth of tradition**: Through Neale's translations, Anglican worship drew on fifteen centuries of Christian song
- **Liturgical rootedness**: Anglican hymns tend to be shaped by the rhythms of the church year and the cadences of the Book of Common Prayer
- **Doctrinal range**: The breadth of the Church of England itself --- Evangelical, Anglo-Catholic, Broad --- is reflected in the variety of its hymnody

## Institutional History (Benson)

Benson's *The English Hymn* (1915) provides the most detailed account available of how the Church of England came to accept congregational hymnody — a process that was slower, harder, and more contested than later histories suggest.

**The First Attempt: John Playford (1671).** The earliest systematic effort to introduce hymns into Anglican parish worship was made by [[John_Playford]], parish clerk of the Temple Church, who in 1671 published *Psalms and Hymns in Solemn Musick*, including hymns of human composition alongside the metrical psalms. The attempt failed completely. The Church was not ready. Playford then gave the Church what it would accept: in 1677 he published his standard Psalter, which dominated Anglican congregational song for roughly a century.

**The Long Resistance.** Through the eighteenth century — the era of Watts' revolution in Nonconformist worship and Wesley's transformation of popular religion — the Church of England remained committed to metrical psalms. Evangelical clergy within the Church felt the lack. The "Olney Hymns" (1779) were written for an Anglican parish, but their circulation was primarily among Evangelicals who used them informally rather than as part of the official service. The Church's official position remained one of psalms only.

**The Evangelical Campaign: Heber and the Hymnal.** The most sustained effort to give the Church of England a proper hymnal before the Oxford Movement was Reginald Heber's decades-long project. Heber composed hymns from around 1811, submitted them for approval to Archbishop Blomfield, and was refused. He continued composing and eventually, in 1821, obtained permission to publish — but the volume, *Hymns Written and Adapted to the Weekly Church Service of the Year* (1827), appeared only after his death. This first genuine Anglican hymnal, Benson notes, remained a relatively personal rather than institutional achievement.

**The Oxford Movement's Transformation.** The Oxford Movement transformed the situation entirely. Beginning in the 1830s with the Breviary translations of Newman, Williams, and Chandler, continuing through Neale's great work of the 1840s-1850s, and culminating in [[Hymns_Ancient_and_Modern]] (1861), the movement gave the Church of England what Benson calls a "Liturgical Hymn" — a body of congregational song ordered by the Christian year, drawing on the full heritage of ancient and medieval hymnody, and provided with music of genuine quality.

The success was immediate and enormous. *Hymns Ancient and Modern* — with its Dykes tunes and its liturgical organization — transformed Anglican worship and, beyond Anglican worship, the whole ideal of English congregational song. Benson traces how Nonconformist denominations gradually absorbed the Oxford ideals, shifting from the homiletical to the devotional conception of hymnody.

**The Evangelical Hymnodic Tradition Within the Church.** Alongside the Oxford stream, the Church of England maintained a strong Evangelical hymnodic tradition. Writers including Charlotte Elliott, Frances Ridley Havergal, Sabine Baring Gould, Bishop W. Walsham How, and others continued to produce hymns that were less liturgically oriented than the Oxford school but deeply valued in Evangelical parishes and widely sung across denominations.
