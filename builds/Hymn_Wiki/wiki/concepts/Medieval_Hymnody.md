# Medieval Hymnody

> [[_overview|Home]] > [[_index|Index]] > Concepts

Between the great patristic age and the Reformation lies nearly a thousand years of Christian song --- the medieval period, stretching roughly from the seventh to the fifteenth century. It is an era too easily overlooked by Protestant readers, but it produced some of the most beloved hymns in the entire tradition, many of them still in use wherever Christians worship.

## The Monastic Heartbeat

The monasteries were the hymn factories of the medieval church. While the Gregorian reform of the late sixth century had drawn congregational singing out of the hands of ordinary worshippers and given it to trained choirs, the monks kept the tradition of sacred song alive and continuously developing. The Divine Office --- the cycle of eight daily prayer services prescribed by the Rule of Saint Benedict --- required hymns for every hour, every day, every season of the liturgical year. Thousands of Latin hymns were written to meet this demand.

The result was an immense treasury. Much of it is anonymous; the individual voice was not prized in the medieval cloister as it would be in later Protestant hymnody. What mattered was the prayer of the community, offered in beauty to God.

## Sequences and Tropes

The medieval period gave hymnody two important new forms. The **sequence** was a melodic extension of the Alleluia chant, gradually filled in with words; it allowed for longer, more complex poetic structures than the Ambrosian hymn pattern. The **trope** was an interpolation of words into existing chant, expanding its meaning.

Of all the sequences ever written, one stands utterly alone: the *Dies Irae* ("Day of Wrath"), attributed to [[Thomas_of_Celano]], a companion of Saint Francis of Assisi. Its thundering vision of the Last Judgment --- translated into English as "Day of wrath, O day of mourning" --- became one of the most celebrated poems of the Middle Ages and was incorporated into the Roman Requiem Mass. Its terrifying grandeur has never been surpassed in sacred verse.

Thomas Aquinas's sequence for the feast of Corpus Christi, the *Pange Lingua*, shows the other face of medieval hymnody: precise theological poetry, every phrase weighed and balanced. [[Thomas_Aquinas]], the greatest scholastic theologian, was also a genuine hymn-writer.

## The Two Bernards

Two towering figures of the twelfth century left a permanent mark on Christian devotional song.

[[Bernard_of_Clairvaux]] (1090-1153), the fiery abbot and theologian, wrote with a warmth for the person of Jesus that was remarkable in his age. A long Latin poem of his, beginning *Jesu dulcis memoria* ("Jesus, the very thought of Thee"), has given the church some of its most tender devotional hymns. The spirit of personal love for Christ that would burst into full flower with [[Pietism]] centuries later was already alive in Bernard's cloister.

[[Bernard_of_Cluny]] (12th century), a monk of the great monastery at Cluny, wrote an extended Latin poem from which John Mason Neale drew the English hymn "Jerusalem the golden." Where Bernard of Clairvaux sang of the present warmth of Christ's love, Bernard of Cluny sang of heaven --- its beauty, its permanence, its welcome rest for weary pilgrims.

## The Gregorian Reform and Congregational Song

A word must be said about the shadow side of this period. The Gregorian reform, whatever its musical achievements, largely silenced the ordinary Christian in worship. The elaborate plainchant of the trained choir was not music for untrained voices. The congregation became audience rather than participant.

This silence was not absolute --- popular religious songs in vernacular languages (the German *Leise*, for instance) continued alongside the Latin liturgy. But the golden age of congregational singing that had flourished under [[Ambrose_of_Milan]] and in the early church would not return until [[Martin_Luther]] put hymns back in the people's hands in the sixteenth century.

## Legacy

The medieval period's contribution to living hymnody is larger than many realize. The translations of [[John_Mason_Neale]] in the nineteenth century gave Protestant congregations direct access to this treasury. "O come, O come, Emmanuel," "Good King Wenceslas," "Jerusalem the golden," "Come, ye faithful, raise the strain" --- all are medieval Latin or Greek originals in English dress.
