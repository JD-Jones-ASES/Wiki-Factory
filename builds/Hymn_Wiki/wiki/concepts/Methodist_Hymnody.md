# Methodist Hymnody

The Methodist revival was, among other things, a singing revival. John Wesley preached --- forty thousand sermons in his lifetime. Charles Wesley sang. And the movement he gave voice to swept England and eventually the world, not through preaching alone but through the hymns that gave the people of the revival something to sing long after they had gone home from the preaching service.

Ryden puts it plainly: *"The real secret of the success of the Wesleyan movement more likely must be sought in the sublime hymns written by his brother Charles."*

## Charles Wesley and the Foundation

[[Charles_Wesley]] (1707-1788) was not the "father" of Methodist hymnody --- that title belongs more properly to [[Isaac_Watts]] for English hymnody generally --- but he was its greatest practitioner. He wrote over 6,500 hymns, and among them are some of the finest sacred lyrics in the English language.

The circumstances of his conversion explain the passion of his hymns. In 1735, John and Charles Wesley sailed for the colony of Georgia as missionaries. On the voyage, a terrible storm struck, and the Wesley brothers were struck by the calm courage of a group of Moravian passengers, who sang hymns amid the raging sea. The brothers made the painful discovery that they themselves lacked that deep assurance. Years later, encountering Luther's teaching about grace through the Moravians in London, both brothers came to living faith --- Charles during a severe illness, John at the famous Aldersgate Street meeting where his heart was "strangely warmed."

Out of this experience the great flood of Methodist song began. Charles wrote on horseback, in stage-coaches, on the decks of ships, even on his deathbed. The famous trio at the summit of his output --- "Hark! the herald angels sing," "Love divine, all love excelling," and "Jesus, Lover of my soul" --- has probably never been equaled by any single author.

## The Olney Hymns

The Olney Hymns (1779) represent a different stream within the broad Methodist-influenced Evangelical tradition. They were produced not by John Wesley's movement directly but by two men who moved within the orbit of the Anglican Evangelical revival: [[John_Newton]] (1725-1807) and [[William_Cowper]] (1731-1800).

Newton, the former slave-ship captain turned minister of Olney, proposed the joint hymn-writing project partly to occupy the increasingly troubled mind of his friend Cowper. The collection of 349 hymns --- 66 by Cowper and the rest by Newton --- contains "Amazing Grace," "How sweet the Name of Jesus sounds," "God moves in a mysterious way," "O for a closer walk with God," and "There is a fountain filled with blood."

The theological contrast between the two men reflects the broad tent of Methodist-influenced Evangelicalism: Newton's robust confidence in grace received out of a dramatic past, Cowper's trembling faith maintained through mental affliction and doubt.

## The Hymns as Theology

Methodist hymnody was always doctrinal. Charles Wesley was not writing praise choruses; he was writing theology in verse. The great Wesleyan hymns cover:

- **Conversion and new birth**: "And can it be that I should gain" and "O for a thousand tongues to sing"
- **Sanctification**: "Love divine, all love excelling" (entire sanctification in lyrical form)
- **Atonement**: "O sacred head now wounded" (translation), "Arise, my soul, arise"
- **Incarnation and Christmas**: "Hark! the herald angels sing"
- **The soul's refuge in Christ**: "Jesus, Lover of my soul"
- **Providence**: (Cowper) "God moves in a mysterious way"
- **Grace freely given**: (Newton) "Amazing Grace"

## The Spread of Methodist Hymns

The Methodist movement spread rapidly through England, then to America, then around the world. Wherever Methodist missionaries went, they carried the hymn-books. Of the 770 hymns in the Wesleyan Hymn Book, 623 are from Charles Wesley's pen alone.

The influence of Methodist hymn forms also shaped hymnody outside the movement. The characteristic Wesleyan warmth --- the direct address to Christ, the personal testimony, the note of assurance --- permeated English and American Protestant worship more broadly, and can be traced in the [[Gospel_Songs]] movement of the late nineteenth century.

## The Watts-Wesley Comparison

Whether [[Isaac_Watts]] or [[Charles_Wesley]] holds first place among English hymnists has long been debated. Dr. Breed's comparison is quoted by Ryden as memorable:

*"Watts is more reverential; Wesley more loving. Watts is stronger; Wesley sweeter. Watts appeals profoundly to the intellect; Wesley takes hold of the heart. Watts will continue to sing for the Pauls and Peters of the Church; Wesley for the Thomases and the Johns."*

The Church needs both. Watts writes from the mountaintop, contemplating the awesome majesty of God; Wesley writes from the valley, reaching up to embrace a Saviour.

## Institutional History (Benson)

Benson's *The English Hymn* (1915) provides the most detailed institutional account of how Methodist hymnody was organized, administered, and transmitted — treating John Wesley not primarily as a theologian but as the most brilliant administrator of congregational song in Protestant history.

**John Wesley as Administrator of Song.** While Charles Wesley wrote the hymns, John Wesley governed their use with extraordinary care. His 1780 *Collection of Hymns for the Use of the People Called Methodists* — reduced from the vast output of Charles and others to a working collection — was the supreme example of editorial judgment applied to practical worship. John's preface defended the collection as a "little body of practical divinity," arguing that the hymns covered the full range of Christian experience and doctrine more completely than any other single volume. His instructions about how hymns were to be sung were equally precise: no "florid" style, no harmonies, no ornament — only "clear, firm, and slow" congregational singing.

**The Methodist Tune Books.** Benson traces the development of Methodist tune books from the early revival publications through the organized denominational system. John Wesley employed various musicians including William Tans'ur to provide tunes; the early Methodist societies often sang to popular secular tunes, a practice Wesley defended pragmatically ("Why should the devil have all the best tunes?"). As Methodism institutionalized, so did its musical practices, and the tune books became more standardized.

**Revisions and Expansions.** The Methodist hymnal was revised repeatedly over the nineteenth century as American Methodism grew and diversified. The major revisions of 1847 and 1905 represented the denomination's efforts to maintain quality while accommodating growth. Benson notes that the 1905 Methodist Hymnal was notable for its literary ambition — its editors reached beyond the traditional Wesleyan canon to include hymns from other traditions and even secular poets.

**The Moravian Influence.** Benson's account is careful to trace the Moravian roots of Methodist hymnody. The Wesleys' encounter with Moravian singing on the Atlantic crossing in 1736, their visits to Herrnhut, and John Wesley's translations of Zinzendorf's hymns all contributed to the distinctive character of early Methodist song. Wesley eventually broke with the Moravians over theology (particularly their quietist "stillness" doctrine), but the devotional warmth and singing culture he had encountered among them remained a permanent feature of the Methodist movement.

**American Methodist Hymnody.** In America, the Methodist movement developed its own hymnodic character alongside and in tension with the dominant Wesleyan inheritance. The camp meeting tradition — born at the Kentucky Revival of 1800 and spreading through the frontier circuits — produced a distinctly American form of Methodist song that was both an extension of and a departure from Wesley's intentions. The Camp Meeting Hymn's refrain-chorus structure, its folk-song tunes, and its individualistic salvation language all represented a democratization of the Wesleyan impulse that John Wesley himself would not have recognized.

Benson documents the eventual Methodist Episcopal Church hymnals as attempting to hold together the Wesleyan inheritance and the American popular tradition — with varying success.
