# Practical Hymnology

Practical hymnology is the study and use of hymns oriented toward their actual effectiveness in worship, teaching, evangelism, and the spiritual formation of congregations. It is distinguished from purely literary or historical study of hymnody by its insistence that the final measure of a hymn is not its poetic quality or its place in literary history, but its capacity to produce concrete spiritual results among the people who sing it.

## Lorenz's Framework

Edmund S. Lorenz's *The Singing Church* (1938) is organized around this practical orientation. His definition of the congregational hymn is itself a practical one: a poem expressing worship, praise, thanksgiving, prayer, personal spiritual experience, and religious instruction, adapted to mass thinking and expression, fitted to be sung by a Christian congregation, and calculated to express and stimulate religious feeling and purpose.

The key word is "calculated." The practical hymnologist asks not whether a hymn is beautiful in the abstract, but whether it accomplishes its intended work in the specific setting where it is used. Lorenz insists that etymological, scholastic, traditional, or abstractly idealistic considerations "can have only minor weight" compared to the practical basis of actual spiritual results.

## Scriptural Foundation

Lorenz grounds his practical approach in the Pauline letters. Paul's exhortation to the Ephesians to be "filled with the Spirit" manifests itself in "speaking to yourselves in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, singing and making melody in your heart to the Lord" (**Ephesians 5:18-19**). To the Colossians he writes: "Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly in all wisdom; teaching and admonishing one another in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, singing with grace in your hearts to the Lord" (**Colossians 3:16**). And Paul insists that his own singing is both devout and intelligent: "I will sing with the spirit, and I will sing with the understanding also" (**1 Corinthians 14:15**).

These passages establish three principles that Lorenz takes as foundational: hymn-singing should be Spirit-filled, it should serve both worship and mutual instruction, and it should engage the understanding as well as the emotions.

## The Purposes of Congregational Singing

Lorenz identifies a rich taxonomy of purposes that hymns serve in congregational life:

**Uniting Christians in worship.** Song is the most practicable method of joining an assembly in common devotion. Prayer, when spontaneous, is largely individual; ritual prayer risks mechanical stiffness. But hymns allow varied sentiment, and the collective expression intensifies individual devotion.

**Concentrating attention.** Common thought, common emotion, and common expression produce a mass attitude in which abstract doctrines about God as Father, Christ as Savior, and the Holy Spirit as Comforter "quicken into blessed realities."

**Giving voice to experience.** Many Christians are unable to articulate their deepest spiritual experiences. The hymns written by saints who possessed the gift of expression unstop the silent tongue and validate experiences that might otherwise seem illusory.

**Teaching Christian truth.** Lorenz follows Paul in recognizing the didactic function of hymns. A hymn that teaches doctrine is not merely a concession to practical needs; it is one of the oldest and most effective forms of Christian instruction. [[John_Wesley]]'s description of his 1780 hymnal as "a little body of experimental and practical divinity" captures this function precisely.

**Providing comfort.** Hymns that express the experiences of saints across centuries reassure the believer that others have shared the same mountain-top visions and the same dark valleys.

**Creating religious atmosphere.** Even unconverted persons attending a service are affected by the atmosphere created by congregational singing, and this atmosphere may become the beginning of spiritual awakening.

## The Minister's Responsibility

Lorenz is pointed in his criticism of ministerial indifference to hymns. He estimates that three out of five ministers have no adequate conception of the possibilities of hymn-singing. The janitor postpones noisy tasks during the sermon but feels free to shake down the furnace during the hymn. Congregants seize the hymn as an opportunity to leave the room or conduct audible conversations about church business.

Against this, Lorenz insists that congregational song is "the supreme element in all worship" --- the one exercise in which individual self-consciousness is most fully lost in collective adoration. The minister who understands this will study his hymnbook as diligently as his Bible, will select hymns with the same care he gives to his sermon, and will lead his congregation into an experience of hymn-singing that transforms the service.

## Practical Criteria for Hymn Selection

From the six chapters of Part I of *The Singing Church*, Lorenz's practical criteria can be summarized:

1. **Emotional genuineness** --- the hymn must arise from or produce authentic religious feeling, not mere animal excitement
2. **Clarity** --- the thought must lie on the surface, grasped instantly by ordinary people
3. **Singability** --- the meter must be regular, the words euphonious, the stanzas self-contained
4. **Theological substance** --- the hymn must be distinctly Christian and grounded in scriptural truth
5. **Congregational fitness** --- the hymn must express what the whole congregation can honestly sing, avoiding extravagant individualism or experiences beyond the reach of ordinary believers
6. **Situational appropriateness** --- different occasions call for different kinds of hymns, and a hymn that is inferior in the abstract may be superior for a specific purpose

## The Hymn in the Home

Lorenz extends practical hymnology beyond the church service to the Christian household. Family singing of hymns sanctifies domestic life and stores the memories of children with the great songs of the faith. He cites [[Ira_Sankey]]'s formative experience singing hymns around the family hearth. The decline of hymn-singing in the home, alongside the neglect of family devotions, represents for Lorenz a significant loss to Christian nurture.

## Hymns and Historical Movements

The practical power of hymnody is demonstrated by its role in every major Christian movement. The victory over Arianism was a singing victory. The Crusades were marked by processional song. The Lollards and Hussites heralded the Reformation with popular hymns. [[Isaac_Watts]] prepared the way for the Wesleyan revival; the Wesleys made that path a great highway of Christian song. Every successful denomination and individual church has been pre-eminently a singing church.
