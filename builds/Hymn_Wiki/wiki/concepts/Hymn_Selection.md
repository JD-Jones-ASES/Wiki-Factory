# Hymn Selection

In *[[The_Singing_Church]]*, [[Edmund_S_Lorenz]] treats the selection of hymns as second in importance only to the minister's choice of sermon text. Poor selection --- mechanical, thoughtless, or ego-driven --- undermines the entire worship service before the sermon even begins. Wise selection, guided by a clear sense of purpose and a thorough knowledge of the hymnal, transforms singing from routine into a genuine means of spiritual encounter.

## Unity of Service, Not Uniformity of Theme

Lorenz criticizes what he calls the "narrow conception of unity," in which every element of worship is yoked to the sermon topic. Under this approach, hymns become merely didactic previews of the discourse, and the congregation --- unaware of the coming subject --- gains nothing from the forced connection.

Instead, Lorenz advocates a unity of purpose. The whole service should bring the soul into conscious relationship with God, and each element --- hymns, prayers, Scripture, sermon --- should contribute to that shared spiritual aim. He quotes the nineteenth-century theologian Austin Phelps, who counseled resemblance of spirit rather than sameness of theme: hymns should lie in the same emotional landscape as the sermon without mechanically restating its points.

The governing question is not "What is my sermon about?" but "What spiritual result do I want this service to achieve?"

## Matching Hymns to Emotional Arc

Lorenz teaches that effective hymn selection follows an emotional progression through the service.

**Opening hymns** should be worshipful and unifying, drawing scattered minds into a shared sacred mood. For a solemn service, hymns like "Holy, holy, holy" or "Before Jehovah's awful throne" (by [[Isaac_Watts]]) set the tone. For a more animated occasion, "Come, Thou Almighty King" or "Oh, worship the King" provide dignity with movement.

**Middle hymns** deepen the mood --- more prayerful, more intimate, or more aggressive depending on the service's aim. These may shift the emotional register: tenderness after majesty, or courage after consolation.

**Closing hymns** should capture the emotional summit of the entire service, not merely summarize the sermon's ideas. After a discourse on God's omnipotence, the congregation is ready for an exhilarating hymn of praise. After a tender message on divine consolation, the closing hymn should release faith and joy.

## Illustrative Programs

Lorenz provides detailed sample service plans to show these principles in action.

**A service on God's omnipotence** might open with Watts's majestic "Before Jehovah's awful throne" to the tune OLD HUNDREDTH, move to the more spirited "Oh, worship the King" for the second hymn, and close with "Let all on earth their voices raise" to the livelier tune ARIEL --- a progression from awe through adoration to joyful exhilaration.

**A service on God's consoling love** calls for a softer palette: a gentle organ prelude, a quiet introit, opening with "Lord, we come before Thee now," building to [[Charles_Wesley]]'s "Love divine, all loves excelling," and closing with the confident "How firm a foundation."

**A missionary service** demands aggressive, stirring energy: Wesley's "Oh, for a thousand tongues to sing," then "Onward, Christian soldiers" or "Lead on, O King Eternal," building to the climactic "Jesus shall reign where'er the sun" (Watts's Christianized version of Psalm 72).

## The Importance of Tunes

Lorenz insists that hymn and tune must be evaluated together. A great text paired with a dull or impractical tune will fail. The minister should study the tunes in the hymnal, re-pairing hymns and tunes where needed, always asking: Is this tune singable by my particular congregation? Does it express and intensify the hymn's sentiment? Lorenz points to [[Fanny_Crosby]]'s lyrics, which owe much of their reach to the melodies of Lowry and Doane, and to [[Charles_Wesley]]'s "Hark, the herald angels sing," whose universal Christmas use depends on Mendelssohn's setting.

## The Sin of Thoughtless Omission

One of Lorenz's sharpest criticisms targets the habitual, unthinking omission of hymn verses. Ministers who routinely announce "We will sing the first three verses" --- regardless of the hymn's structure --- mutilate the text, destroy its progression of thought, and may cut its finest stanza. This practice, Lorenz argues, reflects an egotism that prizes sermon time over the congregation's worship. He invokes **Proverbs 11:24** to warn that withholding the hymn service impoverishes the very sermon it was meant to protect.
