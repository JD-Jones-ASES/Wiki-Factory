# Literary Quality in Hymns

The question of literary quality in hymns is one of the persistent tensions in hymnody: the relationship between poetic craftsmanship and spiritual effectiveness, and whether the standards applied to secular literature can fairly be applied to a form of writing whose primary purpose is congregational worship. Lorenz devotes Chapter III of *The Singing Church* (1938) to this question, arguing that the hymn is genuine literature by the most vital definition of the term, while also insisting that literary quality must never become the supreme criterion of a hymn's value.

## The Case for Hymns as Literature

Lorenz builds his case on three arguments.

**Transcript of life.** In so far as a hymn is a genuine transcript of deep conviction intensified by emotion, or of profound experience, it is literature by any meaningful definition. Vision, feeling, imagination, sincerity, and intimate experience go into the best hymns. It is not meter or rhyme that confers literary standing, but the presence of authentic human experience seeking expression.

**Unprecedented distribution.** The hymnbook has had wider distribution than any other form of literature. Lorenz cites three million copies of the Methodist Hymnal in the United States, sixty million copies of *Hymns Ancient and Modern* across the British Empire, and additional millions of other collections. A single hymnal has had more active readers than all the poetry in the world combined, ancient and modern. Where an edition of Palgrave's *Golden Treasury* might take twenty years to sell a hundred thousand copies, hymnals circulated in the millions and were not shelved for occasional reference but used repeatedly across lifetimes.

**Acceptance across generations.** The core body of hymns shared by most hymnals has been culled from centuries of sacred verse by the consensus of different generations, backgrounds, cultures, peoples, and races. This unanimity of responsiveness through continued use across diverse populations constitutes, in Lorenz's view, a more searching literary test than the judgment of any academic critic.

## The Critics and Their Objections

Lorenz surveys the critical tradition with characteristic directness.

**Samuel Johnson** dismissed the possibility that contemplative piety could be poetical, arguing that the narrowness of religious subject matter "enforces perpetual repetition" and that the sanctity of the content "rejects the ornament of figurative diction." Lorenz observes in mitigation that the golden age of English hymnody had not yet arrived in Johnson's time.

**Matthew Arnold** fully grasped the hymn's wide influence but had no sympathy with its spirit or purpose. His pronouncement that hymns were a form of composition he did "not at all admire" represents, for Lorenz, the anti-religious rationalism of the literary establishment at its furthest reach.

**Professor Boynton** of the *Cambridge History of American Literature* gave as much space to "Yankee Doodle" as to American hymnody and dismissed the tradition as characterized by "sentimental ornateness" and "banalities of evangelistic song" --- a judgment Lorenz regards as more revealing of the critic's spiritual condition than of the hymns criticized.

## The Handicaps of the Hymn Writer

Alfred Tennyson recognized the difficulty: "A good hymn is the most difficult thing in the world to write!" Lorenz identifies the specific constraints that make the hymn a uniquely demanding literary form.

The writer must limit thought to the comprehension and natural feelings of an entire congregation. Unusual or polysyllabic words are forbidden. Striking figures, involved similes, obscure metaphors, and allusions known only to the few are all out of place. Every stanza must be complete in itself, regardless of its role in the hymn's larger argument. There must be unity, simplicity, and condensation of thought, combined with a clarity that permits instant comprehension during singing. The hymn writer, as Lorenz puts it, "is like a violinist called upon to play on a single string."

The meter imposes further limits. Most congregational tunes require iambic or trochaic measure, and the range of available stanza forms is narrow --- about a dozen fundamental meters. The character of each meter constrains what can be expressed: Long Meter is stately and dignified, suited to noble praise; Common Meter is the most adaptable form, accommodating everything from elevated worship to tender devotion; Short Meter is sententious and emphatic, risking choppiness if not handled carefully.

## The Tension: Literary Grace vs. Spiritual Power

Lorenz frames the central tension with care. The emphasis on literary and poetical elements has produced some of the finest sacred lyrics --- notably the hymns of [[John_Keble]] and [[Reginald_Heber]]. But it has also occasionally led to such refinement, subtlety, and conscious artistry that the virility and emotional power of what might otherwise have been an effective hymn are lost.

[[John_Newton]], the great hymn writer, argued for restraint: imagery and the coloring of poetry, if admitted at all, "should be indulged in very sparingly and with great judgment." Sir Roundell Palmer warned that "affectation or visible artifice is worse than excess of homeliness; a hymn is easily spoiled by a single falsetto note."

Lorenz's own position is that literary quality should be felt in the spirit of the hymn rather than pursued as a conscious aim. Dr. Louis F. Benson articulated the danger precisely: the hazard of hymn-singing is "mistaking sugary sentiment for true feeling and its rhetorical expression in 'soft, luxurious flow' for true poetry." The conscious seeking of literary atmosphere is fatal to genuineness of feeling.

## Practical Use as the Final Criterion

[[Isaac_Watts]] and [[John_Newton]] deliberately ignored and even avoided literary values, yet produced some of the most useful hymns in the English language. [[Charles_Wesley]] was a genuine poet, but his valuable hymn "A charge to keep I have" is, by Lorenz's frank assessment, the work of a pedagogue without poetic inspiration --- and yet it has served the Church for centuries.

Lorenz concludes that the two essentials of a successful hymn are spirituality and the power to express it so that it reaches both the understanding and the hearts of the people who sing. Paul's formulation remains the standard: "I will sing with the spirit, and I will sing with the understanding also" (**1 Corinthians 14:15**).

The hymns that would have satisfied literary critics --- with their spiritual delicacy, elegance of phrase, and vagueness of religious idea --- would have been shut out from use in the workaday world. To set aside the practical purpose of the hymn in evaluating it, as Arnold proposed, is to ignore both its reason for existence and the deepest needs of the human soul.
