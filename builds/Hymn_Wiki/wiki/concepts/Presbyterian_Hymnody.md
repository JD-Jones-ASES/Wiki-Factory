# Presbyterian Hymnody

The story of Presbyterian hymnody is, more than any other Protestant denominational story, the story of the Psalmody Controversy — the long, hard debate over whether Calvin's Reformed tradition should sing only metrical psalms or whether hymns of human composition had a place in Christian worship. That debate shaped two centuries of Presbyterian church music and determined when and how different Presbyterian communities entered the broader stream of English hymnody.

## Calvin's Inheritance and the Psalter Tradition

The Presbyterian tradition — in both its Scottish and English forms — received from Calvin an absolute preference for the singing of Scripture in metrical form. Calvin's Geneva Psalter (completed 1562) was the model, and the Scottish Psalter (1650) and Sternhold-Hopkins English Psalter (1562) were its equivalents in the British contexts. For a century and a half after the Reformation, the question for English and Scottish Presbyterians was not which hymns to sing, but which Psalter to use.

This was not simply a matter of aesthetic conservatism. The theological argument was principled: only God's own words, properly versified, could be sung in his presence; human compositions, however devout, introduced human authority where divine authority was required. The Genevan tradition was consistent on this point, and Scottish Presbyterianism in particular maintained it with great tenacity.

## The Ejected Presbyterians and the Hymn Movement

The first sustained challenge to this tradition within English Presbyterian life came not from within the established church but from the ejected ministers of 1662. Among the Nonconformists thrown out of their Church of England livings by the Act of Uniformity, a number — led above all by [[Richard_Baxter]] — began arguing and writing in favor of congregational hymn-singing.

Benson's account identifies Baxter as "the most influential of all the 'Ejected' ministers" in this movement. Through his support of William Barton's hymn collections, beginning with *A Century of Select Hymns* (1659), and through his own hymn-writing, Baxter made the case within the ejected Presbyterian community that the tradition of Psalm-singing was impoverishing English worship — stripping it of the New Testament gospel and confining it to an Old Testament framework that did not adequately express Christian faith.

This argument did not immediately prevail. Presbyterian and Independent congregations in England remained divided on the question through the late seventeenth century. Many of the ejected ministers, while personally sympathetic to hymns, were unwilling to impose them on their congregations. The controversy continued until the figure who resolved it arrived: [[Isaac_Watts]].

## The Watts Revolution

Watts transformed the situation in the first decade of the eighteenth century. His *Hymns and Spiritual Songs* (1707) and *Psalms of David Imitated* (1719) gave the Presbyterian and Independent churches a body of sacred song so clearly excellent that the theological objection to "human compositions" became increasingly difficult to maintain in the face of the evident value of what Watts had produced.

The Presbyterian response was not instantaneous. Scotland held to the Psalter tradition longer than England; the Scottish Presbyterian church did not effectively move to accept hymns until the nineteenth century. In England and America the process was faster, but still contested. Benson documents the gradual process by which Watts' *Psalms and Hymns* became "Watts and Select" — Watts supplemented by selected hymns from other sources — and then eventually replaced by hymnals that made no pretense of Psalter priority.

## American Presbyterian Hymnody

In America, the Presbyterian situation was shaped by the Great Awakening and its aftermath. Whitefield's Calvinistic preaching created an Evangelical party in American Presbyterianism, and this party was naturally disposed to adopt the Evangelical Hymnody rather than remaining confined to Psalmody. But the process was slow. Benson notes that it was not until 1802 that the Presbyterian General Assembly formally authorized the use of Watts' Hymns — a remarkably late date, considering how long Watts had been in use in American Congregational churches.

The American Presbyterian hymnal tradition then developed through a series of denominational collections. Timothy Dwight's revision of Watts' Psalms and Hymns was an important early step. The subsequent history involved the familiar tension between "Watts entire" as the denominational standard and the pressure for "modern" hymnody that could include more recent writers.

The Sabbath Hymn Book (1858), produced by Andover professors Edwards A. Park and Austin Phelps, was a landmark in raising the literary and scholarly standard of American Protestant hymnody generally, and its influence on Presbyterian practice was substantial.

## Charles S. Robinson and the Popular Hymnals

The provision of hymn books for Presbyterian and Congregational churches from the 1860s onward fell to a remarkable extent into the hands of one man: Dr. Charles S. Robinson, a Presbyterian pastor who made hymn-book compilation what Benson calls "a business." Between 1862 and 1892 Robinson published at least fifteen collections. His aims were popular rather than scholarly, but they were not without gradual improvement. His *Psalms and Hymns and Spiritual Songs* (1875) became the official praise book of the Southern Presbyterian Church.

## The Psalmody Controversy and Scottish Presbyterianism

Scotland presents a different and longer trajectory. The Scottish Psalter of 1650 remained the basis of Presbyterian worship in Scotland well into the nineteenth century. The controversy over introducing hymns into Scottish Presbyterian worship was bitter and theologically serious, involving questions of ecclesiastical authority as well as liturgical practice. Scottish Presbyterians did not effectively adopt hymnody alongside the psalms until the later nineteenth century, and even then some congregations and denominations maintained the exclusive Psalter tradition into the twentieth century.
