# Thirty Years' War Hymns

## The Crucible of German Hymnody

The Thirty Years' War (1618-1648) was one of the most devastating conflicts in European history. Germany was its primary theater. Towns were sacked, burned, and plague-swept repeatedly; populations were decimated; entire communities were destroyed. For the Lutherans, the war was both a military conflict and an existential religious struggle --- Catholic Imperial forces under Wallenstein and others were actively attempting to exterminate Protestantism in Germany.

And yet: from this era of unparalleled suffering came some of the grandest hymns the Christian Church has ever produced.

Ryden writes: "Although it was one of the most distressing eras in the Protestant Church, it gave birth to some of its grandest hymns."

## Why Suffering Produces Great Song

The hymnists of this period were not writing from a distance. [[Johann_Heermann]] was sacked, burned out, chased by soldiers, and nearly killed multiple times. [[Martin_Rinkart]] buried more than 4,000 of his own parishioners. [[Philipp_Nicolai]]'s two greatest hymns were written while watching 1,300 of his people die of plague in a single year. [[Paul_Gerhardt]], who came of age during the war, spent his adult life navigating its political and spiritual aftermath and losing wife and children along the way.

Their hymns are not escapism. They are the testimony of people who had stared at death and found that faith in Christ held firm. That is why they grip the heart.

## The Hymn of the Battle of Lützen

Perhaps the most dramatic story in German hymnody surrounds the battle of Lützen (November 6, 1632). Gustavus Adolphus, the Swedish king who had intervened to save Protestantism in Germany, led his army in singing Johann Michael Altenburg's hymn "Be Not Dismayed, Thou Little Flock" before the battle. He was struck by a bullet during the fighting and died of his wounds --- but the Protestants won the field. The hymn became known as the "swan-song" of Gustavus Adolphus.

Before the fog lifted that morning, Gustavus also ordered the bands to play Luther's "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God," and the army sang it as one. It was the Protestant spirit in concentrated form: a king who led his men in prayer and hymn before charging into battle.

## Key Figures and Their War-Born Hymns

### [[Philipp_Nicolai]] (1556-1608)
Technically pre-war, but his two great hymns were written during a plague that killed 1,300 of his parishioners in Unna (1597-1598) --- the same kind of suffering the war would multiply a thousandfold. "Wake, Awake, for Night Is Flying" (the King of Chorales) and "How Brightly Shines the Morning Star" (the Queen of Chorales) were the anthems the Protestant armies carried into battle.

### [[Johann_Heermann]] (1585-1647)
The most prolific of the war-era hymnists. His masterpiece, **"Ah, Holy Jesus"** (*Herzliebster Jesu*, 1630), was written while Koeben was repeatedly sacked. His "Songs of Tears" collection (1630) includes hymns that implore God's protection over his people. Two famous battlefield stories are attached to Heermann's hymns: his "O Jesus, Saviour Dear" was sung spontaneously by Prussian troops before the battle of Leuthen (1757), with Frederick the Great ordering it to continue; and his hymn "Build a Wall Around Us" was literally answered by a snowdrift that protected one family's home in Schleswig in 1814.

### [[Martin_Rinkart]] (1586-1649)
The most iconic figure of this period. His **"Now Thank We All Our God"** (*Nun danket alle Gott*) --- whether written during the war or for the family dinner table --- became the Lutheran *Te Deum*, sung to celebrate the Peace of Westphalia and every German triumph thereafter.

### Johann Michael Altenburg (1584-1640)
Author of **"Be Not Dismayed, Thou Little Flock"** (1631), the hymn Gustavus Adolphus sang before dying at Lützen. Its authorship was long disputed between German and Swedish sources; most scholars now attribute it to Altenburg. It was written upon hearing of the Protestant victory at Leipzig on September 7, 1631.

### Georg Weissel (1590-1635)
Lutheran pastor at Königsberg who wrote one of the finest Advent hymns in the German language: **"Lift Up Your Heads, Ye Mighty Gates."**

### Tobias Clausnitzer (1619-1684)
Chaplain of a Swedish regiment during the Thirty Years' War; preached the thanksgiving sermon at the field service celebrating the Peace of Westphalia. Author of **"Blessed Jesus, at Thy Word."**

### Other figures
Martin Opitz ("Light of Light, O Sun of Heaven"), Simon Dach ("O How Blest Are Ye," translated by Longfellow), Ernst Homburg ("Christ, the Life of All the Living"), Heinrich Held ("Let the Earth Now Praise the Lord"), Josua Stegmann ("Abide with Us, Our Saviour"), Johann Meyfart ("Jerusalem, Thou City Fair and High"), and Johann Quirsfeld ("Sinful World, Behold the Anguish").

## The Peace of Westphalia and After

The Peace of Westphalia (1648) ended the war. But the hymnody it had produced endured. The Lutheran church emerged from those thirty years tested, depleted in numbers --- and immeasurably enriched in song.

The tradition of war-born German hymnody flows directly into [[Paul_Gerhardt]]'s mature work (he was 41 when the war ended) and then into the broader [[Pietism|Pietist movement]] of the later seventeenth century.
