# Hymn Emendation

Hymn emendation is the practice of altering, editing, condensing, or rewriting hymns by editors and compilers other than the original authors. It is one of the most contested practices in the history of Christian hymnody, touching questions of authorial rights, literary integrity, theological accuracy, and practical worship needs.

## The Ancient Freedom

In the earliest centuries of hymnal compilation, the rights of individual hymn writers were, as Lorenz puts it, "entirely below the horizon." Hymns circulated without attribution; authorship was often uncertain. [[Charles_Wesley]]'s claim to "Jesus, Lover of my soul," for example, rests entirely on considerations of style, not documentation. There was no concept of moral copyright, much less legal protection. The guiding principle was adaptation to immediate congregational need, not fidelity to an authorial text.

[[Isaac_Watts]] freely incorporated lines and whole stanzas from other writers into his own hymns. [[John_Wesley]] rewrote lines, stanzas, and entire hymns without hesitation. [[Augustus_Toplady]] made radical revisions to Charles Wesley's work to bring it into line with his Calvinist theology. The hymn was treated as communal property, shaped and reshaped to serve the worshipping congregation.

## The Abuse of Revision

This freedom was sometimes exercised badly. Dr. Worcester's American collections of psalms and hymns, chiefly by Watts, were so extensively mutilated that New England churches revolted. "Rock of Ages" by Toplady suffered particularly: of its twenty-four lines, only eleven survived without alteration across various hymnals. "Jesus, Lover of my soul" fared even worse --- more than thirty variant readings exist for the first stanza alone. Wesley himself protested that "Hymn-cobblers should not try to mend them," yet the alterations continued unabated.

[[James_Montgomery]] spent years amending the hymns of others but asked that his own be left intact, arguing that if editors could not accept his doctrine and diction, it was questionable of them to impose theirs on him. When Montgomery himself replaced the opening verse of [[William_Cowper]]'s "There is a fountain filled with blood" with a substitute he judged "unexceptionable," hymnal editors unanimously rejected his version.

## The Return to Originals

The excesses of editorial revision produced a counter-movement. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, under the leadership of Dr. Louis F. Benson, a movement arose among hymnal editors whose principle was to recover original texts wherever possible. While not always practicable --- some editorial changes were genuine improvements --- this tendency restored many fine original phrases that had been replaced with inferior alternatives.

## Principles of Legitimate Emendation

Lorenz identifies a tension between authorial rights and congregational need, and argues that the practical purpose of the hymnal holds what he calls "the right of eminent domain." A hymnal is not an anthology; it is a tool for worship. If a hymn contains faulty lines or weak stanzas that diminish its effectiveness in congregational use, competent editors have the right to amend or condense --- provided the general spirit of the original is preserved.

Watts himself acknowledged this principle, noting that "where an unpleasing word is found, he that leads the worship may substitute a better one." He also admitted that many of his own lines needed polishing he had neither inclination nor leisure to provide.

## Types of Emendation

**Omission of stanzas** is the most common and least controversial form. "Oh for a thousand tongues to sing" originally had eighteen stanzas; the hymn as used begins at stanza seven. Bishop Brooks's "O little town of Bethlehem" lost its fourth stanza for reasons of awkward rhyme and insufficient contribution to the hymn's progress. Editors have also consistently omitted stanzas whose imagery was too graphic, too doctrinally narrow, or too dated for congregational use.

**Reconstruction and rewriting** is more radical. John Wesley --- despite his protests against "hymn-cobblers" --- was himself one of the most skillful revisers in hymnody. His transformation of Watts's "Sing to the Lord with joyful voice" into the majestic "Before Jehovah's awful throne" is a celebrated example of editorial improvement. Martin Madan changed [[Charles_Wesley]]'s "Hark how all the welkin rings" into the universally known "Hark! the herald angels sing" --- a revision that gave the world one of its best-loved Christmas hymns.

**Minor felicitous changes** include correcting misplaced accents, improving singability (replacing "inhabitest" with "Dost dwell with those" in Cowper), reducing harsh consonant clusters, and replacing archaic or misleading imagery. Bishop Ken's "Under thy own almighty wings" became the more musical "Beneath the shadow of thy wings."

## Biblical Precedent

Lorenz notes that emendation has biblical precedent: the compiler of the Psalms included a version of Psalm 18 that differs from its earlier form in 2 Samuel 22, and New Testament writers freely adapted Old Testament quotations. The psalm versifiers from Marot and [[Martin_Luther]] through Watts routinely condensed, altered, and interpolated new ideas in their renderings of the Psalms without any sense of presumption.

## The Enduring Tension

The practice remains inherently controversial. Every emendation risks losing the original writer's vision, the specific emotional quality that made the hymn effective in the first place. Yet the editor who supplies a needed critical eye, correcting an accent, clarifying a line, or removing an image that distracts rather than inspires, may rescue a hymn for generations of future worshippers. The test, as Lorenz insists, is whether the change increases the perspicuity, precision, propriety, and force of the hymn for its congregational purpose.
