# Women in Hymnody

Nineteenth century hymnody was characterized by an extraordinary number of women hymn-writers. The development came with the great spiritual revivals that aroused evangelical Europe and America from 1800 to 1875, and coincided with the general enlargement of women's influence and activity in all spheres of human endeavor.

Ryden, drawing on Breed, observes that the hymnody of the Church would have been "immeasurably poorer had these consecrated women failed to make use of their heaven-born talent."

## The Tradition Before the Nineteenth Century

Before the nineteenth century, women's contributions to hymnody were rare but not absent. In the early Church, women participated in the antiphonal singing of the early congregations. In the medieval period, women mystics like Hildegard of Bingen composed sacred music of great beauty.

But the Reformed tradition largely excluded women from any public leadership role in worship, and with it from hymn-writing as a public act. The names in the earliest centuries of English and American hymnody are almost entirely male: Ken, Watts, Wesley, Newton, Cowper, Toplady.

## The Rising Tide

The Evangelical revival changed this. The revival emphasized personal testimony, the priesthood of all believers, and the value of every soul's experience of grace. These were conditions in which women could and did speak --- first in private devotional writing, then in publication, then in wide use.

The succession in English hymnody is traced by Ryden:

**[[Charlotte_Elliott]]** (1789-1871) was the forerunner. An invalid who spent most of her eighty-two years on a sickbed, she wrote 150 hymns, chief among them "Just as I am" (1836). Her brother, a clergyman, wrote that he hoped his long ministry had produced some fruit, "but I feel far more has been done by a single hymn of my sister's." D.L. Moody called it the most soul-winning hymn ever written.

**[[Sarah_Flower_Adams]]** (1805-1848) followed with "Nearer, my God, to Thee" (1840) --- called by Ryden "one of the greatest sacred lyrics ever given to the world, and probably the finest ever written by a woman." She died at forty-three.

**Cecil Frances Alexander** (1818-1895), though not covered in detail in Ryden's account of Parts III-V, is worth noting as the author of "All things bright and beautiful," "Once in royal David's city," and "There is a green hill far away" --- perhaps the most beloved children's hymns in the English language.

**[[Frances_Ridley_Havergal]]** (1836-1879) was the "consecration poet" --- a brilliant, vivacious woman who memorized the four Gospels, the Epistles, Revelation, and all the Psalms; mastered Hebrew, Greek, and several modern languages; and died singing. Her "Take my life, and let it be" is the great surrender hymn of English Christianity. She died at forty-two.

In America, the tradition was equally rich:

**[[Fanny_Crosby]]** (1823-1915) is in a class by herself. Blind from six weeks old, she wrote approximately 8,000 hymns over fifty years. No one in the English-speaking world has written more hymns that are sung and loved. "Safe in the arms of Jesus," "Blessed assurance," "Rescue the perishing," "Pass me not, O gentle Saviour" --- they came from a woman who refused to be pitied, who turned her darkness into song.

## The Translators

The same era that produced the great women hymn-writers also produced remarkable women translators. Catherine Winkworth (1827-1878) gave the English-speaking Church access to the treasures of German hymnody through her collections *Lyra Germanica* (1855) and *Chorale Book for England* (1863). Jane Laurie Borthwick and her sister Sarah (active 1846-1877) were Edinburgh women who translated German hymns; Sarah married a Rev. Eric Findlater and continued her work in later life.

Ryden notes that the large increase of women hymnists and translators in the late nineteenth century was viewed by critics like Breed as "indicative of a period of decadence in sacred song." The criticism is not entirely without basis, but it must be weighed against the extraordinary quality of the best work these women produced.

## Common Threads

Looking across the women hymnists of the nineteenth century, several threads appear:

**Suffering as source**: Many of the great women hymn-writers wrote from genuine affliction. Charlotte Elliott was a lifelong invalid. Sarah Adams nursed her dying sister and followed her soon after. Frances Ridley Havergal died at forty-two. Fanny Crosby was blind from infancy. Their hymns speak with authority because they were born from real experience of the God who sustains the suffering.

**The intimate note**: Women hymn-writers tend toward the personal and intimate rather than the doctrinal and declarative. This is not a weakness; it is a gift. The great women hymns speak to the soul in distress more directly than many a doctrinal masterpiece.

**The invitation**: Charlotte Elliott's "Just as I am" and Fanny Crosby's invitational hymns are evangelistic in a way that most classical hymns are not. They call the hearer to come, just as they are, without waiting.

## Legacy

The rise of women in hymnody in the nineteenth century permanently changed the character of congregational worship. The note of personal testimony, intimate devotion, and direct gospel invitation that they brought is now simply part of what Christian worship sounds like. It is a gift that keeps on giving.
