# Welsh Hymnody

Wales is a nation that sings. From the era of the Druids onward, song has been the native tongue of the Welsh spirit, and when the Christian gospel took root in Cambria, it flowered into one of the most distinctive and emotionally rich traditions in all of Christian hymnody.

## Ancient Roots

The "March of the White Monks of Bangor" (7th century) is among the earliest traces of Welsh Christian song --- an epic celebrating Christian martyrs who advanced in faith toward their bloody fate at the hands of Saxon invaders. And when the invading Danes were once driven from the border in fright by an army shouting the great word of praise, the episode became known as the "Battle of the Hallelujahs." Every Welsh Christian poet since has had a hallelujah in his verse.

## The Long Night Before the Revival

For centuries the spiritual life of Wales flickered low. The independent conscience of pious Welshmen rose at intervals against religious formalism, and some suffered for it. The vicar Rhys Pritchard (1579), rescued from a life of drunkenness by a moment of humbling clarity involving his pet goat, became a changed man and wrote searching, blunt verse about the state of Welsh society. Walter Bute (1372) kept the gospel flame alive quietly; brave John Oldcastle the martyr (1417) clung to its truth; William Wroth, saved from a scene of revelry by sudden tragedy, became "the Apostle of South Wales."

It was the era when Wycliffe's New Testament --- burned and suppressed for nearly two centuries --- was passing secretly from hand to hand. Those who read it found their conscience awakened and their hallelujah stirred.

## The Great Eighteenth-Century Revival

When Howell Harris, Daniel Rowland, and their fellow "Methodist Fathers" preached across Wales in the 1740s, they were chased by mobs, ducked in ponds, and pelted with garbage. But they kept singing. The providential genius of that moment was that [[William_Williams_of_Pantycelyn]] was among them --- not as a preacher, but as a song-maker.

Williams wrote 916 hymns. He took the old Welsh country airs --- often minor-mode melodies that would sound almost weird to Anglo-Saxon ears --- and set them to sacred words. His wife Mary, whose voice was so sweet that congregations were often more moved by her singing than by Williams's preaching, performed the hymns to familiar tunes at every revival meeting. The plan was the same Luther had used in Germany two centuries before: sanctify the music the people already love.

The province, Butterworth-Brown write, "soon became a land of hymns."

## Characteristics of Welsh Hymnody

Several features distinguish Welsh sacred song:

**The minor mode.** Welsh voices find in minor-key melodies not gloom but a paradoxical joy. Their nature can make sorrow itself a gladness that the Anglo-Saxon cannot imitate. Tunes like "Cwm Rhondda," "Ebenezer," "Aberystwyth," and the Welsh minors "Clwyd," "Catharine," and "Bryn Calfaria" carry their hymns with what the Welsh call *hwyl* --- an untranslatable word for holy fervour.

**Memorization.** The Welsh learn their hymns by heart as they do the Bible --- a habit inherited from days of scarcity when pious people used memory instead of print. A Welsh prayer-meeting is never embarrassed by lack of books.

**Preferred metres.** The 7-6, 8-7, and 8-7-4 metres are great favorites, lending themselves naturally to the rhythms of Welsh national music. Even the most ancient hymns often appear in these forms.

**The covenant of peace.** Calvinistic Wales gravitates above all to the New Testament's message of Christ and His Cross. Their hymns dwell especially on the atonement --- on the blood of Calvary, the wounds of Christ, the sinner's rescue. The great Welsh "love-song" by Rev. William Rees, D.D., with its refrain of mercies boundless as the ocean, is typical of this emphasis.

## Key Figures in Welsh Hymnody

- **[[William_Williams_of_Pantycelyn]]** (1717-1791) --- "The Watts of Wales," author of 916 hymns including the world-famous "Guide Me, O Thou Great Jehovah." Called "the Sweet Singer of Wales" by his contemporaries.

- **[[Ann_Griffiths]]** (1776-1805) --- Of Dolwar Fechan, Montgomeryshire. She had a Christian life of eight years and a married life of ten months; she died at twenty-nine. Her hymns, many preserved from memory by those who heard her, leave an impression both of sublimity and mysticism.

- **Thomas Williams** (1761-1844) of Glamorganshire --- Author of the penitential "Unto Thy Presence Coming" (the *O'th flaem* hymn beloved in the 1859 revival) and the winged "O Had I the Wings of a Dove."

- **Rev. David Charles** of Carmarthen (1762-1834) --- Author of "The Heights of Fair Salem Ascended," one of the two hymns a Wrexham music publisher identified as the most popular in Wales. A heavenly-minded man, his sermons were spoken of as "apples of gold in pictures of silver."

- **Rev. Morgan Rhys** --- Schoolmaster, preacher, and friend of Williams. Author of "Lo! A Saviour for the Fallen" --- the other hymn named by the same publisher as among the most sung in Wales.

- **Rev. William Rees, D.D.** (died 1883) --- Author of the great Welsh "love-song," "Love Unfathomed as the Ocean" (*Dyma Gariad Fel y Moroedd*). One of the greatest names of nineteenth-century Wales: preacher, poet, politician, and essayist.

- **Rev. Thomas Jones** of Denbigh (1756-1820) --- Calvinistic Methodist preacher, wrote children's and youth hymns including "Early to Bear the Yoke Excels."

## The 1859 Revival

When the wave of spiritual power from the great American revival of 1857-8 reached England, its first messenger to Wales was Rev. H.R. Jones, a Wesleyan, who with Rev. David Morgan, a Presbyterian, led singing bands across the country. The result was described as "lighting a prairie fire." Eighty thousand souls were gathered into the church. The revival of 1859 gave fresh voice to hymns that had been sung for generations, and added some new ones.

## The 1904-5 Revival

The most extraordinary chapter of Welsh hymnody belongs to the revival of 1904-5, led by the young mine-worker Evan Roberts of Loughor, who had prayed thirteen years for a mighty outpouring of the Spirit. When it came, ten percent of the entire population of Wales entered the membership of the church of Christ in one breathtaking harvest season.

This was a praying and singing revival above all. Thousands gathered in churches and chapels, singing without hymnbooks --- for the Welsh had memorized everything. The music was old: "Aberystwyth" and "Tanymarian" by Joseph Parry and Stephens; E.M. Price's "St. Garmon"; R.M. Pritchard's "Hyfrydol"; and the ancient minors of the mountains. Lowell Mason's "Meribah" was sung there, and Edson's "Lenox" --- American tunes transplanted to Welsh soil. But the most cherished hymns were those of [[William_Williams_of_Pantycelyn]] and [[Ann_Griffiths]], of Charles Wesley, and Isaac Watts.

Butterworth-Brown describe the sound of those assemblies: "the most cherished and oftenest chosen hymns were those of William Williams and Ann Griffiths... and the tongues of fire that appeared at Jerusalem took on the Cymric speech, and sang the burning lyrics of the poet-saints."

## Music and Tunes

Among the most characteristic Welsh tunes:

- **"Hyfrydol"** --- by R.M. Pritchard, used for many hymns
- **"Aberystwyth"** --- by Joseph Parry, with its haunting minor quality
- **"Bryn Calfaria"** (Hill of Calvary) --- one of the great Welsh minors
- **"Cwyfan," "Pembroke," "Arabia," "Brymgfryd"** --- old Welsh country airs adapted for hymnody by the revival makers
- **"Ebenezer" ("Ton Y Botel")** --- a contemporary minor first sung at Bethany Chapel, Cardiff, January 8, 1905

Old tunes from England and America also found a home in Wales: Mason's "Ernan," "Lowell," and "Shawmut"; Woodbury's "Nearer Home"; George Hews's "Holley"; and even Daniel Read's old "Windham." The Welsh adopted what moved them and made it their own.

## Legacy

Wales remains a singing nation. The tradition of choral worship, of hymn-singing as the natural expression of the Celtic soul's deepest joys and griefs, did not end with the nineteenth century. The great revivals planted something that grew into the bones of a culture.

Butterworth-Brown conclude: "A temporary excitement" is the verdict of skepticism on a revival wave. "But such excitements are the May-blossom seasons of God's eternal husbandry... If the blossoms had not come and gone there would be no fruit."
