# Gospel Songs

The Gospel Song is one of those phenomena in church history that defies easy evaluation. Serious hymnologists have dismissed it. Millions of ordinary Christians have been brought to faith through it. Both things are true.

The tradition arose in America in the mid-nineteenth century from the confluence of revivalism, Methodism, and the distinctive democratic spirit of American Christianity. It produced the most widely sung sacred songs in the English-speaking world.

## The Character of the Gospel Song

A Gospel song is not, strictly speaking, a hymn in the classical sense. The great hymns of Watts and Wesley are addressed primarily to God --- they are acts of worship, praise, confession, petition. The Gospel song is more characteristically addressed to the hearer: it is testimony, invitation, encouragement.

The distinction is captured in its structure. The Gospel song typically has a verse that narrates spiritual experience and a chorus or refrain that drives home its central appeal:

> Safe in the arms of Jesus,
>   Safe on His gentle breast,
> There by His love o'ershaded,
>   Sweetly my soul shall rest.

The language is simple, the images concrete, the emotional appeal direct. These are not qualities that earn praise from hymnologists, but they are qualities that win souls in tent meetings and evangelistic campaigns.

## Origins and Development

The roots of the Gospel song reach into early American revivalism --- the camp meetings of the early nineteenth century, with their "spiritual songs," their call-and-response structures, and their emotional intensity. But the Gospel song proper emerged from the post-Civil War period, shaped above all by the Moody-Sankey revival campaigns.

Dwight L. Moody was arguably the greatest mass evangelist America produced in the nineteenth century. Ira D. Sankey was his song-leader and composer of Gospel hymns. Together they popularized a form of evangelistic worship in which simple, emotionally compelling songs prepared the crowd for the gospel message. Their campaigns in England as well as America established the Gospel song as the dominant form of popular sacred music in the evangelical world.

[[Fanny_Crosby]] (1823-1915) was the poet laureate of this movement. Blind from infancy, writing at astonishing speed, she produced approximately 8,000 songs, many of them for the Biglow & Main publishing house that supplied the Moody-Sankey campaigns. "Safe in the arms of Jesus," "Blessed assurance," "Pass me not, O gentle Saviour," "Rescue the perishing" --- these are only the most famous among hundreds that were sung by multitudes.

## The Criticism

The criticism of the Gospel song from classical hymnologists has been consistent and not entirely wrong. John Julian, the English hymnologist, said of Fanny Crosby's hymns that they were "with few exceptions very weak and poor." Ryden himself suggests that the predominance of Gospel songs and translations in the late nineteenth century was "indicative of a period of decadence in sacred song."

The criticisms are:

- **Poetically thin**: Gospel songs generally lack the imagery, theological depth, and formal skill of the great classical hymns
- **Subjective**: they tend to focus on the believer's experience rather than on God's attributes or acts
- **Theologically narrow**: the great range of Christian teaching --- creation, covenant, judgment, eschatology --- is largely absent; the Gospel song's theology tends to be limited to salvation, comfort, and heaven
- **Emotionally manipulative**: the refrain structure and the sentimental imagery can produce feelings without genuine spiritual depth

## The Defense

The defense of the Gospel song is equally clear:

- **D.L. Moody** said of "Just as I am" that it had probably brought more souls to Christ than any other hymn ever written. Similar testimonies have been given of dozens of Fanny Crosby's songs.
- The Gospel song's simplicity is not always a defect: it makes it accessible to people who could not follow the complex theology of Watts.
- The best Gospel songs --- Crosby's "Blessed assurance," Philip Bliss's "Man of Sorrows," and others --- are not theologically thin; they are focused.
- The personal, testimonial quality of the Gospel song answers a genuine need. The Psalms themselves are full of personal testimony. The Gospel song stands in that tradition.

## Legacy

The Gospel song tradition did not remain confined to the nineteenth century. The twentieth century saw it develop into the gospel music of the Black church tradition, the contemporary Christian music industry, and the worship song movement. In terms of sheer quantity of what Christians actually sing on any given Sunday, the Gospel song and its descendants dominate the landscape.

The classical hymnody of Watts and Wesley remains the touchstone against which all popular sacred song is measured. The Gospel song remains the medium through which most evangelical Christians encounter sacred music for the first time. The Church needs both.

## Historical Origins (Benson)

Benson's *The English Hymn* (1915) establishes the Gospel Song's lineage more precisely than most subsequent accounts. Far from being a sudden creation of Moody and Sankey in 1875, the tradition had been building for a quarter-century before their great campaigns.

**The Pre-History: Sunday School Songs and YMCA Work.** The Sunday school song tradition — Root, Bradbury, Hull, Waters, Vail, Lowry, Fischer — had from the late 1840s been developing the musical and poetic characteristics of what would become the Gospel Hymn: the simple refrain, the emotional appeal, the popular melodic style. These books "prepared the way"; the Sunday school work and YMCA work, especially during the Civil War, created a vast constituency of people accustomed to this lighter style of religious song.

**Philip Phillips and the Service of Song.** Before Moody and Sankey there was Philip Phillips, the "singing pilgrim," who pioneered the "sacred solo into religious meetings, as defined worship" and whose American campaigns in Britain directly preceded the Moody-Sankey era. Phillips regarded himself as the precedent that Moody and Sankey followed.

**P. P. Bliss's Independent Contribution.** Philip Paul Bliss, working with D. W. Whittle, published his *Gospel Songs* in 1874 — before the Moody-Sankey collaboration fully developed. Bliss's fifty-two original tunes in that collection provided the musical core of what became the joint *Gospel Hymns and Sacred Songs* series.

**The Moody-Sankey Campaigns' Real Achievement.** What Moody and Sankey accomplished was not the invention of the Gospel Hymn but its first great popular success. The combination of Moody's preaching and Sankey's magnetic singing in the Brooklyn Rink and Philadelphia meetings created conditions under which songs that had been known to revival circles became known to the whole English-speaking world. They "brought an older movement to the culmination of a great popular success rather than inaugurated a movement that was novel."

**The Decline and the Reaction.** The deterioration of the movement after its peak — the multiplication of imitators, the commercialization of gospel-book publishing, the descent to what Benson calls "the rag-time level" — was as swift as the rise had been spectacular. By the late 1890s, Sankey and his collaborators were themselves publishing collections that "restored the standard hymns to their rightful precedence" over their own earlier work.
