# Hymn Study

[[Edmund_S_Lorenz]] argues in *[[The_Singing_Church]]* that the hymnbook ranks alongside the Bible and the study of human nature as one of the three essential books a minister must know. The systematic study of hymns enriches the minister personally, equips the congregation spiritually, and transforms worship from routine to living encounter.

## Why Study Hymns

Lorenz identifies three personal benefits for the minister who undertakes serious hymn study.

**Literary pleasure and culture.** The great hymns offer genuine literary beauty --- careful structure, vivid imagery, and elevated thought. Studying hymns by writers such as [[Isaac_Watts]], [[Reginald_Heber]], and [[Charles_Wesley]] sharpens a minister's sensitivity to language, strengthening both his preaching and his appreciation of beauty in worship.

**Emotional and spiritual development.** Hymns bring the student into contact with saints who reached the highest peaks of spiritual insight. A minister whose own devotional life feels dry can find renewal through prayerful engagement with the great hymns. Lorenz insists that hymns must be approached with emotion, not as specimens for cold dissection --- the minister who prays over his hymns will vitalize them for himself and, through that renewed vision, for his congregation.

**Practical discernment.** Each hymn carries a distinct spiritual effect --- didactic, meditative, experiential, exhortatory, or worshipful. Recognizing these differences is as essential as a physician knowing the properties of different medicines. To announce a hymn of solemn crucifixion meditation at a social gathering, Lorenz warns, is a form of spiritual malpractice.

## Methods of Hymn Analysis

Lorenz outlines a disciplined approach to studying individual hymns.

1. **Structural analysis** --- Examine the stanza form, meter, rhyme scheme, and logical progression of thought across verses. Identify which verses can be omitted without destroying the hymn's unity, and note the emotional climax, which typically falls in the final stanza.

2. **Phrase-by-phrase study** --- Locate Scriptural allusions and unpack compressed images. Lorenz gives the example of Watts's line about faith laying "her hand on that dear head," which is unintelligible without knowledge of the scapegoat ritual in **Leviticus 16:21**. Similarly, [[Philip_Doddridge]]'s references to "cloud of witnesses" and "prize" and "race" draw on **Hebrews 12:1**.

3. **Background investigation** --- The personality of the author, the circumstances of composition, and the historical period all shape interpretation. [[Charles_Wesley]]'s warm, experiential hymns reflect the Wesleyan Revival's emphasis on personal conversion, while Watts's more objective hymns mirror the formal worship of his era.

4. **Associational depth** --- Great hymns accumulate meaning across generations. Congregations bring memories of childhood, of beloved mentors, and of past spiritual awakenings to familiar texts, giving old hymns a power that no newly written verse can immediately match.

## Building a Personal Hymnal

Lorenz recommends that every minister create an annotated inner hymnal of roughly one hundred core hymns, marked up in a wide-margined copy of the church hymnal. This personal reference should include structural divisions, obscure allusions needing explanation, phrases packed with meaning that deserve unpacking, Scripture cross-references, notes on tunes, performance directions (soft, loud, retard, accelerate), and dates of previous use to prevent over-repetition.

Such a personal hymnal grows over years of pastoral work, drawing on hymnological literature, daily reading, and lived experience. Lorenz specifically recommends works like Robinson's *Annotations upon Popular Hymns*, Duffield's *English Hymns*, and Benson's *Studies of Familiar Hymns* as sources of practical material.

## Memorization

Lorenz urges ministers to commit at least one hundred hymns to memory. A memorized repertoire enriches devotional vocabulary, supplies apt quotations in preaching and pastoral care, and allows spontaneous use of hymn verses in worship when emotional moments arise --- without the loss of momentum that comes from pausing to locate a page number.
