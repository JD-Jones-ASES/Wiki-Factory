# Gospel Hymn Movement

The Gospel Hymn Movement was not, in Benson's analysis, the sudden creation of Moody and Sankey that its popularizers sometimes claimed. It was the culmination of a long development, and understanding that development is essential for understanding both the movement's genuine achievements and its genuine limitations.

## Roots and Predecessors

The Gospel Hymn as a distinct musical type emerged from the confluence of several streams that had been flowing in American religious music for decades before the 1875 Moody-Sankey campaigns.

**The Sunday School song tradition**: Beginning in the late 1840s, a long series of Sunday school song books by George F. Root, William B. Bradbury, Asa Hull, Horace Waters, Silas J. Vail, Robert Lowry, and William G. Fischer had created in American Protestant children a taste for the lighter type of religious song. These books furnished the evangelists with the earliest examples of what would become Gospel Hymns. Among them were Bradbury's settings of "Sweet hour of prayer" and "He leadeth me"; Fischer's "I love to tell the story"; and Lowry's "Shall we gather at the river."

**The Young Men's Christian Association**: The YMCA, organized in London in 1844 and in Boston and Montreal in 1851, developed an interdenominational evangelistic culture in which "familiar church hymns rendered by male voices" created a new atmosphere of popular religious singing. The 1858 urban revival, centered on the "Union Prayer Meeting," developed spontaneity and brevity in hymn use. YMCA work in the Civil War — distributing hymn books to soldiers in field and hospital — created a vast constituency of men who associated simple religious songs with intense emotional experiences.

**Philip Phillips**: The "singing pilgrim" Philip Phillips pioneered the "sacred solo" as a distinct form of religious entertainment and evangelism, associating himself with Moody's Chicago work and traveling "around the globe" presenting his "Services of Song." Phillips regarded himself as the pioneer who established the precedent for the "gospel partnership of Moody and Sankey."

**P. P. Bliss**: Philip Paul Bliss worked in connection with D. W. Whittle, preparing for their use a small collection of *Gospel Songs* (Cincinnati, 1874) that contained fifty-two tunes of his own composition. The hymns were striking and sometimes dramatic; the tunes, while not wholly original, were vivacious and immediately popular.

## The Moody-Sankey Campaigns

Dwight L. Moody met Ira D. Sankey at the Indianapolis YMCA convention of 1870. In 1872 they started together for their first evangelistic campaign in Great Britain. The encounter with British audiences was significant: Sankey's American melodeon, his solo singing style, and his collection of gospel songs were genuinely novel in many British contexts, especially in Scotland and Ireland, where Presbyterian resistance to instrumental music and the solo format meant that Sankey's methods were more than culturally foreign.

The publishing history is telling. Copies of Sankey's songs were so often requested that Morgan and Scott in London printed them in a sixteen-page pamphlet, *Sacred Songs and Solos*, sold for sixpence. This was the nucleus of what grew to a volume of 1,200 pieces. The evangelists, in a memorable act of integrity, declined the royalties — £7,000 by the end of their British tour.

At home, Bliss and Sankey collaborated. Their joint collection, *Gospel Hymns and Sacred Songs* (Biglow and Main, 1875), was introduced at Moody's meetings in Brooklyn and Philadelphia and circulated in "immense quantities throughout the country." The book carried "the more emotional and less cultivated element of religious people off its feet" and furnished for a time the familiar songs of "vast numbers hitherto unacquainted with hymns and unused to public worship."

The series continued: *Gospel Hymns No. 2* (1876), *No. 3* (1878), through *No. 6* (1891), then similar collections under other names. Bliss, McGranahan, Stebbins, Towner, and others joined Sankey in meeting the demand for new songs.

## Benson's Analysis of the Appeal

Benson's analysis of why the Gospel Hymns succeeded is one of the most penetrating passages of *The English Hymn*. The appeal came partly from the tunes' ability to reach "the average emotional nature through the senses" — they were "easy" and "catchy" and sentimental, swaying with soft or martial rhythm, climaxing in the irresistible refrain. But the appeal was also inseparable from the persons and occasion: "They were first heard in the sweet tones of a magnetic singer in the intense atmosphere created by Moody's preaching, and first sung in unison with a great throng of deeply moved people."

Moody and Sankey were "plain men employing the arguments and illustrations, the music and verse, that appealed to themselves in the conviction that such preaching and song was best adapted to appeal to their hearers." The simplicity was genuine, the sincerity unmistakable. Something of the spiritual impression the songs made was "reflected from the simple and sincere personalities of the evangelists."

## The Deterioration

Benson is equally frank about the movement's decline. The lack of any educational ideal meant rapid deterioration. "Countless imitators" arose "without the inspiration and sometimes without the unmixed motives of the leaders." Every new evangelist following Moody's methods needed his own Sankey and his own hymn book. The "immense pecuniary success" of the Gospel Hymns series — in which Moody and Sankey took no personal share — created powerful incentives for publishers and writers; "the making of such books soon became a trade."

The standard descended to what Benson calls "the rag-time level." The defense of Gospel Hymns — that they reached people the more cultivated hymnody could not — lost force when the quality dropped so far that even the emotional appeal was diluted by mechanical production.

## Effect on Church Hymnody

The movement's effect on church worship was serious. In the Methodist Episcopal Church it "almost uprooted the established Hymnody." In many other denominations the Gospel Hymns "took possession of the Sunday schools, Christian Endeavor societies, and devotional services," creating a generation that grew up largely without the great hymn tradition. "To many of these the tone of Church Praise seems still to lack the 'go' and vivacity to which they had grown accustomed."

By the late 1890s a reaction had begun. Under the inspiration of Charles Cuthbert Hall, the YMCA — which had helped introduce the Gospel Hymns — published *Praise Songs* (1897) aimed at a more sober standard. In 1898 Sankey, McGranahan, and Stebbins themselves published *Church Hymns and Gospel Songs*, in which they "restored the standard hymns to their rightful precedence."

## Lasting Influence

Benson's conclusion is nuanced. He acknowledges the good the Gospel Hymns genuinely did — "many hearts have been quickened through these hymns" — while insisting that "Wesley's elevated standard of Revival Hymnody is more devout and hence more prudent." The conjunction of "the educational process with revival enthusiasm" that Wesley achieved was "the more complete and satisfying system."

The movement did produce one lasting positive development: the recognition by all denominations of a legitimate "Evangelistic Hymnody" — either as a department of "mission services" in the church hymnal or as an authorized "mission hymnal." Some of the best Gospel Hymns found a permanent place in this recognized category. The *English Hymnal* (Oxford, 1906) included five hymns from the first number of *Gospel Hymns and Sacred Songs*, acknowledging their proven worth.
