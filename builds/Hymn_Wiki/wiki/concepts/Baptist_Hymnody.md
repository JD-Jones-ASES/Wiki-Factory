# Baptist Hymnody

Baptist hymnody has one of the most dramatic origin stories in Protestant church music. The tradition was born not in a moment of inspiration but in a decade-long controversy, fought out in pamphlets and congregational votes, against fierce internal opposition. That it emerged at all is largely due to one man's patient courage.

## The Seventeenth Century: Resistance and Pioneer

The earliest English Baptists divided sharply over congregational singing. The General Baptists — the theologically Arminian wing — tended to oppose "promiscuous singing" (all members of the congregation singing together) on the grounds that it mixed the regenerate and unregenerate in a common spiritual act. Some went further, opposing any form of congregational singing.

The Particular Baptists (Calvinist) were more divided. The theological objection was similar, but there was more sympathy for the Reformation tradition of Psalm-singing, and some congregations did sing metrical psalms.

Into this divided landscape came [[Benjamin_Keach]] (1640-1704), pastor of a Particular Baptist church at Horsley-down, Southwark, London. Beginning around 1673, Keach introduced the singing of a hymn at the Lord's Supper — the most cautious possible beginning, restricted to the sacramental moment. He extended the practice gradually over the following years: hymns on Thanksgiving Days, then a weekly hymn after the sermon, with the provision that dissenters "go freely forth" rather than be compelled to participate.

The controversy this provoked was substantial. Isaac Marlow led a pamphlet campaign against Keach's practice; Keach responded in kind. The dispute spread through the Particular Baptist community. By 1691, when Keach published *The Breach Repaired in God's Worship*, his full theological defense of hymn-singing, his congregation had voted to accept the practice permanently — though with a dissenting minority. Benson regards Keach's success in this controversy as decisive for the entire future of Baptist hymnody.

## The Eighteenth Century: Watts and the Baptist Hymnists

Once the principle was established, Baptist hymnody developed rapidly. The Particular Baptists proved remarkably fertile ground for hymn writers, and by the later eighteenth century had produced some of the greatest hymn writers in the English tradition.

[[John_Rippon]] (1751-1836) compiled what became the standard Baptist hymnal: his *Selection of Hymns from the Best Authors* (1787), which was reprinted hundreds of times and remained in use for generations. The 1792 American reprint made it the common hymnal for Baptists on both sides of the Atlantic.

The Baptist hymn writers who contributed to this tradition in the eighteenth century included [[Samuel_Stennett]] ("Majestic sweetness sits enthroned," "On Jordan's stormy banks"), [[Samuel_Medley]] ("I know that my Redeemer lives"), and Anne Steele (1716-1778) — one of the earliest and most influential women hymn writers in the English tradition. Steele's hymns, particularly her hymns of suffering and trust, achieved wide use across denominations.

## The Psalmody Controversy in Baptist Life

English Baptists did not entirely escape the broader denominational controversy over psalms versus hymns. While the Particular Baptists resolved the question in favor of hymns by the early eighteenth century, the General Baptists remained resistant longer, and the tension between the Psalm tradition and the hymn tradition persisted in Baptist life in various forms throughout the eighteenth century.

In America, the Baptist churches were among the earliest to adopt Watts' *Psalms and Hymns*. Their first denominational hymn book (Newport, 1766) was notable for its special provision of Baptismal Hymns — a distinctively Baptist emphasis. The 1790 Philadelphia Association collection and the 1792 American reprint of Rippon's *Selection* established Evangelical hymnody firmly in American Baptist practice.

## American Baptist Hymnody: The Era of Watts and Its Successors

In America, Baptist hymnody in the early nineteenth century was characterized by the dominance of "Watts entire" — the Watts *Psalms and Hymns* as a complete system — supplemented by Rippon's *Selection*. The tension was between educated churches in New England and the Middle States, which favored this solid Calvinist standard, and Western and Southern churches, which preferred "Spiritual Songs" — the more emotional revival-style hymnody.

The *Psalmist* (1843), compiled by Baron Stow and S. F. Smith for the Baptist Publication Society, represented the first successful attempt to provide American Baptists with a modern hymnal that moved beyond the Watts-and-Rippon compromise. It gave the Baptists "precedence over other denominations in leaving behind the 'Psalms and Hymns' era." Its 1,180 hymns — an enormous collection by later standards — reflected the still-prevailing homiletical conception of the hymn book as a minister's resource for sermon illustration.

## The Romantic Movement and Baptist Hymnody

Benson's account identifies a tension in Baptist hymnody during the mid-nineteenth century: while the Romantic Movement and the Unitarian example were raising the literary standard of Congregational and Presbyterian hymnody, many Baptist churches "clung to the homiletical type of Hymnody." The Baptist preference for hymns that directly supported evangelical preaching resisted the move toward the more purely devotional and aesthetic hymns that the literary movement favored.

This tendency was illustrated in the contrast between the Plymouth Collection's enthusiastic adoption by many Baptist churches and the more conservative Baptist collections that continued to favor older evangelical material. The Evangelical type remained strong in Baptist worship well into the later nineteenth century.

## The Gospel Hymn and Its Baptist Impact

The Gospel Hymn movement of the Moody-Sankey era had a particularly significant impact on Baptist churches. Benson notes that the Gospel Hymns "took possession of the Sunday schools, Christian Endeavor societies, and devotional services" of many Baptist churches, creating a generation that grew up without familiarity with the great hymn tradition. The movement toward "restoring the standard hymns to their rightful precedence" in the 1890s was partly a reaction to this displacement.
