# German Rationalism and Hymnody

## The Blight on German Song

In the history of Christian hymnody, the period of German Rationalism (roughly 1740-1820) stands as a cautionary example of what happens when theology abandons its foundations. Ryden calls it a "blight" that cast itself over the hymnody of all Europe but fell most heavily on Germany.

## What Rationalism Was

Rationalism was an attempt to subject all revealed religion to the test and judgment of human reason. What seemed to contradict reason was rejected as superstition. The movement had its roots in the French and English Enlightenment, but it took a particularly destructive form in Germany when it captured the University of Halle --- the very institution that had been the citadel of [[Pietism]].

Christian Wolff and Johann Semler, philosophers at Halle, became its leading voices. Their aim, as they described it, was not to establish a new religion but simply to "purge" Christianity of the elements that seemed unreasonable. But the results were devastating:

- Biblical miracles were dismissed as "fables" incapable of natural explanation
- Christ was stripped of his divinity and regarded only as a moral teacher
- Religion was reduced to "the knowledge of God and the pursuit of virtue"
- Salvation by grace through faith was replaced by the attainability of moral perfection through human effort

What remained of Christianity, Ryden observes, was "a mere shadow: a hypothesis concerning God and immortality, and a teaching of external morality."

## The Effect on Hymnody

The Rationalists were not content to revise theology --- they set out to revise the hymn-books as well. The great hymns of [[Martin_Luther]], [[Johann_Heermann]], [[Nicolaus_Selnecker]], and [[Paul_Gerhardt]] were altered, softened, and "purged" of their evangelical content. The German hymnologist Albert Knapp described the result with biting irony: "The old hymns were subjected to a kind of transmigration of soul by which their spirits, after having lost their own personality, entered into other bodies."

The golden age of German literature produced in this era Goethe, Schiller, Lessing, and Wieland --- but not a single enduring Christian hymn. These geniuses were not filled with evangelical zeal, and they wrote no hymns. The religious verse that was produced was mostly tinged with the "new theology" and had no spiritual life to give it staying power.

## The Few Who Held the Line

Ryden identifies four writers who, despite the Rationalist blight, produced hymns of lasting worth:

**Friedrich Gottlieb Klopstock** (1724-1803) --- The "German Milton," whose epic "Messiah" thrilled Germany. His hymns soared too high for public worship and were too emotional and subjective; only two English translations survive in use. He spent nearly twenty years at the Danish court under royal patronage, returning to Hamburg in 1770 where he died in 1803.

**Christian Fürchtegott Gellert** (1715-1769) --- Born in Hainichen, Saxony, he struggled with an inability to preach from memory and became a professor at Leipzig rather than a pastor. His pupils included Goethe and Lessing. Despite the Rationalist influence of his age, his hymns are notable for their genuine evangelical utterance. He never wrote a hymn, reportedly, except after a season of prayer in the proper frame of mind. His Easter hymn, **"Jesus Lives! Thy Terrors Now"**, has been sung at the funerals of great English churchmen. He died in 1769.

**Matthias Claudius** (1740-1815) --- Perhaps the most interesting figure of the Rationalist period. He intended to prepare for the Lutheran ministry but was led astray by Rationalism at the University of Jena and turned instead to journalism. As editor of a newspaper in Darmstadt he mixed with Goethe and a circle of freethinking philosophers. Then serious illness brought him face to face with the spiritual emptiness of the life he was living, and he returned to his childhood faith. Moving to Wandsbeck, he edited the "Wandsbecker Bote" in a consciously Christian spirit. His most famous hymn, **"We Plow the Fields and Scatter"** (*Wir pflügen und wir streuen*, 1782), became a beloved harvest hymn throughout the Protestant world. His evening ode, "The Silent Moon Is Risen," was written in the spirit and meter of [[Paul_Gerhardt]]'s evening hymn.

**Balthasar Münter** (1735-1793) --- Lutheran court pastor first at Gotha and then at the German Church of St. Peter in Copenhagen. He wrote about 100 hymns, including "Lord, Thou Source of All Perfection" and "Woe unto Him Who Says, There Is No God." Several of his hymns were set to music composed by the greatest musicians of his day.

## The Turn of the Tide

Claudius's story illustrates a broader pattern: by the late eighteenth century, a reaction against Rationalism was building. The philosopher Immanuel Kant helped by demonstrating the limitations of human reason. Friedrich Schleiermacher located the seat of religion not in reason or will but in feeling --- "the feeling of absolute dependence upon God." Claus Harms, in 1817 (the 300th anniversary of the Reformation), published a new set of ninety-five theses calling Germany back to evangelical truth.

The result was the great spiritual renaissance of the early nineteenth century, which produced the hymns of [[Carl_Johann_Philipp_Spitta]] and a new flowering of German evangelical song. The Rationalist interlude had lasted perhaps seventy-five years. The damage it did to the hymn-books would take generations to undo --- but the singing did not stop.
