# Post-Reformation Hymnody

> [[_overview|Home]] > [[_index|Index]] > Concepts

The seventeenth century is one of the most fertile and one of the most agonizing periods in the history of German Lutheranism --- and from that anguish came some of the most beautiful and beloved hymns in the Christian tradition. This is the era that stands between [[Reformation_Hymnody]] and the new dawn of the eighteenth century: post-Reformation, pre-Pietist, shaped above all by the catastrophe of the Thirty Years' War.

## The World These Hymn-Writers Inhabited

The [[Thirty_Years_War_Hymns|Thirty Years' War]] (1618-1648) devastated Central Europe in ways that are difficult to overstate. Entire villages were burned. Populations were cut by a third in some regions. Plague, famine, and marauding armies made ordinary life a sustained encounter with death. The Lutheran church, whose roots went back barely a century, faced what felt like extinction.

In this context, the hymns of the period are not pious decoration. They are survival literature --- theology hammered out in the forge of catastrophe, faith singing in defiance of despair.

## The Great Figures

[[Philipp_Nicolai]] (1556-1608) died before the Thirty Years' War began, but his two great hymns --- "Wake, awake, for night is flying" and "O Morning Star, how fair and bright" --- became the anthems of the Lutheran church throughout that long nightmare. Nicolai wrote them during a plague in Westphalia that killed fourteen hundred of his parishioners; he watched the funeral processions from his window as he wrote.

[[Johann_Heermann]] (1585-1647) lived through the war itself, surviving plague, fire, and the pillaging of his church multiple times. His hymn "O Christ, our true and only light" is a prayer for the unconverted; his passion hymn stands among the finest in the Lutheran treasury.

[[Martin_Rinkart]] (1586-1649) served as the only pastor in the walled city of Eilenburg throughout the war, conducting as many as fifty funerals a day at the worst of the plague. His great hymn "Now thank we all our God" was written, by most accounts, at the table in the midst of this crisis. The hymn that has become a doxology of gratitude for Protestant Christians everywhere was born in one of the darkest places imaginable.

[[Paul_Gerhardt]] (1607-1676) towers above them all. He is, by the common judgment of hymnologists, the greatest German hymn-writer after [[Martin_Luther]] himself. His 120 hymns move from the intimacy of personal devotion to the grandeur of cosmic praise, and they carry a warmth and lyrical grace that marks a new development in Lutheran song. Where the Reformation hymns were often militant proclamations, Gerhardt's are personal conversations with Christ. He bridges the martial spirit of the first generation and the heart-religion of [[Pietism]].

## The Chorale Tradition After Luther

The German Lutheran **chorale** --- the congregational hymn tune that is one of the Reformation's great musical legacies --- continued to develop throughout this period. Johann Sebastian Bach would later harmonize these tunes into masterpieces of choral architecture, but the tunes themselves were already beloved and familiar long before Bach.

[[Johann_Franck]] (1618-1677) contributed both texts and tunes of great beauty, including the communion hymn "Jesus, priceless treasure." [[Johann_Scheffler]] (1624-1677), known as Angelus Silesius, wrote mystic devotional verse of extraordinary lyrical power before his conversion to Catholicism --- some of his hymns remained in Lutheran use regardless.

## The Bridge to Pietism

By the end of the seventeenth century, the character of German hymnody was shifting. The fierce doctrinal controversies of the post-Reformation era were giving way to a new emphasis on personal experience, warm devotion, and heartfelt faith. [[Paul_Gerhardt]] is the hinge figure: rooted in Lutheran orthodoxy, he nevertheless speaks a language of personal intimacy with Christ that would become the hallmark of [[Pietism]].

The movement formally associated with Pietism --- sparked by Philip Jakob Spener's *Pia Desideria* (1675) and developed at Halle by August Francke --- would carry this spirit into the eighteenth century, transforming German hymnody and eventually influencing both the Wesleys' Methodism and the [[Moravian_Hymnody|Moravian movement]] of Count [[Count_Nikolaus_von_Zinzendorf|Zinzendorf]].
