# Gospel Hymn

The Gospel Hymn, as Lorenz defines it in *The Singing Church* (1938), is a form of popular religious lyric characterized by simplicity, emotional directness, and practical fitness for evangelistic and informal worship settings. Lorenz's treatment is notable for its vigorous defense of the genre against its literary and liturgical critics, while maintaining honest standards of discrimination. For the broader historical account of the movement, see [[Gospel_Songs]] and [[Gospel_Hymn_Movement]].

## Lorenz's Defense

Lorenz takes a distinctly practical position. He estimates that four out of five American churches used Gospel hymns to some degree, and argues that critics who dismiss them wholesale show "an utter lack of discrimination." His central argument is that the opposition confuses two separate questions: whether a hymn is literary, and whether it is spiritually effective. The latter, not the former, is the proper criterion.

He identifies several flaws in the critics' approach. First, they typically select the worst specimens of Gospel hymnody for attack while holding up the very best standard hymns as the measure --- an unfair comparison. The standard hymns that survive in modern hymnals are the refined remnant of tens of thousands written over centuries, winnowed through many generations and polished by decades of editorial emendation (see [[Hymn_Emendation]]). Of [[Isaac_Watts]]'s more than seven hundred hymns, only twenty-three appeared in the Presbyterian Hymnal of Lorenz's day. Of [[Charles_Wesley]]'s nearly seven thousand, the Methodist Hymnal used fifty-five. The Gospel hymn tradition had not yet undergone comparable sifting.

Second, Lorenz points out that even the greatest hymn writers produced lines as crude as anything in the Gospel song tradition. Watts published startlingly unpoetic lines; Wesley permitted doggerel; [[William_Cowper]], praised by literary critics as a poetic innovator, wrote stanzas of considerable awkwardness. If the giants of formal hymnody wrote such lines, Lorenz asks, what must the forgotten poetasters of their age have produced?

## An Ancient Feud

Lorenz situates the Gospel hymn controversy within a recurring pattern in church history: the tension between liturgical formalism and popular religious expression. The early church suppressed improvisational song in favor of controlled liturgy. Gregory the Great enforced rigid formalism. The Waldenses, Hussites, and Lollards each developed popular hymnodies that the ecclesiastical establishment resisted. [[Martin_Luther]]'s hymns and Marot's metrical psalms were met with similar opposition, as were the hymns of Watts and the Wesleys in their time. Even in twentieth-century Germany, Lorenz notes, simpler folk-style hymns faced resistance from traditionalists.

## The Proper Place of Gospel Hymns

Lorenz does not argue that Gospel hymns should replace standard hymnody. He insists that the two are complementary, not antagonistic. The wise preacher uses standard hymns for formal worship but finds Gospel hymns indispensable in popular evening services, prayer meetings, evangelistic campaigns, and settings where the audience includes the unconverted or culturally less privileged. A few standard hymns can express the high peaks of spiritual experience; the Gospel songs lead the congregation up to those heights.

He is particularly sharp on the question of worship. Critics who reject Gospel hymns because they are not fit for solemn worship, Lorenz observes, routinely sing didactic, hortatory, and inspirational hymns addressed to human ears in the formal service without objection. The assumption that God accepts only the most literary offerings strikes Lorenz as conceiving of the Divine Being "as a literary connoisseur." What God receives is not the hymn itself but the spiritual results it produces in devotion, love, and consecration.

## Advantages and Limitations

Lorenz identifies the strengths of Gospel hymns as simplicity, emotional accessibility, spontaneity, rhythmic appeal, adaptability to varied situations, directness in personal testimony and exhortation, and the power of the chorus to drive home the central idea. As a tool for evangelistic work, the Gospel hymn "has no substitute, and but one rival --- earnest and spirit-filled preaching."

He is equally candid about the genre's usual faults: aridity, vapidity, and shallowness. The majority of Gospel songs, he concedes, are thin in thought and without lasting musical value. But he insists that every generation has had its ephemeral hymnody, and that this is not a sign of decay but of vitality. When Christians stop writing new songs and are satisfied to repeat only the songs of past ages, "it will prove that the process of ossification has set in."

## Notable Gospel Hymns of Quality

Lorenz names many Gospel hymns that stand comparison with standard hymnody: Ray Palmer's "My faith looks up to Thee," Samuel F. Smith's "The morning light is breaking," Joseph Gilmore's "He leadeth me," Kate Hankey's "I love to tell the story," Annie Hawks's "I need Thee every hour," [[Philip_Paul_Bliss]]'s "Almost persuaded," Horatio Spafford's "It is well with my soul," and John Rankin's "God be with you till we meet again." Many of these were written by college professors, distinguished clergy, editors, and women of education and profound spiritual life --- not the illiterate enthusiasts their critics imagined.
