# Reformation Hymnody

## The Restoration of Congregational Song

Among [[Martin_Luther]]'s many revolutionary acts, one of the most far-reaching was the restoration of congregational singing to Christian worship. For centuries before the Reformation, the congregation had sat in silence while trained clergy sang the Mass in Latin. The music was often magnificent; the people had no part in it.

Luther's declaration of the priesthood of all believers had an inevitable musical corollary: if all believers were priests, all believers should sing. Congregational hymnody was not incidental to the Reformation --- it was one of its clearest expressions.

## The Birth of the Lutheran Hymnal

The story of Reformation hymnody begins in 1523, when the execution of the first Lutheran martyrs in Brussels ignited Luther's own gift for song. In 1524 the "Achtliederbuch" --- the first Protestant hymnal --- appeared with eight hymns: four by Luther, three by [[Paul_Speratus]], and one probably by Justus Jonas. Its impact was explosive.

The same year brought the "Erfurt Enchiridion," with twenty-five hymns. Luther's enemies lamented that "the whole people are singing themselves into his doctrines." By Luther's death (1546), more than sixty hymn collections had been published. Wandering evangelists distributed hymns on leaflets in market squares; whole crowds would join in singing.

## The Character of Reformation Hymns

The hymns of the Reformation reflect their age: militant, earnest, theologically dense, sometimes strident. They are not primarily hymns of personal feeling (that would come later with [[Paul_Gerhardt]] and [[Pietism]]); they are proclamations --- of the Gospel, of God's sovereignty, of the Church's battle against Satan and all enemies of the truth.

Luther himself did not hesitate to name those enemies explicitly. His hymn "Lord, Keep Us Steadfast in Thy Word," written in 1541 during the threat of Turkish invasion, originally prayed for God to "restrain the murderous pope and Turk." The hymn we sing today is considerably softened.

What the early Reformation hymns lack in poetic refinement they more than compensate for with "tremendous earnestness and spiritual exuberance," in Ryden's phrase.

## Key Figures of the Reformation Period

**[[Martin_Luther]]** (1483-1546) --- The founding figure. Author of thirty-six hymns, including "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God." Also the composer of several classic chorale melodies.

**[[Paul_Speratus]]** (1484-1551) --- Luther's chief co-laborer in early hymnody. Wrote "To Us Salvation Now Is Come," called "the poetic counterpart of Luther's preface to Romans."

**Paul Eber** (1511-1569) --- Professor at Wittenberg, "the sweetest singer among the Reformers." His hymns for the dying, including "When in the Hour of Utmost Need," brought comfort to generations.

**Justus Jonas** (1493-1555) --- Luther's bosom friend, who was with the Reformer at his death; wrote "If God Were Not Upon Our Side."

**[[Nicolaus_Decius]]** (d. 1541) --- Former Catholic monk; gave the Reformation its metrical *Gloria in Excelsis* and *Agnus Dei*.

**[[Nicolaus_Selnecker]]** (1532-1592) --- Co-author of the Formula of Concord; wrote "Let Me Be Thine Forever."

## The Reformed Alternative: Psalmody

The Lutheran tradition of free hymnody was not the only Protestant approach to congregational song. Calvin and Zwingli insisted that only the divinely inspired Psalms were proper for public worship. This gave rise to the practice of *psalmody* --- versifying the 150 Psalms in metrical form for singing.

The great Genevan Psalter (1562), produced by Clement Marot, Theodore de Beza, and the composer Louis Bourgeois (who wrote the "Old Hundredth"), became the standard hymn-book of Reformed churches across Europe. For more than 150 years, the Reformed tradition sang Psalms while the Lutheran tradition sang hymns.

It was not until [[Joachim_Neander]] (1650-1680) that the Reformed Church produced its first significant hymn-writer of its own --- and even then, his hymns were quickly adopted by the Lutherans.

## Hymnody of the Controversial Period (Post-Reformation)

The generation after the Reformation brought new trials: not the confrontation with Rome, but bitter internal disputes among Lutherans themselves, particularly over the Lord's Supper and the legacy of Melanchthon.

[[Nicolaus_Selnecker]], Bartholomaeus Ringwalt ("The Day Is Surely Drawing Near"), Martin Behm ("O Jesus, King of Glory"), Valerius Herberger ("Farewell I Gladly Bid Thee"), and Martin Schalling ("O Lord, Devoutly Love I Thee") all lived and wrote during these years of doctrinal strife. Their hymns reflect both the battles of their time and the deep personal faith that sustained them.

## Into the Thirty Years' War

The period of [[Thirty_Years_War_Hymns|the Thirty Years' War]] (1618-1648) produced perhaps the most intense concentration of great hymnody in Lutheran history. [[Johann_Heermann]], [[Martin_Rinkart]], [[Philipp_Nicolai]] (who died before the war but whose hymns became its anthems), and [[Paul_Gerhardt]] all emerged from this crucible.

## The Scope of German Hymnody

By the time [[Paul_Gerhardt]] died in 1676, the tradition of German evangelical hymnody was already vast and varied --- encompassing martial battle hymns, intimate devotional lyrics, hymns of nature, hymns for the dying, communion hymns, Christmas carols, and everything in between. The tradition had proved that the congregation had a voice, and that voice could reach every register of human experience.

The subsequent movements --- [[Pietism]], the Wuerttemberg school, the Age of Rationalism, and the nineteenth-century revival --- all stand on this Reformation foundation.
