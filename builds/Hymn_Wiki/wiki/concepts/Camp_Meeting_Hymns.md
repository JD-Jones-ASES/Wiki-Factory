# Camp Meeting Hymns

The camp meeting hymn is a distinct type of American religious song — not the same as the revival hymn of the Great Awakening, not the same as the Gospel Song of Moody and Sankey — but a form born at a specific moment in a specific place and bearing the marks of that birth throughout its development. Benson's account of this tradition is one of the most illuminating sections of *The English Hymn*, providing the historical grounding for a body of song that many subsequent histories have treated as simply "popular American religious song."

## Origins: The Kentucky Revival (1800)

The camp meeting hymn originated at the Great Revival of 1800 in Logan County, Kentucky. The Revival began in the summer of 1800 under the preaching of James McGready and others, and its most famous expression was the Cane Ridge Meeting of August 1801, which drew an estimated twenty thousand people to a frontier clearing for several days of preaching, prayer, and religious experience of extraordinary intensity.

The conditions of this Revival shaped the music that emerged from it. The songs needed to be singable by enormous outdoor crowds, many of whom were illiterate or musically untrained. They needed to sustain energy through hours of preaching and emotional response. They needed to be learnable immediately by people who had never sung them before.

The result was a characteristic form: a simple verse, often based on familiar evangelical material, to which was added a refrain or chorus that the whole crowd could learn and sing after one or two hearings. The chorus was the innovation — not the verse, which drew freely from Watts, Wesley, and the earlier revival tradition, but the refrain that turned passive listeners into active singers.

## Characteristics of the Camp Meeting Hymn

Benson identifies several defining features of the camp meeting song type:

**The refrain or chorus**: The most distinctive feature, enabling mass participation. Crowds could join in the chorus after hearing it once, even if they did not know the verses. This was the mechanism by which the camp meeting hymn achieved its characteristic effect: the gradual building of communal energy through repetition.

**Individualistic salvation language**: The camp meeting hymn addressed the individual soul with direct urgency. The language of personal conversion, personal assurance, and personal heaven was constant. Benson notes the marked individualism of the salvation focus — "my soul," "my Jesus," "I will" — as a distinguishing characteristic.

**Military and pilgrimage imagery**: Soldiers of Christ, marching to Zion, crossing the Jordan — the imagery was vigorous and physical, suited to people who were literally on the move, living rough lives, facing real danger.

**Folk song form**: The tunes were drawn from folk music, popular song, and whatever was singable. The aesthetic was entirely popular — there was no pretense of literary or musical refinement. This made the songs accessible but also, in the eyes of later critics, spiritually shallow.

## The Cumberland Presbyterian Connection

One of the lasting institutional legacies of the 1800 Kentucky Revival was the birth of the Cumberland Presbyterian Church. The Revival created a vast demand for preaching that the established Presbyterian structures could not meet — they required an educated clergy, and there were not enough educated clergy on the frontier. James McGready and others began ordaining men without the full classical education normally required.

This breach with the established Presbyterian polity led, by 1810, to the organization of the Cumberland Presbytery as a separate body. The Cumberland Presbyterian Church became one of the most vigorous frontier denominations, and its hymnody bore the full marks of the camp meeting tradition from which it sprang.

## Song Books of the Camp Meeting Tradition

As the Revival spread from Kentucky northward and eastward, the need for printed song books created a publishing industry. Benson documents a series of collections:

*A Collection of Spiritual Songs and Hymns* (Philadelphia, 1803) by Richard Allen, first bishop of the African Methodist Episcopal Church, was among the earliest. Allen's collection drew on the camp meeting tradition and served the Black Methodist congregations that the Revival had produced.

*Spiritual Songs for Social Worship* by Hastings and Mason (1832) — a deliberate attempt by educated musicians to provide better alternatives to the camp meeting songs then circulating — represented the educated reaction to the tradition. Mason and Hastings explicitly aimed at offsetting "the evil influences" of the simpler revival collections, but their book succeeded partly because it adopted the camp meeting principle of printing words and music together.

The camp meeting hymnody generated a stream of denominational and interdenominational collections throughout the first half of the nineteenth century, of varying literary and musical quality, but consistent in their commitment to the refrain-chorus form and the direct evangelistic appeal.

## Distinction from Earlier and Later Traditions

Benson is careful to distinguish the camp meeting hymn from adjacent but distinct traditions:

**From the Great Awakening hymns**: The Great Awakening of the 1740s produced hymns of genuine literary quality — Watts' influence was strong, and the Evangelical party's hymnody maintained standards of theological and poetic seriousness. The camp meeting hymn was deliberately simpler, cruder, more immediately accessible.

**From the Wesleyan hymns**: Charles Wesley's hymns, which the Methodist movement carried into America, were theologically rich and poetically accomplished. The camp meeting hymn borrowed the Methodist enthusiasm and emotional directness but shed the theological complexity.

**From the Gospel Hymn**: The Gospel Hymn of the Moody-Sankey era shared the refrain structure and the emotional directness, but was an urban phenomenon — produced by professional composers for organized evangelistic campaigns, often by professional singers performing to city audiences. The camp meeting hymn was a popular and spontaneous creation, born in open air among frontier farmers. Benson treats the Gospel Hymn as the camp meeting hymn's urban successor, not its equivalent.

## Legacy

The camp meeting hymn tradition persisted longest in the rural South and in the Methodist and Baptist frontier communities from which it sprang. Its influence on the shape of American popular religious song was enormous: the refrain-chorus structure, the individualistic salvation focus, and the folk-song musical character that Moody and Sankey popularized were all anticipated in the camp meeting tradition.

Benson's account makes clear that the camp meeting hymn was not a degeneration of something better but a genuine popular response to a genuine spiritual crisis. The frontier produced a form of religious song adequate to the frontier. The question of whether that form should also be the form of settled, educated, urban congregational worship was a different question — one that the later nineteenth century would struggle with at length.
