# Sunday School Hymns

The oldest Sunday-school hymn in existence was not written in the nineteenth century. It comes from the second century, and it is addressed to children. The tradition of singing the faith to young hearts runs as deep as Christianity itself.

## The Ancient Beginning: Clement's Hymn

Titus Flavius Clemens (Clement of Alexandria), whose active life began late in the second century, wrote --- or preserved --- a hymn for children that opens in English as "Shepherd of Tender Youth." The original Greek is addressed to Christ under a profusion of titles: Bridle of untaught foals, Wing of unwandering birds, Helm and Girdle of babes, Shepherd of royal lambs. Twenty-one different titles of Christ appear in the ancient poem. It was found in an appendix to Clement's *Tutor* and is always called "Clement's Hymn."

The English version most commonly used was prepared by Rev. Henry Martyn Dexter, D.D., editor of the *Congregationalist*, and published in 1849. Dexter chose not a translation but a transference of spirit: "Shepherd of tender youth, / Guiding in love and truth / Through devious ways; / Christ, our triumphant King, / We come Thy name to sing." Clement died about A.D. 220. His hymn has been sung in Sunday schools ever since.

## The Scottish Tradition: Michael Bruce

"How Happy Is the Child Who Hears" was one of the most commonly heard hymns in Sunday schools during the 1830s and 1840s. Its author was Michael Bruce, born in Scotland in 1746, son of a weaver. He obtained a good education, taught school, and studied for the ministry --- but died at twenty-one years, three months, and eight days old.

The tragedy of Bruce's story is that his literary executor, John Logan, appropriated the young poet's manuscripts and published them under his own name. For more than a hundred years Logan wore the credit for Bruce's hymn --- until inquiry and discussion finally exposed what Julian's *Dictionary of Hymnology* calls a "long fraud." The tune "Balerma" (from Palermo) was the old companion of Bruce's verses --- resembling more a Palestrina choral than the bright melodies that came later, but suited to the grave, winning words.

## The Sunday School Movement: Early Nineteenth Century

**James Edmeston** (born London, 1791) was an architect who loved to compose religious verses, writing a new piece every Sunday morning for his family. He accumulated a collection he called *Cottager's Hymns*, and is credited with a hundred Sunday-school hymns, including "Little Travellers Zionward."

**Mrs. Jemima Thompson Luke** of Islington, England, wrote "I Think When I Read That Sweet Story" while riding in a stage-coach home from a visit to a mission school for poor children. The hymn, which voices a child's longing to have been with Jesus when he called the little children to himself, was first sung in a village school at Poundford Park and not published until 1841. So many composers have set it to music that it is one of the most frequently rearranged children's hymns in the tradition.

**Mrs. Dorothy Ann Thrupp** of Paddington Green, London (1799-1847), wrote "Saviour, Like a Shepherd Lead Us," which first appeared in Mrs. Herbert Mayo's collection for infant and juvenile schools in 1838. The tune universally accepted and loved is [[William_Bradbury]]'s, written in 1856.

**Andrew Young**, headmaster of Niddrey Street School, Edinburgh (1807-1899), wrote "There Is a Happy Land Far, Far Away" to a Hindu air or natural folk melody that he had heard --- possibly hummed in a pagan temple within his hearing. He captured the tune and made a Christian song of it that became, as Butterworth-Brown put it, "one of the universal hymns."

**Mrs. Elizabeth Mills** (1805-1839) wrote "We Speak of the Realms of the Blest" only three weeks before her death in London at the age of twenty-four. The hymn, wondering aloud what heaven must truly be like, outlived her by more than three-quarters of a century.

## The Revival Era: Fanny Crosby and the Gospel Collections

The history of modern Sunday-school hymnody is largely identified with the *Gospel Hymns* series. [[Fanny_Crosby]] (Mrs. Van Alstyne, 1820-1915) was the era's most prolific contributor, writing hymns that found their way from Sunday-school classrooms to revival meetings to the streets.

Her "Jesus the Water of Life Will Give" was set by [[William_Bradbury]] in his *Fresh Laurels* (1867). Her "Rescue the Perishing" was set by W.H. Doane and became one of the most useful hymns of the entire Gospel period, sung not only in prayer meetings but in Salvation Army camps, mission schools, temperance rallies, and reform organizations.

**Rev. William Orcutt Cushing** (1823-1902) of Hingham, Massachusetts, wrote "When He Cometh, When He Cometh" for his Sunday school in 1856, inspired by Malachi 3:17. [[George_Frederick_Root]] composed the tune while living in Reading, Massachusetts. The story of the tune crossing the Atlantic on an emigrant ship, with hundreds of European passengers joining in voices they had never heard before, is one of the most charming episodes in American hymn history.

**H.R. Palmer** wrote both words and music of "Yield Not to Temptation" in 1868. Horatio Richmond Palmer (1834-1907) was born in Sherburne, New York, of a musical family, and sang alto in his father's choir at nine. The hymn's fighting spirit --- addressed to children who must "battle for the school" --- became a children's anthem.

**Philip P. Bliss** contributed "I Am So Glad That Our Father in Heaven" --- both words and music. He wrote it after hearing the familiar chorus "O How I Love Jesus" and thinking to himself: "I have sung long enough of my poor love to Christ, and now I will sing of His love for me." The hymn, with its direct declaration that Jesus loves me, was widely sung in Scotland as well as America.

## The Music of the Sunday School

The character of Sunday-school hymnody differed markedly from formal church song. Where the church favored measured, harmonically rich chorals, the Sunday school needed:

- **Simple, memorable melodies** --- short enough for a child to learn after one hearing
- **Chorus features** --- the repeated refrain, democratic in allowing even those who stumble on the verses to join for the refrain
- **Duet structures** --- solo and chorus combinations that model and invite participation
- **Story content** --- hymns that told Bible stories or picturesque scenes from Jesus's life

[[George_Coles_Stebbins]]'s tune for "We Speak of the Realms of the Blest" (in his *Gospel Hymns*) is described as "fluent and easily learned and rendered by rote; and while it captures the ear and compels the voice of the youngest, it expresses both the pathos and the exaltation of the words." This dual quality --- accessible to children, meaningful to adults --- is the hallmark of the best Sunday-school hymns.

**Hubert P. Main** harmonized many of these melodies, including "I Shall Not Be Moved," the tune for "I Think When I Read That Sweet Story," and the tune for "When He Cometh."

## The Gospel Hymns Series

During Dwight L. Moody and [[Ira_Sankey]]'s evangelical missions in England and Scotland (1873 onward), the Sunday-school collections merged with revival collections into what became the *Gospel Hymns* series (six volumes). Songs originally composed for children moved into adult prayer meetings and mass evangelism; songs from the adult revival circuit moved back into Sunday-school classrooms. The traffic flowed both ways.

[[Philip_Paul_Bliss]] and Sankey were the chief musical architects of this fusion. Their tunes were designed to be portable --- equally at home in a Sunday school, a tent meeting, or a city tabernacle.

## Legacy

The Sunday-school hymn tradition produced some of the most enduring pieces in Christian worship. "Saviour, Like a Shepherd Lead Us," "What a Friend We Have in Jesus," "When He Cometh," "Rescue the Perishing," and "Safe in the Arms of Jesus" began their lives in collections designed for children but are now sung by the whole church.

Butterworth-Brown note the significance of this with some warmth: "The multitude could be supplied with technical preaching and technical music for their religious wants, but they would not rise to the bait, whereas nothing so soon kindles their better emotions or so surely appeals to their better nature as even the humblest sympathetic hymn sung to a simple and stirring tune."
