# American Hymnody

America came late to hymnody. The Puritans who settled New England brought with them the Calvinist tradition of Psalm-singing, and for more than a century the Bay Psalm Book (1640) --- the first book printed in America --- dominated colonial worship. It was, as critics were quick to note, crude in its versification. But it was what the settlers had, and they sang it.

The slow development of an original American sacred song was further delayed by the sheer difficulty of life on the frontier and the geographic isolation of congregations. The England of Watts and Wesley might as well have been another world.

## The Beginnings

American hymnody begins properly in the late eighteenth century, when the influence of the English hymn tradition first crossed the Atlantic. [[Isaac_Watts]]' hymns, widely printed and used in American congregations from the 1720s onward, were the primary gateway. The Methodist revival also crossed the Atlantic, bringing [[Charles_Wesley]]'s hymns to American shores and planting them deeply in the religious life of the new nation.

The first notable original American hymn-writer was Timothy Dwight, president of Yale, whose paraphrase "I love Thy Zion, Lord" (c. 1800) opened the tradition. But the great flowering came in the nineteenth century.

## The Pioneer Musicians

The improvement of American church music was itself a mission. **[[Thomas_Hastings]]** (1784-1872) devoted his entire life to raising the standard of sacred song. Starting as a boy who trudged six miles to school on the frontier, he became the dominant figure in American sacred music: more than 1,000 hymn tunes, 50 volumes of church music, and hymn texts numbering over 600. His tunes "Toplady" (for "Rock of Ages") and "Ortonville" endure in every hymnal. Lowell Mason, his contemporary, was the other great architect of American hymnody, composing "Missionary Hymn" for Heber's "From Greenland's icy mountains" and "Olivet" for Palmer's "My faith looks up to Thee."

## The Great Hymns

The nineteenth century produced the hymns that most Americans recognize as their own:

**[[Ray_Palmer]]** (1808-1887), a Congregational minister and descendant of John Alden, wrote "My faith looks up to Thee" at twenty-two in a private note-book, with no thought of publication. It is "the most precious contribution which American genius has yet made to the hymnology of the Christian Church."

**[[Samuel_Francis_Smith]]** (1808-1895), a Baptist minister and master of fifteen languages, wrote both the national hymn "My country, 'tis of thee" and the great missionary hymn "The morning light is breaking" in the same winter of 1832, at twenty-four.

**[[Phillips_Brooks]]** (1835-1893), the great Episcopal preacher of Boston, wrote "O little town of Bethlehem" for a Sunday school Christmas festival in 1868, drawing on memories of standing above the actual Bethlehem on Christmas Eve three years earlier.

**[[John_Greenleaf_Whittier]]** (1807-1892), the Quaker abolitionist poet who said he had never succeeded in composing a hymn, furnished at least seventy-five of them from his longer poems. "Dear Lord and Father of mankind" is the most beloved.

**[[Fanny_Crosby]]** (1823-1915), blinded at six weeks old, wrote an estimated 8,000 hymns from age 41 until her death at 92. "Safe in the arms of Jesus," "Blessed assurance," and "Rescue the perishing" are among the most sung hymns in American history.

## The Missionary Spirit

American hymnody in the nineteenth century was deeply shaped by the missionary movement. The "Haystack Meeting" at Williams College (1806) launched the modern missionary movement in America, and hymns reflected and encouraged that spirit. Smith's "The morning light is breaking," Hastings's "Hail to the brightness of Zion's glad morning," and others like them carried the confident, forward-looking optimism of a young nation that believed the world could be evangelized.

## The Gospel Song

The second half of the nineteenth century saw the rise of the [[Gospel_Songs]] movement --- a more popular, emotional form of sacred song associated with the Moody-Sankey revival campaigns. Fanny Crosby was its poet laureate. The Gospel song was widely criticized by classical hymnologists (Julian said Crosby's hymns were "with few exceptions, very weak and poor") but was equally widely sung and loved. Its influence on American church music has been incalculable.

## Character and Legacy

American hymnody is characterized by:

- **Democratic simplicity**: accessible language, personal address, singable tunes
- **Missionary confidence**: the belief that the whole world can and will be reached with the gospel
- **Emotional directness**: the Gospel Song tradition brought feeling to the center of worship
- **Diversity**: Congregational, Baptist, Episcopal, Quaker, and Methodist voices all shaped the tradition

American hymnody never quite equaled English hymnody for sheer theological and poetic richness, but it surpassed it in popular reach. The great Moody-Sankey campaigns carried American sacred song around the world. Today the "Gospel Hymn" style is arguably the dominant form of congregational song in the English-speaking world.

## Institutional History (Benson)

Benson's *The English Hymn* (1915) provides a comprehensive institutional account of American hymnody that differs from most popular histories in its emphasis on the denominational structures and the slow, contested nature of the transition from Psalmody to Hymnody.

**The Era of Watts in America.** The Great Awakening (1740s) under Whitefield's Calvinistic preaching created the "Era of Watts" in American hymnody — not as an immediate transformation but as a gradual displacement of the Psalmody tradition. Watts' *Psalms and Hymns* became "Watts and Select" (Watts supplemented by other hymns) in most Congregational and many Presbyterian churches. The Baptists, who had struggled with the question of singing altogether, were among the earliest to adopt Watts. The Episcopalians, tied to the Psalter by liturgical conservatism, were among the last.

**Timothy Dwight's Revision.** Timothy Dwight (1752-1817), president of Yale and a grandson of Jonathan Edwards, produced a revision of Watts' Psalms that Americanized the texts and supplemented them with additional hymns. His revision was adopted by Connecticut Congregationalism and became an important step in the Americanization of the hymnodic tradition.

**The Transition: Beecher and the Andover Book.** The decisive transition from "Psalms and Hymns" compromise to modern Hymnody came through two books of 1855-1858. Henry Ward Beecher's Plymouth Collection (1855) established congregational rather than choir-led singing as the American ideal; the Andover professors' Sabbath Hymn Book (1858) applied scholarly standards to the selection and editing of hymns. Together these two books, Benson writes, mark "the transition from the hesitations and limitations of the compromise era... to the free and catholic-hearted use of available resources that characterizes modern Hymnody."

**The Unitarian Literary Standard.** American Unitarian hymnody — through the work of Samuel Longfellow, Samuel Johnson, Oliver Wendell Holmes, and others — set the literary standard that other American denominations measured themselves against. The 1864 Hymns of the Spirit represented the fullest expression of this tradition, though at the cost of moving beyond recognizable Christian content.

**The Oxford Influence.** The Oxford Movement's influence on American hymnody came primarily through the Episcopal Church, which was naturally the American denomination most responsive to Anglican developments. But the influence spread beyond the Episcopal Church through the improved standards of hymnal editing that the Oxford example inspired.

**The Gospel Song Phenomenon.** Benson documents the Gospel Song's overwhelming impact on American church music from the 1870s onward — its near-displacement of the established hymnody in Methodist and many other churches, the deterioration of the movement through commercialization, and the gradual reaction that restored the standard hymns to predominance by the late 1890s.
