# Missionary Hymns

The great missionary hymns of the nineteenth century were, as Butterworth-Brown observe, largely anticipatory — written before the great Missionary Movement rather than during it, but providentially timed to become its voice. They gathered speed as the century's missionary enterprise reached momentum, and together they formed the musical rhetoric of the Protestant world mission.

## The Genre

Missionary hymns combine prophetic scope with evangelistic urgency. They are not, strictly speaking, congregational petition or praise — they are rallying calls, forecasts of global Christendom, meditations on the distance between the light that the singer possesses and the darkness where others still sit. The recurring image is geography: hills, mountains, seas, climes, the "utmost limit of the zone."

Butterworth-Brown describe the finest of them as "silver trumpets among all the rallying bugles of the church."

## Key Hymns and Their Histories

### "Jesus Shall Reign Where'er the Sun" — [[Isaac_Watts]]
One of Watts's sublimest hymns, expanding the prophecy of Psalm 72, this was written decades before the modern missionary movement but served as its most majestic anthem. In 1862, a newly converted native king in the South Sea Islands who had adopted a Christian constitution gathered five thousand of his subjects, and they joined with one voice in singing it.

### "From Greenland's Icy Mountains" — [[Reginald_Heber]]
Written in twenty minutes (three stanzas) on a Saturday afternoon in 1819 at Wrexham Vicarage, at the request of Dean Shipley who needed something for a missionary meeting the following morning. Heber insisted on adding a fourth stanza — "Waft, waft, ye winds, His story" — before he was satisfied. By common consent it became "the silver trumpet among all the rallying bugles of the church."

The tune "Missionary Hymn" was composed by [[Lowell_Mason]] — then a young bank clerk in Savannah, Georgia — in half an hour, after a lady in the congregation sent her son to ask if he could write something to fit the metre. "Like the hymn it voices, it was done at a stroke, and it will last through the ages."

### "The Morning Light Is Breaking" — [[Samuel_Francis_Smith]]
Written in the same remarkable winter of 1832 that produced "America," when Smith was twenty-four. It has been called "not far behind Heber's *chef-d'oeuvre* in lyric merit" and described as the most frequently sung missionary hymn, with versions in Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, French, Chinese, Swedish, and several East Indian dialects. Smith heard it sung by native Christians in Burma and was welcomed with an ovation when they knew who he was. The tune is "Webb" by [[George_James_Webb]].

### "Joy to the World" and "Jesus Shall Reign" — [[Isaac_Watts]]
Both hymns were regularly sung at missionary meetings despite being written for general worship — their prophetic scope made them natural companions to the Great Commission.

### "Hasten, Lord, the Glorious Time" — [[Harriet_Auber]]
Written in the early nineteenth century and included in her *Spirit of the Psalms* (1829), this hymn of anticipation looks forward to the day when "every nation, every clime / Shall the Gospel call obey."

### "On the Mountain Top Appearing" — [[Thomas_Kelly]]
One of the more than 700 hymns of the Dublin-born Nonconformist, used widely in missionary contexts, set to Thomas Hastings's tune "Zion."

### "O'er the Gloomy Hills of Darkness" — William Williams
A Welsh Calvinistic Methodist hymn, probably written about 1750 — decades before the great missionary movement — that anticipated the global reach of the Gospel with prophetic force. Its closing stanza ("Fly abroad, thou mighty Gospel!") remained in many memories even as the hymn dropped out of modern hymnals. Set to Hastings's "Zion."

### "Speed Away! Speed Away!" — [[Fanny_Crosby]]
Written in 1890 at the request of [[Ira_D_Sankey]] to Woodbury's melody. Three hymn-stanzas of farewell to departing missionaries — the key-word adapted from Woodbury's original 1848 song, but the substance Crosby's own.

## The Tune "Missionary Hymn"

[[Lowell_Mason]]'s tune is one of the most celebrated examples of a tune and text arriving together by what was perceived as providential timing. Written in Savannah, Georgia, in 1823 by a young bank clerk at a lady's request, it "doubled Heber's fame and made his own."

## Significance

The missionary hymn as a genre reflects the self-understanding of nineteenth-century Protestant Christianity: that it carried a light the whole world needed, and that the singing church was the driving force behind the missionary enterprise. These hymns were not incidental to the movement — they were, in many cases, the emotional and rhetorical engine that sustained it.
