# 18th Century Hymnody

> [[_overview|Home]] > [[_index|Index]] > Concepts

If one century more than any other established the shape of congregational singing as the English-speaking world knows it, that century is the eighteenth. When it opened, most English churches sang only metrical Psalms in crude translations. When it closed, a vast treasury of original hymns had been composed, the Methodist revival had given those hymns millions of eager singers, and the foundations of American hymnody had been laid. It was, simply, the century that taught the English-speaking church to sing.

## The Watts Revolution

The transformation begins with one man: [[Isaac_Watts]] (1674-1748). The son of a Nonconformist minister, Watts was reportedly so dissatisfied with the quality of congregational song at his home church in Southampton that he was challenged to produce something better. He did. His *Hymns and Spiritual Songs* (1707) was the first serious hymn-book in the English language. His *Psalms of David* (1719) so thoroughly re-read the Hebrew Psalter through New Testament eyes that the Old Testament origin is sometimes forgotten.

Watts understood something essential: that Christian praise needed to speak in the voice of the New Covenant, not merely repeat the vocabulary of ancient Israel. His contemporaries were startled by the boldness of this move. Posterity has simply called it genius.

[[Philip_Doddridge]] (1702-1751), Watts's close friend and fellow Independent, carried the Wattisian spirit into a second generation, adding hymns of warmth and evangelical clarity to the growing treasury.

## The Methodist Flood

The second great force of the century was the Methodist revival. John Wesley's preaching converted tens of thousands, but it was his brother [[Charles_Wesley]] (1707-1788) who gave the revival its singing voice. With more than 6,500 hymns to his name, Charles Wesley is the most prolific hymn-writer in Christian history. He combined the precision of a classical scholar with the fire of an evangelist and the tenderness of a poet.

The [[Methodist_Hymnody|Methodist tradition]] did not merely produce hymns --- it gave them a congregation hungry to sing. Camp meetings, class meetings, and field preaching created occasions for congregational song that the established church had never imagined. The hymn became an instrument of revival, a vehicle for theology, and a bond of community all at once.

## The Great Awakening

Across the Atlantic, the Great Awakening of the 1730s and 1740s produced a parallel explosion of religious intensity. Jonathan Edwards and George Whitefield were its preachers; its musical counterpart in America was the gradual development of a native taste for evangelical song. The full flowering of [[American_Hymnody]] would come in the nineteenth century, but its roots are here.

## The Olney Hymns

No account of eighteenth-century English hymnody is complete without the [[Olney_Hymns]] (1779), produced by the curate [[John_Newton]] and his parishioner [[William_Cowper]] in the village of Olney, Buckinghamshire. Newton was a former slave-ship captain converted under dramatic circumstances; Cowper was a sensitive poet who struggled with severe depression. Together they produced a collection of 349 hymns that captures the full range of Evangelical Anglican spirituality.

"Amazing Grace" (Newton) and "God moves in a mysterious way" (Cowper) are the most famous survivors from this collection, but the whole is a treasury of authentic Christian experience --- triumph and despair, certainty and doubt, gratitude and longing.

[[Augustus_Toplady]] (1740-1778), the Calvinist controversialist who was Wesley's theological opposite, contributed "Rock of Ages" to the century's legacy --- a hymn that would outlive every argument that produced it.

## Continental Influence

The German tradition did not stand still during this century. [[Pietism]] was transforming Lutheran hymnody, the [[Scandinavian_Hymnody|Scandinavian Lutheran churches]] were producing their own golden age, and the [[Moravian_Hymnody|Moravian movement]] under [[Count_Nikolaus_von_Zinzendorf|Count Zinzendorf]] was developing its distinctive warm and Christ-centered devotional song. These traditions would feed into English and American hymnody through translation and contact.

## What the Century Established

By 1800, the eighteenth century had given the English-speaking church:
- A large body of original hymns covering the full range of Christian doctrine and experience
- The precedent that congregational hymn-singing was a normal and desirable part of Protestant worship
- A living Methodist tradition that made singing the heartbeat of devotional life
- The seeds of American hymnody, ready to bloom in the coming century
