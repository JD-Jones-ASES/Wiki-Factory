# Congregational Singing

[[Edmund_S_Lorenz]] devotes the final chapters of *[[The_Singing_Church]]* to the practical art of leading congregational song. His central conviction is that the hymn, properly handled, is the peer of the sermon in spiritual power --- and that most ministers squander this resource through neglect, mechanical habit, and a failure to understand the psychology of corporate worship.

## The Hymn as Means to an End

Lorenz frames every practical consideration around one principle: the hymn exists to serve spiritual ends. Literary beauty and doctrinal precision matter, but the primary measure of a hymn's value is its power to meet spiritual needs and advance the work of the church. Two forces cooperate in every hymn: the musical or nervous stimulus of the tune, and the message or emotional burden of the text. Both must work together for full effect.

## Creating Religious Interest

Lorenz distinguishes sharply between two types of hymn use: expressing religious feeling that already exists, and creating religious interest where none yet exists. This distinction governs the opening of every service.

When a congregation gathers in a secular state of mind --- distracted, socially chattering, mentally scattered --- the minister must not begin with intimate devotional hymns like "My faith looks up to Thee." Such hymns presuppose a spiritual readiness the people do not yet possess; asking them to sing such words amounts to encouraging insincerity.

Instead, the opening should use simpler, more energetic hymns --- "Onward, Christian soldiers," "Come, we that love the Lord," or even a lively [[Gospel_Songs|gospel song]] --- to unify the crowd physically and mentally through common action. Only after this psychological preparation can the congregation be led into deeper, more genuinely worshipful singing.

In a church where sacred architecture and tradition already dispose the congregation toward reverence, the progression can begin at a higher level: a choir introit, the Gloria Patri, and then a solemn hymn of worship. But even here, Lorenz warns, the minister must read his people's actual state of mind, not assume an ideal one.

## Announcing and Introducing Hymns

Lorenz devotes sharp attention to the moment of announcement --- a point where most ministers fail. A hymn number announced in a listless, mechanical voice sets an unconscious emotional keynote of indifference. Twenty seconds of effective introduction, on the other hand, can transform the congregation's engagement.

The minister should never treat the hymn as a mere procedural interruption. Before singing, he should briefly orient the congregation to the hymn's message, its emotional character, or the spiritual purpose it serves in the service. Options include a brief doctrinal comment, a compact anecdote illustrating the hymn's theme, a moment of silent prayer, having the congregation read a verse before singing it, or having a soloist sing a verse first.

These introductions must be concise --- ideally under two minutes, often under thirty seconds. Prolixity defeats the purpose. The goal is to strike the emotional key of the hymn, not to deliver a hymnological lecture.

## The Hymn as Teaching Instrument

Lorenz sees didactic and meditative hymns as opportunities for brief doctrinal instruction. A few minutes of comment before singing [[Charles_Wesley]]'s "Depth of mercy! can there be" can impress the reality of sin's power. "Rock of Ages" opens a door to teaching assurance of salvation. "God moves in a mysterious way" (by [[William_Cowper]]) becomes a lesson in faith amid providence. A score of such hymn discussions spread through the year can clarify Christian doctrine more effectively than abstract sermons on the same themes.

## Hymn Sermons and Hymn Services

The "hymn sermon" replaces proof texts with sung hymn verses at key points in the discourse, weaving congregational song into the fabric of preaching. The "hymn service" strings together eight to ten hymns with brief historical, illustrative, and devotional comment. Both formats require careful planning to maintain momentum and spiritual focus.

## Reading and Performing Hymns Well

Lorenz acknowledges that many congregations dislike having hymns read aloud --- because so few ministers read them with genuine feeling. But when the minister's mind and heart are alive to the hymn's meaning, reading it before singing restores worn-out words to freshness. Familiar hymns, Lorenz observes, are like coins whose inscription has been rubbed smooth by long circulation; intelligent reading re-stamps their meaning.

He also advocates having congregations read verses in unison before singing, starting a hymn with a soloist, varying dynamics (soft, loud, retard, accelerate), and occasionally repeating a particularly powerful stanza.

## Responding to Emergencies

Corporate singing can even serve in crises. During a violent storm, a disruptive intruder, or the threat of panic, a well-known, energetic hymn like "Onward, Christian soldiers" --- started promptly by a leader of presence of mind --- can hold a congregation together. In the case of a sudden death or medical emergency, a softer hymn like "Rock of Ages" or "He Leadeth Me," sung gently, transforms shock into spiritual consolation.

## The Minister's Responsibility

Lorenz's epilogue, grounded in **Ecclesiastes 12:7**, summarizes the entire argument: the hymn has been recognized in every age and by every culture as both the expression and the generator of religious emotion. That emotion --- when genuine, when grounded in clear ideas, and when led by a minister whose own heart is alive --- moves the human will toward God. The minister who neglects hymn singing in favor of exclusive reliance on preaching commits, in Lorenz's words, a form of professional egotism that impoverishes both pulpit and pew.
