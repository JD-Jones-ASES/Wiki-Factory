# Greek and Syriac Hymnody

The first Christian hymns were sung in Greek. The New Testament was written in Greek. The first great scholars and theologians of the Christian church thought and wrote in Greek. And the earliest original Christian hymns --- the ones that moved beyond the Psalms and Scriptural canticles into newly composed sacred poetry --- were Greek too.

Syriac, the Aramaic dialect spoken in Syria and Mesopotamia, was the second great early language of Christian song. It was the language of the church in the regions immediately surrounding the birthplace of Christianity, and it produced one of the most remarkable hymn writers in the history of the church.

## The Greek Tradition

### The Oldest Hymn

The oldest surviving Christian hymn with any attribution is [[Clement_of_Alexandria]]'s "Shepherd of Tender Youth," appended to his work *The Tutor* around 200 AD. It is a children's hymn --- gentle, clear, addressing Christ as shepherd, king, healer, and guiding Word. Ryden calls it "the oldest Christian hymn in existence" (outside of Scripture-derived chants).

From roughly the same period, a beautiful anonymous Greek hymn known as *Phos hilaron* ("O Gladsome Light") has come down to us. Ryden quotes Longfellow's translation:

> O Gladsome Light
> Of the Father immortal,
> And of the celestial
> Sacred and blessed
> Jesus, our Saviour!

It was used as a vesper hymn --- sung as the lamps were lit at the close of the day --- and its serene simplicity has kept it in use in Eastern and Western churches alike for eighteen centuries.

### Gregory Nazianzen

The greatest figure of the Greek hymn tradition in the patristic period is [[Gregory_Nazianzen]] (325-389), whom Ryden calls "the greatest name among the Greek hymnists of this period." Son of a bishop, forcibly ordained against his will at thirty-six, eventually enthroned as Patriarch of Constantinople --- and then, through the machinations of his Arian enemies, compelled to resign and return to his hometown. In retirement, he devoted himself to writing sacred poetry of "singular beauty and lofty spirit."

### Anatolius

[[Anatolius]] is a more shadowy figure --- Ryden notes that "concerning this man very little is known except that he lived in the seventh or eighth century." But he left about a hundred hymns, at least three of which are still in common use:
- "Fierce was the wild billow" --- a meditation on the stilling of the storm, with its famous evocation of "Peace! It is I"
- "The day is past and over"
- "A great and mighty wonder" --- a Christmas hymn of unusual charm

### John of Damascus and Stephen the Sabaite

The great late flowering of Greek hymnody came with [[John_of_Damascus]] (c. 675-749) and his nephew [[Stephen_the_Sabaite]] (c. 725-794), who both lived as monks in the Lavra of St. Sabas near Bethlehem.

John gave the church its two most popular Easter hymns: "The day of resurrection" and "Come, ye faithful, raise the strain." Stephen gave it the quietly searching "Art thou weary, art thou languid?" All three were later translated into English by [[John_Mason_Neale]].

### Joseph the Hymnographer

The last major figure in the Greek tradition is [[Joseph_the_Hymnographer]], who lived in Constantinople in the ninth century. He wrote the hymn for St. Michael's Day that begins:

> Stars of the morning, so gloriously bright,
> Filled with celestial resplendence and light...

After Joseph, Ryden notes, the Eastern church's hymnody became increasingly liturgical and clerical in character, and eventually the great popular tradition of Greek sacred song gave way to more formal liturgical forms.

## The Syriac Tradition

### Bardesanes: Heresy Through Music

The Syriac tradition begins with an unusual figure: a heretic. [[Bardesanes]] was a Gnostic teacher of the second century who discovered that his theology could spread far more effectively through hymns and melodies than through theological argument. He wrote hymns in Syriac, and his son Harmonius continued the work. Only fragments of their hymns have survived.

The significance of Bardesanes is that he demonstrated, before the orthodox church had fully grasped it, the power of congregational song as a vehicle for theological formation. Unfortunately he grasped it first in the service of error.

### Ephrem the Syrian: The Cithern of the Holy Spirit

The orthodox response came in the fourth century through [[Ephrem_the_Syrian]] (c. 307-373), who is called "the cithern of the Holy Spirit" --- an image that captures both the beauty and the devotion of his life's work.

Ephrem had been present at the Council of Nicaea in 325 AD, where the full divinity of Christ was formally affirmed against the Arians. That experience seems to have made him a lifelong champion of orthodox Christianity. He fought theological battles not only with argument but with song, training large choirs to sing his own hymns against the Arian and Gnostic teaching that threatened the Syrian church.

Ryden calls Ephrem "the greatest teacher of his time in the Syrian Church, as well as her most gifted hymnist." The tradition he created has remarkable staying power: his hymns are still used by Maronite Christians in Lebanon today.

## The Character of Eastern Hymnody

The hymns of the Eastern church, Ryden notes, are "rich in adoration and the spirit of worship." They have an exalted, otherworldly quality that reflects the Eastern Christian tradition's deep sense of the holiness and transcendence of God. "Because of their exalted character and Scriptural language they have found an imperishable place in the liturgical forms of the Christian Church. As types of true hymnody, they have never been surpassed."

The gradual decline of congregational singing in the Eastern church, following the Council of Laodicea's decree (c. 363 AD) restricting public singing to trained singers, gave Eastern hymnody an increasingly clerical and liturgical character. Unlike the Western tradition, which would eventually rediscover congregational song through the Reformation, the Eastern church's hymnody remained primarily the domain of trained choirs and monks.

## Recovery Through Translation

The Greek and Syriac hymns were effectively lost to Protestant Christianity until the nineteenth century, when [[John_Mason_Neale]] began his extraordinary work of translation. From 1851 onward, Neale rendered scores of Greek and Latin hymns into English, making them available to congregations that had never had access to them. His translations of John of Damascus's Easter hymns, Stephen the Sabaite's "Art thou weary," Anatolius's "Fierce was the wild billow," and Clement's "Shepherd of Tender Youth" (rendered as such) are among the great gifts of Victorian scholarship to the living church.
