# Scandinavian Hymnody

The Lutheran Church took root in Scandinavia in the sixteenth century, bringing with it the great tradition of German sacred song. For more than a century, Scandinavian Christians worshiped primarily with German and translated German hymns. Then, one after another, three great Danish poets arose to give their people a native voice --- and in Sweden, a single architect of genius shaped a hymn-book that would last more than a century.

## The German Foundation

The Reformation came to Denmark in 1536, to Sweden and Norway shortly thereafter. Luther's own hymns and those of the great German tradition became the inheritance of the Scandinavian churches. The Lutheran Psalm-books of the sixteenth century drew heavily from German sources.

But the genius of the Scandinavian peoples required its own expression. The argument was made by Denmark's first great hymnist, [[Thomas_Kingo]], writing to King Christian V: *"The soul of the Danes is not so bound and impoverished but that it can soar as high toward heaven as that of other peoples, even if it be not upborne by strange and foreign wings."*

## Denmark: Three Great Hymnists

Danish hymnology recognizes a celebrated triumvirate, each the dominant voice of a successive century:

### Thomas Kingo (1634-1703) — The Poet of Easter-Tide

[[Thomas_Kingo]] was the son of a Scottish immigrant tapestry-weaver. He gave Denmark its own singing voice through his *Spiritual Songs* (1673, 1681) and eventually shaped the official Church hymn-book, which contained 85 of his hymns. His characteristic symbol was the sun: he was "in love with the sun" and regarded light as the "true element." His Easter hymns and morning hymns are his greatest legacy.

### Hans Adolph Brorson (1694-1764) — The Poet of Christmas

[[Hans_Adolph_Brorson]] was shaped by the Pietist movement from Halle. He gave Denmark its warmest and most tender hymns, many of them written for Christmas. His characteristic symbol was the rose: his people learn to know Christ as the Friend in the prayer chamber, the Rose that spreads its quiet fragrance. His *Swan-Song* collection (1765), published posthumously, contains "Behold a host, arrayed in white" --- perhaps the greatest glory-hymn in Nordic Christianity.

### N.F.S. Grundtvig (1783-1872) — The Poet of Whitsuntide

[[N_F_S_Grundtvig]] was the last and greatest of the three. He fought with extraordinary passion against the rationalism that had dried up the streams of spirituality in Denmark, and his hymns are primarily hymns of the Holy Spirit. His characteristic symbol was the bird. "Built on the Rock, the Church doth stand" is his most famous hymn. He lived to eighty-nine, was made bishop at seventy-eight, and preached the day before his death.

The Danish hymnologist Brandt's comparison of the three is memorable: Kingo's sun, Brorson's rose, and Grundtvig's bird --- three symbols for Christ the risen Saviour, the intimate Friend, and the living Spirit. Together they represent a full Trinitarian theology in image.

## Sweden: Johan Olof Wallin and the Psalm-Book

While Danish hymnody had three great voices, Swedish hymnody had one man who shaped an entire national tradition: [[Johan_Olof_Wallin]] (1779-1839).

Of the 500 hymns in the Swedish Psalm-book of 1819 --- the hymn-book of the Swedish church for more than a century --- 342 reflect Wallin's genius in some form: 128 originals, 178 revisions, 23 translations, 13 semi-originals. This is an achievement without parallel in hymnody.

Wallin rose from poverty to become Archbishop of Uppsala, the Primate of the Church of Sweden. Longfellow called him "David's harp in the Northland." He held an uncompromising standard for both spiritual content and literary excellence, and the Swedish language was shaped by the century of singing his Psalm-book.

## Norway

Norwegian hymnody developed later and is represented in Ryden's account primarily by Magnus Brorstrup Landstad (1802-1880), who compiled the great Norwegian hymnal of 1861 and wrote important original hymns. Bernhardt Severin Ingemann, though Danish, contributed "Through the night of doubt and sorrow," which became beloved in Norway as well.

## Connections to the Wider Tradition

Scandinavian hymnody did not develop in isolation. Pietism, which shaped Brorson so deeply, came from Germany. The German Lutheran tradition of Bach-era chorales provided tunes for many Scandinavian hymns. [[Sabine_Baring_Gould]] translated Ingemann's "Through the night of doubt and sorrow" into English, linking the Nordic and English traditions. The great Scandinavian immigrant communities of the American Midwest carried their hymn-books to a new world.

## Character

Scandinavian hymnody as a whole is characterized by:

- **Deep Lutheran rootedness**: the theology of grace, the centrality of Christ's atoning work, the sacraments
- **Seasonal richness**: the great festal hymns of Christmas, Easter, and Pentecost
- **Poetic range**: from Kingo's sun-drenched morning hymns to Brorson's quiet intimacy to Grundtvig's Spirit-fire
- **National character**: the hymns are often deeply rooted in the landscapes and cultures of the North, which makes them resistant to translation but irreplaceable for the people who sing them
