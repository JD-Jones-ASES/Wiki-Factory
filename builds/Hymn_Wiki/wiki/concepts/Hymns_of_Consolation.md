# Hymns of Consolation

Among all the offices that sacred song performs, none is more intimate or more necessary than the song that meets the soul in grief. The hymns of consolation --- those that speak to the dying, to the bereaved, to the weary pilgrim near the end of the road --- represent some of the most enduring poetry in all of Christian literature.

## The Great Latin Sources

**"Jerusalem the Golden"** (*Urbs Sion Aurea*) comes from the three-thousand-line poem "De Contemptu Mundi" by Bernard of Cluny (c. 1100), a monk at the great monastery of Cluny in France. After a long, dark vision of the world's wickedness, his poem turns and breaks into sunrise with a vision of the Golden City. Dr. John Mason Neale, translating while "inhibited" from his priestly functions and so poor he was writing children's stories to earn his living, gave the English-speaking world what Butterworth-Brown call an "unsurpassable" condensation. Neale's poverty, they note, added to the wealth of Christendom. The tune "Ewing," composed by Alexander Ewing (born Aberdeen, 1830; died 1895), an army paymaster, is "a stately harmony in D major with sonorous and impressive chords."

## The English Tradition

**Isaac Watts** wrote what may be "the sweetest hymn he ever wrote" --- "Why Should We Start and Fear to Die?" --- though it has been crowded out of many modern hymnbooks despite its extraordinary hopefulness. The last two stanzas envision the soul stretching her wings in haste and flying "fearless through death's iron gate, / Nor feel the terrors as she passed," then breathing her life out sweetly on Christ's breast.

Watts's "Lo! What a Glorious Sight Appears" (on the New Jerusalem from Revelation 21) has been "dear for two hundred years to every Christian soul throbbing with millennial thoughts." Its last stanza, crying out "How long, dear Saviour, O how long?" has been the household prayer of myriads of burdened saints.

**James Montgomery**'s "Forever with the Lord" was written in his old age and grew to twenty-two quatrains --- he could not quit the theme. The image of pitching a moving tent "a day's march nearer home" has become a permanent phrase in Christian devotional language. His elegy "The Grave" --- "There is a calm for those who weep" --- is pure poetry of peace, its stanzas like an evening garden.

**Charles Wesley** wrote "Shrinking from the Cold Hand of Death," whose third stanza John Wesley used almost as a personal creed in his old age, concluding every admonition to his societies with a prayer that he might not live to be useless, but "cease at once to work and live."

## The American Contributions

**Phebe Cary** (born near Cincinnati, 1824; died Newport, 1871), the younger of the famous Cary sisters, wrote "One Sweetly Solemn Thought" in 1852 during a visit to a friend's house, in a "little back third-story bedroom, one Sunday after coming from church." She set little value on it as a poem, but when compilers saw it, its intrinsic worth was recognized. The phrase "I am nearer home to-day / Than I ever have been before" became one of the beloved sentences of consolation hymnody.

The Philip Phillips air "Nearer Home," which carries Phebe Cary's verses, once stopped a card game in Macao, China, when an older American recognized the melody his companion was thoughtlessly humming and threw down the cards in tears of memory and resolve.

**Mrs. Ellen M.H. Gates** wrote "I Will Sing You a Song of That Beautiful Land" at the request of Philip Phillips in 1865, based on the passage in *Pilgrim's Progress* where Christian and Hopeful enter heaven. Phillips composed the melody with his little boy on his knee, reading Bunyan's passage, then turning to the organ. The "Home of the Soul," as the hymn is called, has been printed in seven languages and sung, Phillips wrote, at over one hundred and twenty funerals by a single song-leader.

**Horatio Gates Spafford** (born New York state, 1828), a lawyer who lost property in the Chicago fire and then his children in a ship disaster on the Atlantic (November 22, 1873), wrote "It Is Well with My Soul" at the end of that year of catastrophe, in a "gentle, gliding melody" by [[Philip_Paul_Bliss]]. "If he can feel like that after suffering what he has suffered," said a friend who read the hymn, "I will cease my complaints."

## The Scottish Tradition

**Margaret Mackay** (1801-1887) wrote "Asleep in Jesus, Blessed Sleep" --- carried to millions of ears by [[William_Bradbury]]'s tune "Rest." The hymn has been one of the most consistently used funeral pieces in English Protestant worship.

**Horatius Bonar**'s "Beyond the Smiling and the Weeping" is one of his most unusual and haunting hymns --- strangely rune-like in its rhythms, with a refrain of "Love, rest and home!" that interrupts each stanza's enumeration of earthly contrasts. Bradbury wrote a tune for it; Stebbins also set it to music.

## Poems on Death's Threshold

**Alfred Lord Tennyson**'s "Crossing the Bar" ("Sunset and Evening Star") was his swan-song, written three years before his death in 1892 and expressing the faith of a man who had long known the Divine Presence --- "God is with us now, on this down, just as truly as Jesus was with his two disciples on the way to Emmaus," he once said to a niece. Tennyson's death was serene, his prayer answered. Joseph Barnby's tune "Crossing the Bar" gives it a noble choral setting.

**Fanny Crosby**'s "Safe in the Arms of Jesus" (1868), with W.H. Doane's tune, occupies a different register entirely from Tennyson's --- simpler, more direct, feminine and maternal in its imagery. Yet Butterworth-Brown hold both in the same esteem, writing that between Tennyson singing of his Pilot and Crosby singing "Safe in the Arms of Jesus," "is only the width of the choir. The organ tone and the flute-note breathe the same song."

**Henry Kirke White** (born Nottingham, 1795; died 1806) died at barely twenty years old after overwork at Cambridge. His sailor hymn "When Marshalled on the Nightly Plain" tells the story of his own conversion --- a soul once sceptical, lost in the storm, guided to peace by the Star of Bethlehem. The figure of a ship in a storm finding harbor has made it appropriate for both sailor's hymnody and consolation services.

## Funeral Practice

By the early 1900s, the use of hymns at funerals had changed considerably in America. The old "China" with its terrifying chords was gone; Dr. Muhlenberg's "I Would Not Live Alway" was fading. Hymns now considered standard for funeral services included:

- Watts's "Unveil Thy Bosom, Faithful Tomb" --- to the "Dead March" from Handel's *Saul*
- Montgomery's "Servant of God, Well Done" --- to "Olmutz"
- Woodbury's "Forever with the Lord" (text by [[James_Montgomery]])
- Anna Steele's "So Fades the Lovely, Blooming Flower" (for a child's funeral)
- Sir John Bowring's "I Cannot Always Trace the Way" (often sung by a male quartet)

The male quartet had by this period become a favorite vehicle for funeral music, allowing a more intimate and flexible rendering than full congregational song.

## The Gospel Consolation Tradition

Within the *Gospel Hymns* collections, several pieces found their special home at deathbeds and gravesides:

- **"Eternity"** (words by Mrs. Ellen M.H. Gates; tune by [[Philip_Paul_Bliss]]) --- an awe-inspiring meditation on time's clanging bells against eternity's silence
- **"One More Day's Work for Jesus"** (words by Miss Anna Warner; tune by [[Robert_Lowry]]) --- a gentle counting of days with devotion
- **"We Shall Meet Beyond the River"** (words by Rev. John Atkinson, D.D., 1867; tune by Hubert P. Main) --- written in his study near midnight after his mother's death
- **"My Ain Countree"** (words by Miss Mary Augusta Lee, 1860; tune by Mrs. Ione T. Hanna, 1864, harmonized by Hubert P. Main, 1873) --- a Scots-dialect vision of heaven that brings unshed tears when sung as a closing hymn

## Legacy

The hymns of consolation do something beyond comfort the bereaved: they form the Christian imagination. A person who has sung these hymns through a lifetime arrives at the threshold of death with a vocabulary already in place --- words for what cannot otherwise be spoken. Butterworth-Brown observe that James Montgomery's "Forever with the Lord" was written and revised so many times because "the contemplation so absorbed him that he could not quit his theme till he had composed twenty-two quatrains." The best of these hymns are simply forms of prayer that refused to stop.
