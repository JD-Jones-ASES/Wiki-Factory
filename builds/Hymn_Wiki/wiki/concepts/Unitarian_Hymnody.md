# Unitarian Hymnody

Unitarian hymnody occupies a curious place in the history of English and American sacred song. It began with the great tradition of Watts, which Unitarians claimed as their heritage before most other denominations had thought to do so. It ended — or rather, opened out — into a hymnody that many would no longer recognize as Christian at all. In between, it produced some of the most literarily distinguished sacred verse in the English language, and it set the standard by which other denominations measured their own hymnodic aspirations for a generation.

## Origins: The Watts Connection

The Unitarian movement in England and America grew largely out of eighteenth-century Presbyterianism and Congregationalism — the very traditions that had produced Watts. Isaac Watts himself occupied a somewhat ambiguous theological position in his later years, and the Unitarian movement found much in him that it could claim. His *Psalms* and *Hymns* remained in use in many Unitarian congregations well into the nineteenth century.

The critical transition came when Unitarians began to feel that Watts did not go far enough — that his Calvinist theology, even in its gentler expressions, made claims about God, atonement, and human nature that Unitarian theology could not endorse. The task became building a Hymnody that expressed what Unitarians actually believed.

## British Unitarian Hymnody: Martineau and His Work

In Britain, the dominant figure in the development of Unitarian hymnody was [[James_Martineau]] (1805-1900), minister, philosopher, and relentlessly discriminating editor.

Martineau's first major collection, *Hymns for the Christian Church and Home* (1840), represented a departure from the Watts tradition. Selected with a scholar's taste and a poet's ear, it included material from across the tradition of English sacred verse, but organized and edited according to Martineau's own theological and literary judgments. He did not hesitate to alter texts that he considered theologically incorrect or poetically inferior — a practice that drew both praise for its results and criticism for its liberties.

His second and more influential collection, *Hymns of Praise and Prayer* (1873), went further. By 1873 Martineau's theology had moved well beyond anything that could be called historically orthodox Christianity. The collection aimed to provide, in Benson's phrase, "a Hymnody that confessedly transcended the limits of historical Christianity" — a sacred song adequate to "universal religion" rather than specifically Christian worship.

The literary quality of both collections was exceptional. Martineau's standards were exacting, and the resulting books became reference points for editors of other denominations who were trying to raise the literary standard of their own collections.

## American Unitarian Hymnody: The Literary Standard

In America, Unitarian hymnody in the nineteenth century produced a remarkable flowering of sacred poetry. The writers associated with the American Unitarian tradition were among the most celebrated poets of their generation — and several of them wrote hymns of genuine and lasting quality.

**Oliver Wendell Holmes** contributed hymns of intellectual clarity and warmth. **Henry Wadsworth Longfellow** wrote "I heard the bells on Christmas Day." **Samuel Longfellow** and **Samuel Johnson** — both Harvard Divinity graduates and leaders in the movement toward "free religion" — published *Hymns of the Spirit* (1864), a collection that deliberately moved beyond Trinitarian Christianity and into the territory of broadly theistic religion.

**John Greenleaf Whittier**, the Quaker abolitionist poet, was not formally a Unitarian but occupied adjacent theological territory; his hymns (extracted from longer poems by sympathetic editors) achieved enormous use in Unitarian, Congregational, and broader Protestant worship.

The American Unitarian tradition also influenced non-Unitarian hymnody through the model it provided: a hymnody of literary distinction, personal religious sincerity, and avoidance of what its editors regarded as credal narrowness. The *Rivulet Controversy* in British Congregationalism (c. 1856) — the conflict over the Congregationalist T. T. Lynch's *The Rivulet* hymn collection, which was attacked for insufficient evangelical doctrine — illustrated how the Unitarian literary standard had begun to penetrate other Nonconformist denominations, creating controversy where it did.

## The Theological Trajectory

Benson's account traces a clear trajectory in Unitarian hymnody: from its origins as a reformed Calvinism using the hymnody of Watts, through Martineau's reshaping of the tradition toward a broadly Christian but non-dogmatic devotion, to the American "free religion" collections of the 1860s-1880s that moved beyond Christianity into universal theism.

This trajectory was significant for the whole history of English hymnody because it represented the logical working-out of principles that the hymn-singing tradition had implicitly contained from the beginning. If congregations could sing human compositions rather than Scripture alone, the question of which human compositions and on what authority they were selected could not be avoided. Unitarianism pressed that question to its limit.

## Influence on Other Denominations

The Unitarian hymnodic tradition influenced other denominations in two ways.

Positively, it raised the literary standard. Other editors, seeing what Martineau and his American counterparts had achieved, were embarrassed by the poor verse that often passed for hymns in their own collections. The Unitarian example was part of what drove the improvement of hymnody across the Nonconformist spectrum in the later nineteenth century.

Negatively, it served as a warning. The movement's theological trajectory made traditional Evangelicals wary of accepting the "literary motive" in hymnody without theological guardrails. If literary quality was the primary criterion, where did doctrine end? The Rivulet Controversy showed that this was not an abstract question.
