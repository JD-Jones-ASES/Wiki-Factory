# Revival Hymns

The revival hymn tradition of colonial and early national America emerged from the collision of Puritan severity with the emotional energy of the 1740s Great Awakening and the subsequent Methodist and Baptist frontier revivals. It produced a body of song distinct in character, style, and cultural context from the formal hymnody of the established churches.

## The Pre-Revival Background

Butterworth-Brown describe the sober religious music of the colonial period with a telling specificity: "The sober churches of the 'Old Thirteen' states... sustained evening prayer-meetings... 'Lebanon' set forth the appalling shortness of human life; 'Windham' gave its depressing story of the great majority of mankind on the 'broad road.'" Even joyful occasions were subject to C minor. "The Puritans took their pleasures seriously, and this did not cease to be true till at least two hundred years after the Pilgrims landed."

The change came with the Great Awakening of 1740, which "swept the country with positive rather than negative music." Even Jonathan Edwards admitted the need of better psalm-books and better psalmody.

## Characteristics of the Revival Hymn

The revival hymn differs from the literary hymn of Watts or Wesley in several ways:
- Direct, urgent address to the unconverted: "Come, ye sinners, poor and needy"
- Dramatic personal testimony: "I once was lost, but now am found"
- Sturdy, memorable melody — often borrowed from secular or folk sources
- Communal refrain or chorus designed for mass participation
- Emotional immediacy rather than theological elaboration

The genre reaches across denominational lines: Baptist (Leland, Medley), Methodist (Cennick, Cartwright), Congregational (Hart, Robinson), and even Mohegan (Occum).

## Key Hymns and Their Histories

### "Come, Ye Sinners, Poor and Needy" — [[Joseph_Hart]]
Born in London in 1712, Hart went astray to the point of publishing writings against Christianity. He was converted in Passion Week 1767 after "an amazing view of the sufferings of Christ" and became minister of Jewin Street Congregational Chapel. His hymn contains one of the most honest lines in all revival song: "If you tarry till you're better, you will never come at all." The tune was Weber's "Wilmot," from an operatic source — itself an instance of secular music sanctified for revival use.

### "Come, Thou Fount of Every Blessing" — [[Robert_Robinson]]
Written around 1758 when Robinson was a young Methodist convert. He later became theologically restless, passed through Methodist, Baptist, and Independent phases before becoming Socinian, and struggled with the faith of his early hymn. The story is told of him encountering a woman on a stagecoach singing the hymn and confessing: "Madam, I am the unhappy man who wrote that hymn many years ago; and I would give a thousand worlds, if I had them, if I could feel as I felt then." The tune "Nettleton" by John Wyeth (~1812) became the standard companion.

### "Awaked by Sinai's Awful Sound" — [[Samson_Occum]]
The first hymn written by a Native American, by a Mohegan from New London County, Connecticut, who was the first Native American ordained in America (1759). Occum raised £10,000 in England for Moore's Indian Charity School, which became Dartmouth College. His hymn was set to the stentorian tune "Ganges."

### "The Day Is Past and Gone" — [[John_Leland]]
The only surviving hymn of the Baptist revival preacher who delivered a 1,450-pound Cheshire Cheese to President Jefferson by ox-team in 1801, "preaching all the way." Gray-headed men and women remembered being sung to sleep by it. The tune was by Amzi Chapin (appearing as early as 1805).

### "A Charge to Keep I Have" — [[Charles_Wesley]]
Heard at camp grounds "from the rows of tents in the morning while the good women prepared their pancakes and coffee," invariably to old "Kentucky" by Jeremiah Ingalls — a tune Butterworth-Brown judge "not field music" and unworthy of words that deserve something like "St. Thomas."

### "Jesus My All to Heaven Is Gone" — [[John_Cennick]]
By the Moravian convert who had worked under the Wesleys and then Whitefield before finding his spiritual home at Herrnhut. Tune "Duane Street" by George Coles, 1835.

### "If You Cannot on the Ocean" (Your Mission) — [[Ellen_Gates]]
Written during the winter of 1861-62, this was sung before Lincoln and his cabinet at a Senate Chamber meeting in February 1865. Lincoln twice requested it to be repeated. The event first impressed [[Ira_D_Sankey]] with "the power of evangelical solo song" and was what caused Philip Phillips to devote himself to gospel singing.

## The Camp Meeting and Frontier Revival

The camp meeting hymn — sung outdoors at multi-day revival gatherings on the American frontier — took the most direct emotional approach. [[Peter_Cartwright]], the muscular Methodist circuit-preacher, embodied this tradition: he reportedly sang "Then my soul mounted higher / In a chariot of fire / And the moon it was under my feet" over a blacksmith who had defied him, and the defeated blacksmith became his friend and follower.

The frontier revivalists were not literary men. John Leland was described as "the minister who wrote his own hymns" — a peculiarity by which he imitated Watts and Doddridge. The poetry was often rough, but it served. Elder Leland's "The Day Is Past and Gone" survived all his other work, kept alive by nothing more than the memory of being sung to sleep.

## Transition to the Gospel Song

The revival hymn tradition flows directly into the [[Gospel_Songs]] of the Moody-Sankey era. The emotional directness, communal refrain, and evangelistic address of the revival hymn are all present in the gospel song. The difference is primarily cultural: the gospel song is urban, accompanied by organ or harmonium, and professionally arranged; the revival hymn was frontier, a cappella or led by a single uneducated singer, shaped by the landscape it inhabited.

## Camp Meeting Hymns as a Distinct Type (Benson)

Benson's *The English Hymn* (1915) is the source for the most careful institutional account of camp meeting hymnody as a distinct type within the broader revival tradition.

**The Origin.** Benson locates the camp meeting hymn's specific origin at the Kentucky Revival of 1800 — the "Great Revival" that began in Logan County under James McGready and reached its peak at Cane Ridge in August 1801. The conditions of the frontier camp meeting — enormous outdoor crowds, predominantly illiterate, emotionally charged — shaped a specific song form: simple verses built on familiar evangelical material, to which a refrain or chorus was added that the whole crowd could learn after one hearing.

**The Refrain as Innovation.** The refrain or chorus, enabling mass participation by people who did not know the verses, was the camp meeting hymn's defining contribution to American religious music. The verse could draw freely from Watts, Wesley, and the older revival tradition; the chorus was the new element that transformed passive listeners into active singers.

**Distinctions.** Benson carefully distinguishes the camp meeting hymn from adjacent traditions: it was simpler and cruder than the Evangelical Revival hymns of the Great Awakening, which maintained the influence of Watts; it lacked the theological richness of the Wesleyan hymns, though it borrowed their emotional directness; and it differed from the later Gospel Hymn in being a popular and largely spontaneous creation rather than a professional urban production.

**The Cumberland Presbyterian Church.** One lasting institutional legacy of the Kentucky Revival was the birth of the Cumberland Presbyterian Church — formed when the scarcity of educated clergy on the frontier led Presbyterian ministers to ordain men without full classical training. The Cumberland Presbyterians became one of the most vigorous frontier denominations, and their hymnody bore the camp meeting tradition's full marks.

**Song Books and Publishing.** The camp meeting tradition generated its own publishing stream: Richard Allen's *Collection of Spiritual Songs* (1803) for the African Methodist Episcopal Church; the Hastings-Mason *Spiritual Songs for Social Worship* (1832) as an educated response; and a long series of denominational and interdenominational collections throughout the first half of the nineteenth century.
