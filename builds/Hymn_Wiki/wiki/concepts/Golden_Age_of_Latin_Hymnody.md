# Golden Age of Latin Hymnody

The phrase "golden age" is often overused, but when Ryden applies it to the twelfth and thirteenth centuries of Latin hymnody, it is genuinely apt. This was the period when the great tradition of Western Latin sacred poetry reached its highest point of beauty, depth, and variety --- producing hymns that have never lost their power.

## The Context: Dark Times and Bright Stars

The medieval church, for all the beauty of its art and architecture, was also a church marked by deep moral failure. Corruption in monasteries, the exploitation of ordinary people, the violence of the Crusades, the growing distance between the clergy and the people --- these are the shadows that hang over the Middle Ages.

Yet Ryden points to something remarkable in this darkness: "in the midst of the spiritual decay and worldly depravity that characterized the age there were noble souls whose lives shone like bright stars in the surrounding darkness." Their sacred poetry, much of it written for private devotion rather than public performance, bears witness to a genuine and deep love for Christ that survived the institutional failures around it.

The Crusades, paradoxically, played a role in stimulating this flowering of religious feeling. Beginning in 1098, the Crusades awakened an intense emotional religiosity --- a longing for the holy places, a meditation on the Passion of Christ, a yearning for the heavenly Jerusalem. These feelings found expression in some of the most beautiful Latin verse ever written.

## The Major Writers and Their Hymns

### Theodulph of Orleans (c. 750-821)

A slightly earlier figure who stands at the beginning of this tradition, [[Theodulph_of_Orleans]] wrote "All glory, laud, and honor" --- the Palm Sunday processional --- while imprisoned in a monastery cell around 821. The hymn's triumphant praise, sung from behind prison walls, is a remarkable testimony to the spirit that animates the whole era.

### Rhabanus Maurus (c. 780-856)

Rhabanus Maurus, Archbishop of Mainz, is usually credited with the *Veni, Creator Spiritus* --- "Come, Holy Ghost, our souls inspire" --- a Pentecost hymn that became one of the most widely used in the entire Catholic tradition. It was the hymn sung at ordinations, at councils, at moments of solemn decision. Its majestic invocation of the Holy Spirit has made it a standard of Christian worship across many centuries and traditions.

### Bernard of Clairvaux (1091-1153)

The dominant figure of the twelfth century, [[Bernard_of_Clairvaux]] brought to Latin sacred verse both extraordinary spiritual intensity and the gifts of a great poet. His Good Friday hymn beginning *Salve caput cruentatum* --- "O sacred Head, now wounded" --- is the masterpiece of the era and perhaps the finest Passion hymn ever written in any language.

He also wrote *De Nomine Jesu*, a long poem on the Name of Jesus from which three beloved English hymns have been drawn: "Jesus, the very thought of Thee," "O Jesus! King most wonderful," and "O Jesus, Joy of loving hearts!"

Philip Schaff's description of "O sacred Head" captures the scope of its influence: "This classic hymn has shown in three tongues --- Latin, German and English --- and in three confessions --- Roman, Lutheran and Reformed --- with equal effect the dying love of our Saviour and our boundless indebtedness to Him."

### Bernard of Cluny (c. 1100-c. 1156)

A contemporary of Bernard of Clairvaux, [[Bernard_of_Cluny]] wrote a 3,000-line Latin poem called *De contemptu mundi* ("On Contempt for the World") --- a fierce satire of the vices of his age. Out of this massive work, [[John_Mason_Neale]] extracted three hymns about heaven that are among the most beloved in English-language hymnody:
- "Jerusalem the golden"
- "Brief life is here our portion"
- "For thee, O dear, dear country"

### Adam of St. Victor (c. 1110-c. 1180)

Adam of St. Victor was, according to Ryden, "the composer of more than one hundred sequences of high lyrical order." A sequence was a form of Latin liturgical poetry inserted into the Mass between the Epistle and Gospel. Adam was the undisputed master of the form --- prolific, technically accomplished, and devotionally rich. While his hymns are less well known today than those of the two Bernards, they represent a remarkable body of sacred poetry.

### Thomas of Celano (c. 1185-c. 1265)

The Franciscan friar [[Thomas_of_Celano]] wrote what Ryden calls "the masterpiece among judgment hymns" --- the *Dies irae, dies illa* ("That day of wrath, that dreadful day"). A meditation on the Last Judgment, it combines cosmic dread with personal penitence in a way that has made it the standard text for Requiem settings by Verdi, Mozart, Berlioz, and others. For seven centuries it was part of the Latin Requiem Mass.

### Jacobus de Benedictis (c. 1230-c. 1306)

[[Jacobus_de_Benedictis]] (also known as Jacopone da Todi) is believed by many scholars to be the author of *Stabat mater dolorosa* --- the pathetic Good Friday hymn that meditates on Mary standing at the foot of the Cross. Its adapted form appears in English as "Near the cross was Mary weeping." The *Stabat mater* became one of the most set texts in Western music history, with famous settings by Pergolesi, Vivaldi, Schubert, and Dvořák.

### Thomas Aquinas (1225-1274)

[[Thomas_Aquinas]], the greatest philosopher-theologian of the medieval church, closes the golden age with his *Lauda Sion salvatorem* ("Praise, O Zion, thy salvation"), a Eucharistic hymn of great doctrinal precision and lyrical beauty. With Thomas, Ryden writes, "the age of Latin hymnody is brought to a close."

## The Closing

The golden age ends not with a failure but with a transition. After the thirteenth century, the energy of Christian sacred song moved gradually into the vernacular languages --- into German, English, French --- and the great flowering of Protestant hymnody that began with Luther drew on this Latin inheritance even as it moved beyond it.

Many of the hymns of the golden age survived into Protestant use through the work of translators. [[John_Mason_Neale]] in England and [[Catherine_Winkworth]] in Germany made many of these ancient hymns available to nineteenth-century Protestant congregations for the first time, ensuring that the gifts of the golden age would not be lost when the tradition that produced them passed into history.
