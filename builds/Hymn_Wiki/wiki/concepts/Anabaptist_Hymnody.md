# Anabaptist Hymnody

## The Singing Church Under Persecution

The Anabaptist movement of the sixteenth century produced one of the most remarkable bodies of hymns in Christian history — not despite persecution, but because of it. These are songs written in prison, composed on the way to execution, and sung in defiance of fire and water. They form the earliest direct ancestor of what would become the English and American Baptist hymn tradition.

The standard scholarly source for these hymns is the *Auss Bundt* (full title: *Einige schöne christliche Lieder, wie die in der Gefangenschaft zu Passau im Schloss von den Schweizer Brüdern und andern rechtgläubigen Christen hin und her gedichtet worden* — "Some Beautiful Christian Hymns, Composed by the Swiss Brethren in the Prison in the Castle at Passau, and by other evangelical Christians here and there"). First published in 1583, the collection preserves twelve martyr hymns, along with many other devotional pieces. Philip Wackernagel, in his monumental study of German hymnology (*Das Deutsche Kirchenlied*), devoted an entire section to these "Hymns of the Martyrs."

## The Character of the Hymns

According to Burrage, citing Liliencron's paper published by the Bavarian Academy of Sciences (Munich, 1875), these Anabaptist hymns contain "no trace of anything revolutionary or fanatical." This is important: the name "Anabaptist" had long been applied by their opponents as a term of reproach, and the movement was associated (unfairly, by and large) with the violent Münster uprising. The hymns tell a different story entirely.

What they contain is:
- **Faith in Christ's redemption** — the atonement and resurrection are central
- **Emphasis on the moral life** — faith must appear in how one lives, not merely in what one believes
- **Steadfastness under persecution** — suffering unto death is the mark of true discipleship, not a contradiction of it
- **Devotion, not dogma** — there is "little, even, that is polemical" (Burrage)

These were the household hymns of scattered congregations from the Alps to the Baltic, from Bohemia to the borders of France. They circulated in handwritten copies and small printed collections at a time when possession of such materials could cost a person their life.

## The Martyr Hymn Writers

The major writers represented in the *Auss Bundt* collection were all executed for their faith:

- **[[Felix_Mantz]]** (d. 1527, drowned in the Limmat River, Zurich) — his 18-stanza hymn opens with an expression of joy in God who saves from eternal death. He sang "Into thy hands, O Lord, I commend my spirit" as he was thrown into the water.
- **[[Michael_Sattler]]** (d. 1527, burned at Rotenburg) — a former monk whose 13-stanza hymn is steeped in the martyr spirit: "O Christ, help thou thy little flock / Who faithful follow thee, their Rock."
- **[[George_Wagner]]** (d. 1527, burned in Munich) — pastor of the Anabaptist church in Munich, who at the stake prayed, "Nothing shall separate me from thy love."
- **[[Carius_Binder]]** (d. 1527, burned at Salzburg) — a cabinet maker who wrote the hymn beginning "With all our hearts we thank thee, / Thou holy one and true."
- **[[Leonhart_Schiemer]]** (d. 1528, beheaded at Rotenburg on the Inn) — a former monk educated at Vienna; his hymn describes the scattered flock: "We wander in the forests dark / With dogs upon our track."
- **[[Hans_Schlaffer]]** (d. 1528, executed at Schwatz) — a former Catholic priest who was immovable on believers' baptism: "The Scriptures demand that we believe and be baptized, but there is no command that infants be baptized." He wrote two hymns.
- **[[John_Leopold]]** (d. 1528, executed at Augsburg) — a tailor who replied as he went to execution: "No, gentlemen of Augsburg — if God wills, from death to life."
- **[[Hans_Hut]]** (d. 1528, died in prison at Augsburg) — wrote his hymn in prison; his body was burned posthumously.
- **[[Ludwig_Hetzer]]** (d. 1529, executed at Constance) — the most prolific of the Anabaptist hymn writers, a learned translator of Scripture who went to his death with his Hebrew Bible in hand, translating Psalm 25 aloud to the crowd.
- **[[George_Blaurock]]** (d. 1529, burned at Claussen) — called "the second Paul" for his oratorical gifts; his 33-stanza hymn is a sustained prayer for steadfastness.
- **[[Martin_Maler]]** (d. 1531, executed at Gemünden) — wrote a hymn of glad praise even as he went to death.
- **[[Peter_Riedemann]]** (1506–1556) — not a martyr but a repeatedly imprisoned leader; author of several important hymns in the *Auss Bundt*, including the communal declaration "We all believe in one true God."

## Luther and the Anabaptists

Martin Luther himself wrote a martyr hymn as early as 1523, commemorating two Augustinian monks burned at Brussels for holding Lutheran views. But the Lutheran tradition produced only three martyr hymns total. From 1525 onward, according to Burrage, "the Anabaptists furnished the martyrs," and their hymns vastly outnumber and outweigh the Lutheran examples.

This is not simply a historical curiosity. It reflects the structural difference between a state church, which has recourse to civil power, and a gathered believers' church, which must find its resources in faith, fellowship, and song.

## The Bridge to Baptist Hymnody

Burrage opens his survey of Baptist hymn writers with the Anabaptist tradition precisely because he sees a direct spiritual lineage. The English General Baptists and Particular Baptists of the seventeenth century, and the American Baptists who followed them, inherited not the specific hymns of the Anabaptists (those were in German and largely unknown in England) but the theological convictions that produced them: believers' baptism, the gathered church, the separation of church and state, the willingness to suffer rather than compromise.

When [[Benjamin_Keach]] fought in 1664 to be allowed to publish religious books, and stood in the pillory at Aylesbury with a paper on his head, he was standing in a direct line with George Wagner and Felix Mantz. And when Keach became the first English Baptist minister to introduce congregational hymn-singing — a controversial step — he was also standing in that line, even if he did not know the Anabaptist hymns by name.
