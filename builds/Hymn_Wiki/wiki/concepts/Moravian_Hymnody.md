# Moravian Hymnody

## A Community That Sang

The Moravian Church --- formally the Unity of the Brethren, revived under the patronage of [[Count_Nikolaus_von_Zinzendorf|Count Zinzendorf]] at Herrnhut in Saxony beginning in 1722 --- is famous for two things: missionary zeal and love for music. Both, as Ryden observes, "owe both of these distinguishing characteristics to its great founder and patron leader."

Music was not incidental to Moravian community life. It was central. The Herrnhut community sang as they worked, sang at worship, sang before battle, sang at death. The Moravian love for singing was one of the things that most deeply impressed John Wesley when he encountered Moravian travelers on his voyage to America in 1736 --- singing calmly in a storm that terrified everyone else on board.

## Origins in the Bohemian Brethren

The Moravians traced their spiritual lineage back to the Bohemian Brethren, the heroic evangelical community that grew from the movement of the martyred Jan Huss (burned at the Council of Constance, 1415). Hunted and persecuted for more than three centuries, the Brethren survived as "the hidden seed" --- secret religious circles maintaining their faith in the face of relentless Catholic pressure.

When a band of these refugees from Moravia arrived at Zinzendorf's estates in 1722, they established the colony of Herrnhut ("The Lord's Watch"). Zinzendorf helped them draft a statement of faith based on the Lutheran Augsburg Confession while allowing them to maintain their distinct identity and organizational structure. In 1737 he accepted ordination as a Moravian bishop.

## Zinzendorf and His Hymns

Zinzendorf wrote more than 2,000 hymns --- an extraordinary output. Not all are of equal quality, and some critics found them too intimate and emotional. But at their best, his hymns express a warm personal love for the Saviour that proved electrifying.

His most famous hymn, **"Jesus, Still Lead On"** (*Jesu, geh voran*, 1721), is a pilgrimage hymn perfectly suited to a community that regarded itself as permanently on the move for God:

> *Jesus, still lead on,*
> *Till our rest be won,*
> *And although the way be cheerless,*
> *We will follow, calm and fearless.*

John Wesley translated this hymn, as well as Zinzendorf's **"Jesus, Thy Blood and Righteousness"** (*Christi Blut und Gerechtigkeit*) --- the latter is one of the sturdiest hymns of assurance in the evangelical tradition.

## The Broader Moravian Collection

Zinzendorf's Moravian hymn collection drew from many sources, not only from Moravian writers. He included no fewer than 79 hymns by the Roman Catholic convert and mystic Johann Scheffler (Angelus Silesius), whose intimate love lyrics for Christ the Heavenly Bridegroom fit naturally into Moravian devotional aesthetics even though Scheffler had been a bitter opponent of Protestantism.

Later Moravian hymnists of significance include **Carl Bernhard Garve** (1763-1841), who served Moravian congregations in Amsterdam, Berlin, Ebersdorf, and other cities, and spent his final years at Herrnhut itself. Garve is considered the most important among the later Moravian hymn-writers. His beautiful tribute to Holy Scripture, **"Thy Word, O Lord, Like Gentle Dews"**, was adopted by many other communions including the Lutherans.

## James Montgomery

The distinguished English hymn-writer James Montgomery (1771-1854) was himself a member of the Moravian communion, having been raised in the tradition. His hymns --- including "Angels from the Realms of Glory," "Stand Up and Bless the Lord," and "Hail to the Lord's Anointed" --- represent the Moravian tradition flowering in the English language.

## Influence on the Wesleyan Revival

The Moravian influence on John Wesley and Charles Wesley was deep and transforming. It was Moravian singing on the Atlantic crossing that first showed Wesley what faith looked like under pressure. It was a Moravian meeting on Aldersgate Street, London (May 24, 1738), during which Luther's preface to Romans was read, that Wesley described as the moment his heart was "strangely warmed." In the months following, Wesley visited Herrnhut and the Halle Pietist institutions to learn from the movement firsthand.

The Wesleyan revival that transformed England and then America was thus significantly shaped by the Moravian stream that flowed from Herrnhut.

## Character of Moravian Hymnody

Moravian hymns tend toward:
- Warmth and intimacy in addressing Christ
- Strong emphasis on the blood of Christ as the ground of salvation
- A pilgrim or stranger motif (the believer has no lasting home on earth)
- Missionary urgency (the whole world must hear)
- Joy and resilience in the face of hardship

At its weakest, Moravian hymnody could become excessively sentimental and focused on physical details of Christ's suffering in ways that later generations found problematic. At its best --- in Zinzendorf's finest hymns, in Garve, and especially in the English flowering through James Montgomery --- it produced some of the most tender and doctrinally rich hymnody in the Protestant tradition.

The Moravians also maintained one of the oldest continuous liturgical traditions in Protestantism, including the famous "Lovefeast" service and the Christmas Eve candle service, both of which incorporated distinctive hymnody.

## Institutional History (Benson)

Benson's *The English Hymn* (1915) provides a detailed institutional history of Moravian hymnody in England, distinguishing carefully between two very different periods — the eccentric period of Zinzendorf's direct influence and the later, normalized period.

**The Eccentric Period (1741-1754).** During the years when Zinzendorf dominated the Moravian movement personally — visiting England from 1741 onward and presiding over the English Moravian congregations — the hymnody took on an extreme and, in Benson's account, embarrassing character. The "Sifting Time" (as it is known in Moravian history) saw a flood of hymns, many written by Zinzendorf himself, that were characterized by an extravagant focus on the physical details of Christ's wounds, an excessive intimacy of devotional address, and a sentimentality that embarrassed later Moravians and observers alike. The published *Litany of the Wounds* and associated hymn collections represent this period. John Wesley, who had initially been deeply attracted to Moravian piety, was repelled by these developments and eventually broke with the English Moravians, publishing a sharp critique.

**Zinzendorf's Death and Recovery (1760 onward).** With Zinzendorf's death in 1760, the eccentric period ended and the Moravian community undertook a serious revision of its hymnody. The English Moravian hymnal was substantially reformed, the most extreme material removed, and the tradition placed on a more sober footing.

**The Normalized Period (1789-1901).** Benson traces the steady development of Moravian hymnody through the nineteenth century, including the various revised editions of the official English Moravian hymnal. The 1789 collection represented a significant normalization; subsequent revisions continued the process. By the late nineteenth century, Moravian hymnody had achieved the settled, devotionally warm but theologically responsible character that made it attractive to Christians of other traditions.

**The American Moravian Tradition.** American Moravian communities — centered especially in Bethlehem, Pennsylvania, and Winston-Salem, North Carolina — maintained their own distinctive musical traditions, including elaborate orchestral and choral music that was sophisticated far beyond what most American Protestant communities could produce. The famous Bethlehem Bach festivals, which preserved Bach's music when much of Germany had forgotten it, were an expression of this broader Moravian musical culture.
