# Early Christian Hymnody

From the very beginning, Christians sang. The last act of worship at the Last Supper was a hymn. Paul and Silas sang in prison at midnight. Paul's letters exhort congregations to sing psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs to one another and to God.

This is the story of how that impulse to sing --- born in an Upper Room in Jerusalem --- grew and developed through the first thousand years of Christian worship into the rich tradition of sacred song that would eventually flower in the Reformation, the Great Awakening, and the golden age of English and American hymnody.

## The First Songs: Scriptural Chants

The earliest Christian hymns were not newly composed at all. They were drawn directly from Scripture, chanted responsively in the weekly gatherings.

The most important of these were:
- The **Gloria in Excelsis** ("Glory be to God on high") --- based on the angels' song at Christ's birth
- The **Magnificat** --- Mary's song of praise (Luke 1:46-55), sung at vespers
- The **Benedictus** --- the song of Zechariah (Luke 1:68-79)
- The **Nunc Dimittis** --- the prayer of Simeon (Luke 2:29-32)
- The **Te Deum Laudamus** --- a great hymn of praise derived from a very ancient Greek version
- The **Gloria Patri** ("Glory be to the Father") --- the Lesser Doxology

Ryden also notes that early Christian scholars believe fragments of actual hymns are embedded in the New Testament letters --- in 1 Timothy 3:16, 2 Timothy 2:11, Ephesians 5:14, and elsewhere. These passages have the rhythmic, antiphonal quality of song, and may have been quoted by Paul from hymns already in use in the churches.

## The Character of Early Christian Worship

The younger Pliny, writing to Emperor Trajan in 112 AD, reported that Christians came together before daylight and sang hymns "alternately" (*invicem*) --- that is, antiphonally --- "to Christ as God." This brief description captures something essential: early Christian worship was participatory, musical, and Christ-centered.

The music was unaccompanied. Musical instruments --- flutes, lyres, harps, trumpets --- were associated in the early Christian mind with the pagan cults, with the degenerate theater and circus, and with forms of entertainment that clashed with Christian sensibility. Jerome said flatly that a Christian maiden "ought not even to know what a lyre or a flute is." The human voice alone was the proper instrument of Christian praise.

Chrysostom captured the spirit of early Christian song beautifully: "David formerly sang in psalms, also we sing today with him; he had a lyre with lifeless strings, the Church has a lyre with living strings. Our tongues are the strings of the lyre, with a different tone, indeed, but with a more accordant piety."

## The First Original Hymns

The first hymns that were not directly drawn from Scripture came in the late second and third centuries. The oldest surviving example with a known attribution is [[Clement_of_Alexandria]]'s "Shepherd of Tender Youth," appended to his work *The Tutor* around 200 AD. Ryden calls it "the oldest Christian hymn in existence" (outside of Scripture-derived chants).

Several other beautiful Greek hymns survive from this period without attribution, including "O Gladsome Light," a vesper hymn of serene beauty:

> O Gladsome Light
> Of the Father immortal,
> And of the celestial
> Sacred and blessed
> Jesus, our Saviour!

## Doctrinal Controversy and Hymn Writing

A crucial development in the history of Christian song was the use of hymns as instruments of theological persuasion. The Gnostic teacher [[Bardesanes]] in the second century had already discovered that heretical ideas could spread through catchy hymns more effectively than through theological argument. His son Harmonius followed in his footsteps.

The orthodox church's response came in the fourth century, most powerfully through [[Ephrem_the_Syrian]] in the Syriac tradition and [[Ambrose_of_Milan]] in the Latin West. Both men fought theological battles with music as their weapon --- Ephrem training choirs to sing orthodox hymns against the Arians in Syria, and Ambrose mobilizing the congregation of Milan to sing through the night when imperial soldiers surrounded their church.

This pattern --- the use of congregational song as an instrument of theological formation and defense --- would repeat itself at every major turning point in the history of Christian worship.

## Three Streams: Greek, Syriac, and Latin

Early Christian hymnody flows in three distinct streams, corresponding to the three great languages of the early church.

**Greek hymnody** is the oldest and most intellectually refined. See [[Greek_and_Syriac_Hymnody]] for the major figures: [[Clement_of_Alexandria]], [[Gregory_Nazianzen]], [[Anatolius]], [[John_of_Damascus]], [[Stephen_the_Sabaite]], and [[Joseph_the_Hymnographer]].

**Syriac hymnody** belongs primarily to the Syrian church of Mesopotamia and the Levant. Its key figures are the heretical [[Bardesanes]] (whose hymns spread Gnostic teaching) and the orthodox [[Ephrem_the_Syrian]], who responded with a magnificent tradition of congregational sacred song. Ephrem's hymns are still used by Maronite Christians today.

**Latin hymnody** is the tradition that would have the longest direct influence on the history of Western Christianity. It began with [[Hilary_of_Poitiers]] in the fourth century and reached its first great height under [[Ambrose_of_Milan]], whose rhythmical, singable hymns introduced congregational singing to the Western church. See [[Latin_Hymnody]] for the full story.

## The Decline of Congregational Singing

A recurring theme in the early history of Christian song is the tension between the people's voice and the trained clergy's voice.

The Council of Laodicea (c. 363 AD) decreed that only appointed singers should sing in church. Congregational singing gradually declined in the Eastern church, giving way to a more liturgical, clerical character. In the West, Gregory the Great's reform of 590 AD had a similar effect: his new Gregorian chant style was majestic, but it could only be rendered properly by trained choirs. The common people fell silent.

This silence would last, in the Western church, for nearly a thousand years --- until [[Martin_Luther]] and the Reformation restored the congregation's voice in the sixteenth century.
