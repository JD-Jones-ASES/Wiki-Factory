# Christian Ballads

Butterworth-Brown's chapter "Christian Ballads" (Chapter VI) gathers a range of devotional verse that does not quite fit the tighter categories of praise, mission, or doctrinal hymn. These are hymns of personal trust, private devotion, and lyric feeling — often marked by narrative or reflective qualities associated with the ballad tradition.

## The Genre

The "ballad" designation reflects these hymns' character more than their form. They tend to be:
- Personal and intimate in address
- Marked by feeling and sympathy rather than doctrine
- Suited for private use as much as congregational worship
- Often narrative in structure, telling a spiritual journey or recounting an experience
- Drawing on secular ballad traditions, which are then "baptized" into sacred use

Butterworth-Brown open the chapter with an observation about the instinct for religious song that runs through pagan and Hebrew literature alike — noting that St. Paul quoted Aratus to the Athenians, and that much of the finest devotional literature of the secular tradition points, unknowingly, toward the sacred.

## Key Hymns and Their Histories

### "When All Thy Mercies, O My God" — [[Joseph_Addison]]
Written around 1701 by the finest English prose writer of his age (Secretary of State 1717) as a personal act of gratitude for deliverance from shipwreck off the coast of Genoa. Originally 13 stanzas, all published in the *Spectator*. Addison was born at Milston Rectory, Wiltshire, on May 1, 1672, and died at Holland House, Kensington, on June 17, 1719. Among the most literary of all English hymns — and one of the most personal.

### "From Every Stormy Wind That Blows" — [[Hugh_Stowell]]
Canon of Chester Cathedral, born Douglas, Isle of Man, 1799; educated at Oxford; wrote this hymn of shelter in the mercy-seat. The tune "Retreat" was by Thomas Hastings. The hymn's historical weight is carried by a specific martyrdom: eight American missionaries — Revs. Albert Johnson, John E. Freeman, David E. Campbell and their wives, Mr. and Mrs. McMullen, and children Fannie and Willie Campbell — were said to have sung it before their martyrdom at Cawnpore at the hands of Nana Sahib in 1857.

### "The Sands of Time Are Sinking" — [[Anne_Ross_Cousin]]
Born at Melrose around 1824, daughter of Dr. David Ross Cundell, widow of Rev. William Cousin of the Free Church of Scotland. Her magnificent hymn, first published in *Christian Treasury* in 1857, draws on the dying words of the Scottish Covenanter [[Samuel_Rutherford]]: "Glory shineth in Immanuel's Land." The tune "Rutherford" was composed by Chrétien Urhan (D'Urhan), a French violinist born at Montjoie around 1788 and died in Paris in 1845.

### "My Hope Is Built on Nothing Less" — [[Edward_Mote]]
Born in London in 1797 to parents who kept a public house and "were not God-fearing" — "no Bible was allowed" in the school he attended. Converted at sixteen from a sermon by John Hyatt at Tottenham Court Chapel. The famous refrain "On Christ the Solid Rock I Stand" came to him one Sabbath morning on his way to preach in Holborn. He settled Horsham Sussex Baptist Church for 26 years until his death on November 13, 1874. The tune "The Solid Rock" was composed by William Bradbury in 1863.

### "I Love to Steal Awhile Away" — [[Phebe_Hinsdale_Brown]]
Born in Canaan, New York, in 1783, orphaned at two, "childhood of bitter fare and ceaseless drudgery." In August 1818 she wrote her hymn with a baby on her lap, "half-blinded by tears," after a neighbor rebuked her for slipping into their garden at sunset to pray. Originally titled "An Apology for My Twilight Rambles." She died in Henry, Illinois, in 1861; her son became a pioneer American missionary to Japan.

### "While Thee I Seek, Protecting Power" — [[Helen_Maria_Williams]]
By the brilliant English writer (born 1762) who spent much of her life in London and Paris. Her hymn is inseparably paired with Ignaz Pleyel's tune "Brattle Street." Pleyel (1757-1831), the 24th child of a village schoolmaster, was Haydn's "best and dearest" pupil and the founder of the Pleyel piano firm in Paris.

### "My Lord, How Full of Sweet Content" — [[Madame_Guyon]]
Translated by William Cowper from the French of Jeanne-Marie Guyon (1648-1717), who was imprisoned in successive dungeons for "Quietism" culminating in four years in the Bastille. She wrote that the dungeon stones "looked like jewels" — the peace that passes understanding rendered literal. The tune "Alsace" was arranged from a Beethoven sonata.

### "God Calling Yet, Shall I Not Hear" — [[Gerhard_Tersteegen]]
German Reformed mystic, translated by [[Catherine_Winkworth]]. The associated tunes include J.B. Dykes's "Rivaulx" and [[Henry_Kemble_Oliver]]'s "Federal Street" (named for the street where Oliver lived in Salem or Boston), the latter composed while Oliver was reading Anna Steele's verse "So Fades the Lovely Blooming Flower."

## The "Accidental Sacred"

Several of the hymns in this category illustrate what might be called the "accidental sacred" — secular literary or musical material that found its way, unexpectedly, into Christian use:

- Rousseau's "Greenville" — originally a love serenade from the opera *Le Devin du Village* (~1752), "Days of absence, sad and dreary," repurposed as the universal tune for Joseph Hart's "Come, Ye Sinners" and later for other hymns. Butterworth-Brown's verdict: "He builded better than he knew ... inspired, like Balaam, to utter one sacred strain."
- Pleyel's "Brattle Street" — named for Brattle Street, Boston, though the composer never visited America
- Weber's "Wilmot" — from operatic sources, sanctified for revival use

## The Ballad and Women Hymnody

An unusually high proportion of the Christian ballads are by women writers — Phebe Brown, Helen Maria Williams, Anne Ross Cousin, Madame Guyon — suggesting that the intimate, experiential quality of the ballad genre suited women hymn-writers in an era when the grand public hymn of praise was still considered primarily a male domain.
