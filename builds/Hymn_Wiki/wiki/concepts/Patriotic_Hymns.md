# Patriotic Hymns

The ethnic anthologies growing out of love of country are a mingled literature of filial and religious piety, ranging from war-like paeans to lyric prayers. They become the cherished inheritance of a nation, and once fixed in the common memory and the common heart, the people rarely let them die.

## The Ancient Prophetic Tradition

American pride has long gloried in Seneca's "Vision of the West" (the *Ultima Thule* passage from his tragedy *Medea*), written near the time of St. Paul's first epistle --- a prophecy of vaster lands beyond the ocean's bounds. Washington Irving wrote that "the predictions of the ancient oracles were rarely so unequivocal." Whether or not Seneca knew of Paul, his Roman Stoic vision became, across eighteen centuries, a document of American self-understanding.

## The Great American Hymns

### "The Star-Spangled Banner"
Written by [[Francis_Scott_Key]] of Baltimore on the night of the British bombardment of Fort McHenry, September 14, 1814. Key was detained on a British flag-ship where he had gone under a flag of truce to negotiate the release of a captured friend. Forced to watch the bombardment through the night, he penciled his exultant lines on the back of a letter when dawn revealed the flag still flying. Within twenty-four hours the words were printed on flyers and sung in the streets to the air of "Anacreon in Heaven" --- an old English hunting melody by John Stafford Smith (born at Gloucester, 1750; died 1836). The original flag that inspired the poem was made by a girl of fifteen and is preserved in the Sanderson family at Baltimore.

Dr. [[Oliver_Wendell_Holmes]] added an additional stanza to the hymn in 1861 during the Civil War.

### "My Country, 'Tis of Thee" (America)
Samuel Francis Smith, while a theological student at Andover in 1832, was given several German hymn and song books by [[Lowell_Mason]] for possible translation. A national song of German character caught his eye, and he wrote four new American stanzas. Mason printed them and introduced the hymn publicly at Park Street Church, Boston, on July 4, 1832. The tune itself --- "God Save the King" / "America" --- had been attributed to Henry Purcell, Lulli, Dr. John Bull, and others, but Butterworth-Brown conclude it was most likely Henry Carey, who sang the melody publicly in 1740 at a dinner honoring Admiral Vernon. The American history is simpler: Woodbridge gave it to Mason, Mason gave it to Smith, and Smith gave it "My Country 'Tis of Thee."

### "The Battle Hymn of the Republic"
Julia Ward Howe (born in New York City, May 27, 1819) wrote this hymn in Washington in November 1861 after a visit to Federal camps on the Potomac. She woke before dawn and, without rising, composed her lines in the dark by a kind of mental dictation. The tune had been an 1855 camp-meeting melody by John William Steffe of Richmond, Virginia, for a fire company; it was then taken up as the "John Brown Song" by soldiers at Fort Warren in Boston Harbor, who improvised words about the abolitionist martyr. Mrs. Howe's text lifted the rustic original to the level of prophecy. Butterworth-Brown call it "a poem that made its rusticity sublime."

### "Old Chester"
Both words and tune by [[William_Billings]] --- the self-taught Boston tanner who became the first great American psalmist. "Chester" was described as "frequently heard from every fife in the New England ranks" during the Revolution. Billings was a zealous patriot; the spirit of liberty breathed through his "Lamentation over Boston," his "Retrospect," his "Independence," and his "Columbia" as well. He died in 1800 and was buried in an unmarked grave in the Old Granary Burying Ground in Boston.

### "Hail Columbia"
Written in 1798 by Judge Joseph Hopkinson (born Philadelphia, 1770; died there, 1843) for a theatre singer who wanted a patriotic song for Independence Day. The music was "The President's March" by a composer named Fyles.

## The Colonial and Revolutionary Tradition

The patriotic songs of early America were rough-hewn and vigorous. Butterworth-Brown trace the line from Billings and Elder Swan through the Hutchinson family, Ossian E. Dodge, Covert's "Sword of Bunker Hill," and Philip Phillips the "Singing Pilgrim." These were songs of the people, sung at fireside vespers and rural feasts alongside the adopted lyrics of Burns, Moore, and Mrs. Hemans --- flavored with the country air and soil.

**John Pierpont** (born Litchfield, Connecticut, 1785; died Medford, Massachusetts, 1866), a Unitarian minister, Yale graduate, and poet, wrote "Warren's Address at the Battle of Bunker Hill" ("Stand! The ground's your own, my braves") on the model of Robert Burns's "Scots Wha Hae." The poem held a place for many years in school-books as a favorite declamation, and whenever sung the tune was "Bruce's Address" --- an ancient Scottish air played on Highland bagpipes long before Burns was born.

**Ralph Waldo Emerson** wrote the "Concord Hymn" ("By the rude bridge that arched the flood") for the dedication of the Battle Monument in Concord on April 19, 1836, sung there to "Old Hundred." The fourth line of its first stanza --- "And fired the shot heard round the world" --- has become one of the most quoted lines in American literature.

## The British Patriotic Tradition in Christian Hymnody

**Felicia Dorothea Browne (Mrs. Hemans)** (born Liverpool, 1766; died 1845) wrote "The Breaking Waves Dashed High," celebrating the landing of the Pilgrims. Inspired by reading an old account of the scene over tea, she captured the spirit of the moment: the Pilgrims shaking "the depths of the desert's gloom with their hymns of lofty cheer." The poem was inaccurate in historical detail but true in spirit. An autograph copy was given to James T. Fields of Boston and is preserved at Pilgrim Hall, Plymouth.

**Bishop George Berkeley** (born 1684; died Cloyne, Ireland, 1753), philosopher and Anglican prelate, wrote the famous "Westward the Course of Empire Takes Its Way" --- commonly but incorrectly quoted as "Westward the *star* of Empire." Berkeley's poem is not a hymn but shares the prophetic quality of Seneca's vision: a forecast of American destiny from a European mind.

**Thomas Moore** contributed two pieces to the patriotic tradition: "Sound the Loud Timbrel" (a bravura song of triumph based on Miriam's song at the Red Sea, set to a chorus from Charles Avison's compositions) and "The Harp That Once Through Tara's Halls" (the only one of Moore's Irish Melodies that lives wherever poetic feeling finds answering hearts).

## Kipling's "Recessional"

Rudyard Kipling's "God of Our Fathers, Known of Old" (the Recessional, 1899) was written in a moment of serious reflection on British militarism during the South African War. Reportedly thrown into a waste-basket, it was rescued by Kipling's watchful wife and sent to the London *Times*. Butterworth-Brown call it "a tonic for true patriotism" --- a hymn that reminds a victorious nation of the God before whom all empires are temporary. The tune "Magdalen" by John Stainer --- "a grave and earnest melody in D flat" with two bars of unison at the refrain --- gives it exceptional dignity.

## Keller's American Hymn

Matthias Keller (born Ulm, Germany, March 20, 1813; died 1875) was a naturalized American who composed the grand national anthem that bears his name. His story is one of the most touching in American musical history: he invested his brother's house-savings in a disastrous New York premiere, then doggedly rebuilt his reputation in Boston, eventually hearing his anthem performed by ten thousand voices at the Peace Jubilee of 1872. Yet the $500 prize offered for a national hymn was withheld on a technicality, and Keller remained a poor man. In his last years, partially paralyzed, he supported himself by painting flowers and retouching portraits.

## "God Bless Our Native Land"

This familiar patriotic hymn has two authors: John Sullivan Dwight (born Boston, 1813; died 1893) and Rev. Charles Timothy Brooks (born Salem, Massachusetts, 1813; died 1883), who independently translated it from the German. Older hymnbooks credited only Dwight; later editions attached both names. Lowell Mason's tune "Dort" was united to the hymn by Mason himself and proved braver and more suitable to the words than even the famous "America" tune.

## Every Nation Has Its Hymns

Butterworth-Brown close their chapter on patriotic hymns with a reflection worth preserving: "Every civilized nation has its patriotic hymns. In fact what makes a nation a nation is largely the unifying influences of its common song. Even the homeless Hebrew nation is kept together by its patriotic Psalms."

The range of quality among patriotic hymns is wide --- from the defiant energy of the Marseillaise, to the thrilling drive of "Scots Wha Hae," to the joyful pride of "The Star-Spangled Banner," to the quiet sweetness of the "Switzers' Song of Home." Each nation sings what it is.
