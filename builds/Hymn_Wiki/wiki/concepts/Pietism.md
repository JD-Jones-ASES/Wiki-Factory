# Pietism

## The Lutheran Awakening

Pietism was a renewal movement that arose within Lutheranism in the latter part of the seventeenth century and continued through the first half of the eighteenth. In many respects it resembled the contemporary Puritan movement in England and anticipated the Wesleyan revival that would follow it by a generation. Its central concerns were personal conversion and spiritual experience, Bible study, practical holiness, and the transformation of formal, orthodox Christianity into living faith.

It gave birth to a great revival in hymnody.

## Origins: Spener and Halle

The leader of the movement was **Philipp Jacob Spener** (1635-1705), pastor of St. Nikolai Church in Berlin. Spener looked at the Lutheran Church after the Thirty Years' War and saw formalism and dead orthodoxy where there should have been spiritual life. His response was to organize small-group Bible studies and gatherings for mutual spiritual edification --- the *collegia pietatis*, "colleges of piety," which gave the movement its name.

His foremost disciple, **August Hermann Francke** (1663-1727), became professor of theology at the newly founded University of Halle in 1691. Halle became the nerve center of Pietism. What Francke built there is astonishing: starting with no money and no state support, he opened a room in his own house for poor children, and within years had established a great orphanage, a high school, and a home for destitute students. In the year of his death, more than 2,000 children were receiving care and instruction from 170 teachers.

The monument erected to his memory bears a simple inscription: "He trusted in God."

Halle also became the cradle of the modern Protestant missionary movement. In 1705 --- nearly a century before William Carey sailed for India --- Bartholomew Ziegenbalg and Henry Plutschau left Halle as the first Protestant missionaries to India. The young [[Count_Nikolaus_von_Zinzendorf|Count Zinzendorf]] was a student at Halle under Francke, and from there drew the missionary passion that founded the [[Moravian_Hymnody|Moravian]] movement. John Wesley came to Halle in 1738, shortly after his conversion, to learn more of Luther and the Pietists.

## Pietism and Hymnody

Neither Spener nor Francke were remarkable hymn-writers. But the movement they led generated a great outpouring of song throughout German-speaking Christianity.

At Halle the representative Pietist hymnist was **Johann Anastasius Freylinghausen** (1670-1739), who succeeded Francke as head of the Halle institutions. His *Geistreiches Gesangbuch* (first edition 1704, containing 683 hymns) became the standard hymnbook of the movement. A second collection in 1714 contained 815 more hymns; the two were combined in 1741 into a vast collection of 1,582 hymns and 600 tunes. Freylinghausen wrote 44 of these hymns and composed some of the melodies.

His most famous hymn, **"O Jesus, Source of Calm Repose"**, was greatly admired by John Wesley, who translated it into English in 1737.

## The "Jesus Hymns"

One of Pietism's most characteristic contributions was the proliferation of "Jesus hymns" --- hymns addressed directly to Christ with warm, personal intimacy. [[Johann_Franck]] had pioneered this tradition earlier in the seventeenth century ("Jesus, Priceless Treasure"), and the Pietists developed it fully. At its best, the Jesus hymn expressed genuine evangelical love for the Saviour. At its worst (see below), it descended into overwrought sentimentality.

## The Wuerttemberg School: Pietism at Its Best

The finest flowering of Pietist hymnody appeared in southern Germany, particularly in Wuerttemberg, where the great theologian and Bible scholar Johann Albrecht Bengel provided a sound doctrinal foundation for a healthy Pietism.

**Philipp Friedrich Hiller** (1699-1769), a student of Bengel, became the representative hymnist of this school. He wrote 1,075 hymns, most for his devotional book "Geistliches Liederkästlein." After losing his voice shortly after beginning his pastorate in 1748, he could no longer preach --- and his spirit turned to writing hymns "richer and sweeter than ever." His rule for hymn-writing is instructive: "I have always striven for simplicity. Bombastic expressions of a soaring imagination, a commonplace and too familiar manner of speaking of Christ as a brother, of kisses and embraces... all these I have scrupulously avoided."

**Baron Christoph Carl Ludwig von Pfeil** (diplomat, 1713-1784) was another Wuerttemberg representative. A personal friend of Bengel and eventually a Prussian ambassador, he wrote approximately 850 hymns as his "chief diversion" throughout life.

## The Silesian Conservatives

Two Silesian pastors, Johann Andreas Rothe (1688-1758) and Johann Mentzer (1658-1734), occupy a middle ground between Pietism and conservative Lutheranism. Rothe's **"Now I Have Found the Ground Wherein"** --- a profound meditation on the wounds of Christ as the soul's anchor --- was admired and translated by John Wesley and appeared in a Moravian hymnbook before Lutherans even knew it was by a Lutheran.

Mentzer's exuberant hymn **"O Would, My God, That I Could Praise Thee"** (also known by the first line "O That I Had a Thousand Voices") is a soaring hymn of praise to God for the gift of creation and redemption.

## Pietism and the Broader Revival

Pietism in Germany is the direct forerunner of two of the most significant revival movements in the English-speaking world:

1. **The Moravian movement** of [[Count_Nikolaus_von_Zinzendorf]], which grew from seeds planted at Halle and gave rise to [[Moravian_Hymnody]].
2. **The Wesleyan revival** in England. John Wesley's famous "heart-warming" experience in 1738 on Aldersgate Street came shortly after he had been deeply influenced by Moravian singing and community life, and shortly before his visit to Halle to study the Pietists firsthand.

The "Jesus hymns" of the Pietists also shaped the subsequent development of English evangelical hymnody through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

## The Dangers of Pietism

Pietism was not without its excesses. The later Halle hymn-writers, particularly Woltersdorf, sometimes employed overwrought and even sensuous imagery. Woltersdorf dwelt morbidly on the physical details of Christ's suffering in some Passion hymns, and borrowed the image (from the Catholic convert [[Reformation_Hymnody|Scheffler]]) of the soul as a bee drawing sustenance from the wounds of Christ. This tendency toward emotional self-indulgence and inordinate language was one reason that conservative Lutherans like [[Benjamin_Schmolck]] and Erdmann Neumeister positioned themselves deliberately against the Halle extremes.

The Pietist movement also contributed to the separation of hymnody from sound doctrine in some quarters --- a trend that would worsen as the eighteenth century gave way to Rationalism.
