# Hymns Ancient and Modern

*Hymns Ancient and Modern* is the most influential hymnal in the history of the English church. Its first edition (1861) transformed Anglican worship; its successive editions shaped English congregational song for generations; and its reach extended far beyond the Church of England to influence Nonconformist and even American denominations that had no formal connection with Anglicanism. Benson describes it as the defining expression of the Oxford Movement's revolution in English hymnody.

## Origins and the Oxford Movement Context

The Oxford Movement — begun formally in 1833 with Keble's Assize Sermon and driven theologically by Newman's Tracts for the Times — had as one of its central aims the recovery of the liturgical and devotional heritage of the ancient church. Part of that recovery involved hymns: not the evangelical hymns of Watts and Wesley, but the office hymns of the ancient Latin Breviary, translated and made available for Anglican congregations.

John Henry Newman had pioneered this work with his translations appended to Tract 75. John Keble's *Christian Year* (1827) had given the movement its devotional atmosphere. Most significantly, [[John_Mason_Neale]] had through the 1840s and 1850s produced a stream of translations from Greek and Latin that gave English congregations access to fifteen centuries of Christian song they had never sung before.

The stage was set for a hymnal that would gather all this material — the ancient office hymns, the newer Anglican verse, and the best of the evangelical tradition — into a single collection ordered by the Christian year.

## The First Edition (1861)

*Hymns Ancient and Modern* appeared in 1861, edited under the general supervision of Sir Henry Williams Baker, vicar of Monkland, Herefordshire, who became the book's guiding spirit. The musical editor was William Henry Monk of King's College, London — a figure of decisive importance in the book's success.

The book was organized liturgically, by the calendar of the Christian year: Advent, Christmas, Epiphany, through to Pentecost and then the Sundays after Trinity. This arrangement was itself a departure from the prevailing homiletical organization of most English hymn books, which arranged hymns by doctrinal topic for the minister's use as sermon illustrations. *Hymns Ancient and Modern* was organized for congregational worship, not for the minister's convenience.

The book's scope was broad: ancient Latin hymns in translation (many by Neale), Greek hymns, German chorales, and new hymns by contemporary Anglican writers including Baker himself, Christopher Wordsworth, Bishop W. Walsham How, Sabine Baring Gould, and Cecil Frances Alexander.

## The Role of John Bacchus Dykes

The immediate and extraordinary success of *Hymns Ancient and Modern* owed much to the musical contribution of John Bacchus Dykes (1823-1876), a Durham clergyman and organist who composed fifty-five of the tunes in the first edition. Dykes' tunes — "Horbury" for "Nearer, my God, to Thee," "Melita" for "Eternal Father, strong to save," "Hollingside" for "Jesu, lover of my soul," and above all "Lux Benigna" for Newman's "Lead, kindly Light" — were immediately popular. They combined genuine harmonic interest with singability, and they gave *Hymns Ancient and Modern* a musical character quite distinct from the older evangelical tune books.

Benson's account is frank: Dykes' tunes were so appealing that they carried the book even into Nonconformist churches that had no particular sympathy with its Anglican liturgical assumptions. The tunes were used as a tune-book by congregations whose theology had nothing to do with the Oxford Movement.

## Successive Editions

The book went through major revisions across the Victorian era:

- **1861**: First edition — the version that established the book's reputation
- **1868**: Enlarged and revised edition — 386 hymns, extended reach
- **1875**: Significantly expanded — more contemporary material, including many new tunes
- **1889**: Supplemental Hymns — added material responding to the continued demand for new hymns

Each edition consolidated the book's position as the standard Anglican hymnal. By the later Victorian era, *Hymns Ancient and Modern* was being used not just in Anglican churches but by many Nonconformist congregations who had adopted its tunes while using their own words.

## Influence Beyond the Church of England

The reach of *Hymns Ancient and Modern* extended well beyond the Anglican community. Benson documents in detail how the Oxford ideals it embodied — liturgical organization, quality of both words and music, the Christian year as the framework for worship — gradually spread into Nonconformist practice.

The process was slow and uneven, but consistent. By the end of the nineteenth century, many Congregational, Baptist, and Methodist churches had modified their worship in ways that reflected the Oxford movement's influence, partly through the direct use of *Hymns Ancient and Modern's* tunes and partly through the indirect influence of its example on their own hymnals' editors.

The American impact was also significant. The Oxford ideals, diffused through the Episcopal Church and through the educated ministers of various denominations who had encountered Anglican worship, gradually changed the standard of American hymnal compilation.

## Critical Assessment (Benson)

Benson's assessment of *Hymns Ancient and Modern* is broadly positive but not uncritical. He acknowledges its extraordinary influence while noting limitations: the tendency to provide vast quantities of material for saints' days and other liturgical occasions that resulted in much "dull and lifeless verse"; the tension between the book's liturgical completeness and its literary quality; and the controversies that surrounded the 1904 edition, which many considered a step backward.

The book's supreme importance, in Benson's view, lies in having "taken the leadership in Hymnody out of the hands of the Evangelicals within the Church of England" and having established the Oxford movement's conception of what congregational song should be — not a minister's tool for sermon illustration, but the congregation's act of devotion ordered by the Christian year.
