# Hymn Tunes

The story of hymn-singing cannot be told without the story of hymn tunes. Butterworth-Brown's entire premise is that "the melody is the psychological coefficient of the metrical text. Without it the verse of a seraph would be smothered praise. Like a flower and its fragrance, hymn and tune are one creature, and stand for a whole value and a full effect."

## The Nature of the Relationship

Hymns and tunes form bonds that, once established, prove nearly impossible to dissolve. Butterworth-Brown document this phenomenon repeatedly:

- "Antioch" is "Joy to the World" — the tune "has become the habit of the hymn"
- "Toplady" is "Rock of Ages" — "to call for the tune of 'Toplady' is as unintelligible to the multitude as 'Key' would be to designate the 'Star-Spangled Banner'"
- "Bethany" is "Nearer, My God, to Thee"
- "Missionary Hymn" is "From Greenland's Icy Mountains"
- "Woodworth" is "Just as I am"
- "Eventide" is "Abide with Me"

Each of these pairings was, historically, an accident of compilation or circumstance. None was inevitable. But once made, each became permanent in popular memory.

## Origins: Sacred and Secular

Hymn tunes come from a remarkable variety of sources:

### Adaptations from Classical Music
- **"Antioch"** — adapted from Handel's *Messiah* by Lowell Mason (originally from an English collection by Clark of Canterbury)
- **"Alsace"** — arranged from a Beethoven sonata (for Madame Guyon's "My Lord, How Full of Sweet Content")
- **"Vox Jesu"** — from an andante in a quartet by Louis Spohr (for Bonar's "I Heard the Voice of Jesus Say")
- **"Wilmot"** — by Carl Maria von Weber, from operatic sources (for Hart's "Come, Ye Sinners")

### Compositions Inspired by Texts
- **"Eventide"** — composed by [[William_Henry_Monk]] in ten minutes at the request of the editors of *Hymns Ancient and Modern* (for Lyte's "Abide with Me")
- **"Missionary Hymn"** — composed by [[Lowell_Mason]] in half an hour at a lady's request (for Heber's "From Greenland's Icy Mountains")
- **"Meribah"** — by Lowell Mason, "one of his masterpieces," inspired by Lady Huntingdon's hymn
- **"He Leadeth Me"** (tune) — composed by [[Robert_Lowry]] for Gilmore's text

### Secular Melodies Sanctified
- **"Greenville"** — Jean-Jacques Rousseau's love serenade from *Le Devin du Village* (~1752), repurposed for evangelical use. Butterworth-Brown: "He builded better than he knew ... inspired, like Balaam, to utter one sacred strain."
- **"Brattle Street"** — by [[Ignaz_Pleyel]], originally a secular composition
- **"Federal Street"** — by [[Henry_Kemble_Oliver]], composed while reading Anna Steele's devotional verse "So Fades the Lovely Blooming Flower"
- **"Webb"** — by [[George_James_Webb]], composed at sea to secular words en route to America, later pressed into service for missionary hymns

### Inspired Compositions
Some tunes are famous for the speed or spontaneity of their composition:
- **[[A_J_Gordon]]** composed the tune for "My Jesus, I Love Thee" in "a moment of inspiration" after meditating and praying over the words; on a railway car passengers fell silent listening, deeply moved
- **William Monk** composed "Eventide" "in ten minutes"
- **Lowell Mason** composed "Missionary Hymn" in half an hour
- **Ira D. Sankey** composed "The Ninety and Nine" on a train to a newspaper clipping

## The Tune Taxonomists

Butterworth-Brown's musical assessments are often sharply opinionated:

**On "Vox Dilecti" (Dykes):** "Goes farther and writes the Voice in B flat minor — which seems a needless substitution of divine sadness for divine sweetness."

**On slow tempos for Wesley's hymns:** "A slow pace of single semibreves or quarters is not suited to Wesley's hymns. They are flights."

**On "China" (Timothy Swan) for Watts's funeral hymn:** "A queer medley of melody" (Lowell Mason's phrase) — made "children weep" though it "may have sounded consolatory to mature mourners."

**On old "Kentucky" for Wesley's "A Charge to Keep":** "Not field music" — unworthy of a hymn that merits "St. Thomas."

**On Rousseau's "Greenville":** The tune "enjoys a popularity quite independent of the hymn" and its secular origin is entirely forgotten in its sacred use.

## American Tune Composers

A cluster of American composers in the early to mid nineteenth century established the standard tune repertoire for English-language hymnody:

- **[[Lowell_Mason]]** (1792-1872) — 1,600+ tunes; "Missionary Hymn," "Bethany," "Antioch," "Meribah," "Dennis" (from Nageli), "Evan" (from Havergal Sr.)
- **[[Thomas_Hastings]]** (1784-1872) — 1,000+ tunes; "Toplady," "Ortonville," "Zion," "Retreat"
- **[[William_Bradbury]]** (1816-1868) — "Woodworth" (Just as I am), "Solid Rock" (Mote), "He Leadeth Me," "Olive's Brow"
- **[[Robert_Lowry]]** (1826-1899) — "He Leadeth Me," "I Need Thee Every Hour," "Something for Jesus"
- **[[Henry_Kemble_Oliver]]** (1800-1885) — "Federal Street"
- **[[George_James_Webb]]** (1803-1887) — "Webb" / "Millennial Dawn"

## English Tune Composers

- **[[William_Henry_Monk]]** (1823-1889) — "Eventide," "Hursley"
- **[[John_B_Dykes]]** (1823-1876) — "Nicaea," "Lux Benigna," "Rivaulx," "Vox Dilecti," "Horbury"
- **Arthur Sullivan** (1842-1900) — "St. Gertrude" (Onward Christian Soldiers), "St. Edmund" (Nearer My God)
- **Samuel Sebastian Wesley** (1810-1876) — "Aurelia"

## The "Tune Named for Its Hymn" Problem

Butterworth-Brown observe a curious convention: many tunes are named not for their composers or places but for the hymn texts they accompany — a naming practice that makes the tune unidentifiable outside its hymn context. "Toplady," "Lux Benigna," "Woodworth," "Solid Rock," "Federal Street" — all these names are meaningful only in the context of the text they carry.
