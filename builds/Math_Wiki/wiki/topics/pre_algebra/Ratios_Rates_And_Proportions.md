# Ratios, Rates, and Proportions

A **ratio** is a way of comparing two amounts. A **rate** is a ratio where the two amounts have different units. A **proportion** is what you get when you set two ratios equal to each other. Almost every real-world question that involves phrases like "$X$ per $Y$," "miles per hour," "cost per pound," or "recipe scaled up" sits inside this one small family of ideas.

This topic is the workhorse of pre-algebra. It shows up in shopping, cooking, travel, science labs, currency exchange, and later on in similar triangles, percent problems, and scale drawings. Get it solid here and a whole category of word problem becomes routine.

## Ratios

A ratio compares two quantities by saying how many of one thing there are for every so many of another. Maya's baking project uses $3$ cups of flour for every $2$ cups of sugar. That relationship is a ratio and can be written three different ways:

$$
3 : 2, \qquad \frac{3}{2}, \qquad \text{"3 to 2."}
$$

All three forms describe the same comparison. The order is part of the meaning — $3 : 2$ (flour to sugar) is not the same as $2 : 3$ (sugar to flour). Always say (or write) which quantity is in which spot.

Ratios can be simplified the same way fractions can. A ratio of $12 : 18$ describes the same comparison as $2 : 3$, because you can divide both sides by their common factor of $6$. The simplified form is usually preferred because it is the smallest whole-number version of the same relationship.

A ratio may be between two counts (students to teachers), two lengths (centimetres to centimetres), two weights, or any two amounts. When the two amounts share the same unit — say, both are in centimetres — the units cancel out and the ratio is a plain number.

## Rates and unit rates

When the two quantities carry *different* units, the ratio is called a **rate**. "$120$ miles per $3$ hours" compares distance and time, and the two units do not cancel. Rates are the language of speed, price, density, population, and pretty much every "per" phrase in math and science.

A rate where the denominator has been simplified to $1$ is a **unit rate**. It tells you exactly how much of the first quantity goes with a single unit of the second. The unit rate for $240$ miles in $4$ hours is

$$
\frac{240 \text{ mi}}{4 \text{ h}} = \frac{60 \text{ mi}}{1 \text{ h}} = 60 \text{ mi/h}.
$$

Unit rates are useful because they make rates easy to compare. A loaf of bread that costs $\$4.50$ for three loaves is $\$1.50$ per loaf; a different loaf at $\$3.00$ for two loaves is also $\$1.50$ per loaf. Once both are expressed as unit rates, you can see the prices are identical, which would have been harder to spot in the original numbers.

## Proportions and cross multiplication

A **proportion** is an equation stating that two ratios share the same value. Kai's hiking club noted that for every $4$ miles they walk, they drink $3$ bottles of water. If you want to know how many bottles they should bring for a $12$-mile hike, you are really asking: what number makes the ratio $4 : 3$ the same as the ratio $12 : x$? That question is a proportion:

$$
\frac{4}{3} = \frac{12}{x}.
$$

Proportions are the easiest way to solve "if this, then that" scaling questions. To find $x$, you use **cross multiplication**. The idea is that whenever

$$
\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}
$$

is true (with $b$ and $d$ nonzero), the two diagonal products must also be equal:

$$
ad = bc.
$$

That equality comes from multiplying each side of the proportion by $bd$ — the $b$ cancels on the left and the $d$ cancels on the right, leaving $ad = bc$. Once you have written the cross-product equation, you are left with a one-step or two-step equation in $x$, which you already know how to solve.

Cross multiplication is only valid when the equation is a proportion — two fractions separated by an equals sign and nothing else. It is not a universal rule for fractions; it depends on that exact shape.

The big trap with proportions is **mismatched units**. If the ratio on the left is "miles to hours," the ratio on the right must also be "miles to hours," not "hours to miles." The moment you flip one side, the proportion is broken even if the numbers look fine. Always label which quantity is on top before setting up cross multiplication.

## How to do it (recipe)

1. Read the problem and identify the two quantities being compared.
2. Decide which quantity goes on top and which on the bottom, and write both ratios in the same order.
3. If you only need a comparison, simplify the ratio to lowest terms or convert it to a unit rate.
4. If you need to find a missing value, set up a proportion with the known ratio equal to the unknown ratio.
5. Cross multiply to get a linear equation.
6. Solve the equation and write the answer with the correct units.
7. Sanity-check the answer against the original relationship.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: simplifying a ratio

Give the simplest form of the ratio $12 : 18$.

A ratio simplifies just like a fraction. Find the greatest common factor of $12$ and $18$. The factors of $12$ are $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$; the factors of $18$ are $1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18$. The largest number appearing in both lists is $6$. Divide both sides of the ratio by $6$:

$$
12 : 18 \;=\; \frac{12}{6} : \frac{18}{6} \;=\; 2 : 3.
$$

The simplest form is $2 : 3$. The two forms describe the same relationship — for every $2$ of the first quantity there are $3$ of the second — but the smaller numbers are easier to read.

### Example 2: a unit rate

Rohan's family drove $240$ miles in $4$ hours on the trip to the farmer's market. What is their unit rate of travel?

The rate is $\tfrac{240 \text{ mi}}{4 \text{ h}}$. To turn it into a unit rate, divide the top by the bottom so that the denominator becomes $1$:

$$
\frac{240 \text{ mi}}{4 \text{ h}} = \frac{60 \text{ mi}}{1 \text{ h}} = 60 \text{ mi/h}.
$$

So the unit rate is $60$ miles per hour. You read the unit rate as "for every one hour, the car covers $60$ miles." That single-hour view is what makes rates easy to compare with other rates and easy to extend to other time windows — $2$ hours would give $120$ miles, $5$ hours would give $300$ miles, and so on.

### Example 3: solving a proportion by cross multiplication

What is the value of $x$ that makes the equation $\dfrac{x}{8} = \dfrac{15}{24}$ true?

The equation is a proportion — two fractions separated by an equals sign — so cross multiplication is legal. Multiply the top of each side by the bottom of the other:

$$
24 \cdot x = 8 \cdot 15.
$$

Compute the right side: $8 \cdot 15 = 120$. That leaves

$$
24 x = 120.
$$

Now divide each side by $24$:

$$
x = \frac{120}{24} = 5.
$$

Check by substituting back into the original: $\tfrac{5}{8}$ should equal $\tfrac{15}{24}$. Multiply the top and bottom of $\tfrac{5}{8}$ by $3$ and you get $\tfrac{15}{24}$, so the two fractions are indeed equal. The answer $x = 5$ is confirmed.

## Common pitfalls

- **Writing the ratio in the wrong order.** "Flour to sugar" is not the same as "sugar to flour." Always pin down which quantity is on top.
- **Mismatched units across a proportion.** If one side is "miles to hours," the other must be "miles to hours," not the reverse.
- **Cross multiplying the wrong equation.** Cross multiplication is a shortcut for proportions specifically. Do not try to cross multiply something like $\tfrac{x}{3} + 2 = 5$ — it is not a proportion.
- **Forgetting units in the final answer.** A rate answer is incomplete without its units. "$60$" means almost nothing; "$60$ miles per hour" is the full answer.
- **Not simplifying when asked.** If the question asks for a ratio in simplest form, leaving it as $12 : 18$ is incomplete. Reduce to $2 : 3$.
