# Unit Rates

Walk into any grocery store and you will see numbers like "$1.89 per pound" or "$0.12 per ounce" printed on tiny labels below the price tag. Those little numbers are unit rates. They exist for one reason: they let you compare two things that otherwise have nothing in common. A 14-ounce jar of peanut butter and a 22-ounce jar use different amounts and cost different prices, so the raw numbers are impossible to line up. But once you know the price per ounce, picking the better deal takes two seconds.

A unit rate is nothing mysterious. It is simply a rate where the bottom number has been boiled down to 1.

---

## What it means

A **rate** is a ratio of two quantities that are measured in different units. "150 miles in 3 hours" is a rate: miles on top, hours on the bottom. "$14 for 4 pounds" is another rate: dollars on top, pounds on the bottom. The two units are what distinguish a rate from an ordinary ratio (where both quantities share the same kind of measurement, such as "3 boys to 5 girls").

A **unit rate** is a rate whose denominator has been reduced to 1. Instead of "150 miles in 3 hours" you say "50 miles per hour." Instead of "$14 for 4 pounds" you say "$3.50 per pound." The word **per** is the tell — whenever you hear it, someone is quoting a unit rate. Speed, wages, density, and price tags are all unit rates in disguise.

---

## The rule

To turn any rate into a unit rate, divide the top quantity by the bottom quantity:

$$
\text{unit rate} = \frac{\text{first quantity}}{\text{second quantity}}
$$

The answer carries the unit of the top quantity, followed by "per" and the unit of the bottom quantity. If you started with dollars and pounds, your unit rate is dollars per pound. If you started with words and minutes, your unit rate is words per minute.

---

## Why it works

Think of a rate like $\frac{14 \text{ dollars}}{4 \text{ pounds}}$ as an ordinary fraction. Dividing both the numerator and denominator by the same nonzero number produces an equivalent fraction, and dividing by 4 turns the denominator into 1:

$$
\frac{14}{4} = \frac{14 \div 4}{4 \div 4} = \frac{3.50}{1}
$$

Every rate has an equivalent form with denominator 1 — that form is the unit rate. It is just the same ratio rewritten so the second quantity is easy to think about.

---

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Price per pound at the farmer's market

> A crate of 7 pounds of apples costs $21. What is the price per pound?

Set up the rate with dollars on top and pounds on the bottom, then divide:

$$
\frac{21 \text{ dollars}}{7 \text{ pounds}} = \frac{21 \div 7}{7 \div 7} = \frac{3}{1}
$$

The unit rate is **$3 per pound**. You can read this as "for every 1 pound, you pay 3 dollars." Sanity check: 7 pounds times $3 per pound gives $21, which matches the total.

### Example 2: Road-trip speed

> A family drives 260 miles in 4 hours on a straight highway. What is the average speed as a unit rate?

Set up the rate with miles on top and hours on the bottom:

$$
\frac{260 \text{ miles}}{4 \text{ hours}} = 65 \text{ miles per hour}
$$

Their average speed was **65 mph**. Notice that "mph" is literally shorthand for "miles per 1 hour" — the unit rate is baked into the abbreviation.

### Example 3: Comparing two packages (the "better buy")

> A 15-ounce box of cereal costs $4.50. A 20-ounce box of the same cereal costs $5.60. Which box is the better deal?

Compare the unit prices. For the 15-ounce box:

$$
\frac{4.50}{15} = 0.30 \text{ dollars per ounce}
$$

For the 20-ounce box:

$$
\frac{5.60}{20} = 0.28 \text{ dollars per ounce}
$$

The 20-ounce box costs 28 cents per ounce and the 15-ounce box costs 30 cents per ounce, so the **larger box is the better buy**. Without converting to unit rates, the comparison is impossible — you are looking at two different totals spread over two different sizes.

---

## Common mistakes

- **Flipping the ratio.** If a problem asks for "miles per hour," miles must go on top and hours on the bottom. Dividing hours by miles gives you a different unit rate (hours per mile), which answers a different question.
- **Forgetting the units.** A bare number like "65" means nothing by itself. Always carry the units through the calculation and write them in the final answer.
- **Comparing rates with mismatched units.** Before deciding which is faster, a "miles per minute" rate and a "miles per hour" rate must be converted to the same denominator. See [[Units_Of_Measurement_And_Conversion]].
- **Rounding too early.** When comparing unit prices that are close, an early round-off can flip the answer. Carry at least two decimal places until the final comparison.
