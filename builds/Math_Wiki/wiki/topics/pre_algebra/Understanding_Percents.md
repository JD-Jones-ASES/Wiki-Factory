# Understanding Percents

Percents are everywhere: battery indicators, test scores, sale signs, weather forecasts, sports stats. The word itself comes from the Latin _per centum_, meaning "for each hundred." That little history lesson is actually the whole concept. A **percent** is hundredths written as a fraction, wrapped in the shorthand symbol $\%$. The $\%$ sign is doing the same job as "out of $100$" without anyone having to say it. Once you internalize that, percent problems stop feeling like a new kind of math and start feeling like the same ratio and fraction work you already know.

## What it means

A **percent** is a way of writing a ratio whose denominator is always one hundred. Formally:

$$
p\% = \frac{p}{100}
$$

So $45\%$ means $\tfrac{45}{100}$. $7\%$ means $\tfrac{7}{100}$. $100\%$ means $\tfrac{100}{100} = 1$, the whole thing. The elegance of percent is that the bottom number is fixed at $100$, which makes comparison effortless. If one student got $82$ out of $100$ on a quiz and another got $17$ out of $20$, it is hard to tell at a glance who did better. Convert to percents — $82\%$ and $85\%$ — and the comparison is instant.

It is important to notice that a percent is a **rate**, not a **count**. Saying "$35\%$ of the pumpkins in the patch are small" tells you the proportion, but not how many pumpkins there are. That proportion describes the relationship, not the raw numbers. You need a whole to turn the rate into an actual count.

## How it works

Three mental habits will carry you through almost every percent problem at this level.

**Read percents as hundredths.** Whenever you see a percent, translate it instantly into "out of $100$." $62\%$ is $62$ out of $100$, which means the fraction $\tfrac{62}{100}$ and the decimal $0.62$. This translation is the single most important move.

**Use a percent grid to visualize.** A handy mental picture is a $10 \times 10$ grid — one hundred small squares total. Each little square is one percent. Shading $30$ of the squares gives $30\%$. Shading half of all the squares gives $50$ squares, or $50\%$. The grid makes it easy to estimate: $25\%$ is a full column of ten plus five more squares, and $75\%$ is three columns.

**Percents as a form of ratio.** Because a percent compares a part to a whole of one hundred, it is just a ratio in disguise. Any ratio $a : b$ can be turned into a percent by asking "what would $a$ be if $b$ were $100$?" This is exactly the same as setting up a proportion:

$$
\frac{a}{b} = \frac{p}{100}
$$

and solving for $p$.

## Why it works

The reason percents are so useful is that they fix the "whole" at a shared benchmark. When you say "$40\%$ of the jars are full," you are comparing the number of full jars to a hypothetical $100$ jars — even if the real count is $23$ jars, or $580$ jars, or $7$. The fraction does the scaling for you. That shared benchmark is what makes it fair to compare percents from two totally different-sized groups. You could never say "a score of $17$ is better than a score of $82$" without context, but you can always say "$85\%$ is better than $82\%$."

Percents above $100$ are perfectly legal. They just say "more than the whole." A $150\%$ increase means the new amount is one and a half times the original size. Percents below $1$, such as $0.5\%$, are also legal — they just describe a very small slice.

## Worked examples

### Example 1

A survey asks $200$ students which elective they would like to add next semester. $72$ students pick a pottery studio. What percent picked pottery?

Set up the ratio of part to whole:

$$
\frac{72}{200}.
$$

Turn it into a percent by finding what the numerator would be if the denominator were $100$. Since $200$ is twice $100$, divide both parts by $2$:

$$
\frac{72}{200} = \frac{36}{100} = 36\%.
$$

So $36\%$ of the students voted for pottery. A sanity check: $72$ is a little over a third of $200$, and $36\%$ is a little over a third of $100$. The two estimates agree.

### Example 2

Kai watches a battery meter on his tablet. It starts the morning full and now shows a bar that is filled in for the first $3$ segments out of $10$ equal segments. What percent of the battery is left?

The picture is a ratio of part to whole: $3$ out of $10$ equal pieces. Turn it into hundredths by scaling the denominator to $100$:

$$
\frac{3}{10} = \frac{3 \cdot 10}{10 \cdot 10} = \frac{30}{100} = 30\%.
$$

The battery is at $30\%$. You can double-check with a grid picture: $3$ out of $10$ columns shaded is the same as $30$ out of $100$ squares shaded.

### Example 3

A school band has $25$ members. $18$ of them are woodwinds, and the rest are brass. What percent of the band plays woodwinds, and what percent plays brass?

Start with woodwinds. The ratio is $18$ out of $25$. To turn this into a percent, find an equivalent fraction with denominator $100$:

$$
\frac{18}{25} = \frac{18 \cdot 4}{25 \cdot 4} = \frac{72}{100} = 72\%.
$$

So $72\%$ of the band plays woodwinds. Because every member plays one or the other, the brass percent must be the rest of the way to $100\%$:

$$
100\% - 72\% = 28\%.
$$

$28\%$ of the band plays brass. Sanity check by translating that back into counts: $25 \cdot 0.28 = 7$, and indeed $25 - 18 = 7$ brass players. The percents match the counts.

## Common pitfalls

- **Confusing a percent with the actual count.** $35\%$ of a class does not mean $35$ students. It means $35$ per hundred. The count depends on how big the class is.
- **Forgetting that $100\%$ is the whole.** If parts of a group add up past $100\%$, something is wrong. Categories that do not overlap should always sum to $100\%$.
- **Mixing up "of $100$" and "out of $100$."** When you say $40\%$, you mean the ratio $\tfrac{40}{100}$, which is four-tenths or $0.40$. It is not the number forty by itself.
- **Ignoring the whole when you jump groups.** "$60\%$ of Group A is tall, and $60\%$ of Group B is tall" does not mean the same number of tall people. It depends on the size of each group.
